### Campaign deliverables

- **Endowed Centennial Records Stewardship Fund (~$3.5M endowment, ~$175K/yr sustainable draw).** Funds technical-staff capacity, observer training and succession support, instrument maintenance, and data-system continuity for the basin's longest continuous datasets — the marmot demographic record, the meadow-warming experimental lineage, the snowmelt-phenology series, and the East River instrumentation suite. Insulates these records from grant-cycle volatility.
- **Endowed Continuity Fellowships (~$1M endowment, ~$50K/yr).** Two early-career researchers per year, embedded in the long-running programs, working with the senior observers who carried the records through prior decades. Designed to resolve the founding-observer succession problem the basin faces in the late 2020s and 2030s.
- **In-house data-science capacity preservation (~$0.5M endowment for staffing + ~$0.5M capital for cyberinfrastructure tooling).** Permanently funds the geospatial-data-science and cyberinfrastructure capacity that the innovation-to-infrastructure flywheel has built over recent years — the capacity that produced this Knowledge Commons and that operates the data systems on which centennial-record accessibility depends. The continuity-leaning case still funds this in-house capacity because losing it would compromise the records the Stewardship Fund protects.
- **Capital refurbishment of Gothic facilities (~$1.5M).** Selective housing and lab upgrades prioritized around what the long-running programs and the in-house technical staff require — not comprehensive renovation, but the targeted improvements that prevent deferred-maintenance debt from compounding through the next 15 years.
- **Archival and digitization investment (~$0.5M capital).** Pre-digital field notebooks (some going back to the 1960s), plot maps for meadow experiments, historical photographs, and observation records brought into accessible digital form usable by AI synthesis tools and future researchers. Builds on the cyberinfrastructure investment above.

### Frontier support strategies

- **F.cont.1 Centennial-scale ecological observation as global infrastructure** — Stewardship Fund covers protocol stewardship, succession capacity, instrument maintenance; data-science capacity makes the records accessible at scale; archival investment digitizes the pre-digital decades.
- **F.cont.2 Yellow-bellied marmot social ecology and climate vulnerability** — Stewardship Fund + Continuity Fellowships specifically address the marmot record's approaching centennial under conditions where the founding observers are retiring.
- **F.innov.3 AI-assisted retrospective synthesis of long basin records** — the in-house data-science and cyberinfrastructure preservation IS this frontier's support; in-house capacity is load-bearing because the synthesis depends on data systems RMBL builds and operates. Continuity-leaning support level: the capacity is preserved at its current scale rather than expanded.
- **F.innov.5 Institutional independence as platform for inquiry** — implicit but real: every endowed dollar diversifies RMBL's funding base, reducing its dependence on the federal funding sources that are currently contracted. The campaign itself is a form of independence-building.

### Forgone

The campaign does not fund: a Translation Function at staffed scale (F.innov.2 not in this portfolio); new partnership infrastructure for hosting visiting research teams; significant expansion of in-house data-science or cyberinfrastructure capacity beyond preservation; new sensing or instrumentation development capability (the existing instrumentation suite is maintained but not extended into new measurement domains); community-science partnerships at the F.innov.4 scale; substantial renovation of RMBL365 (the year-round Crested Butte facility RMBL acquired in 2025 continues operating in its current configuration rather than being built out into expanded laboratory, staging, and community-engagement functions — the Capacity scenario allocates capital to that renovation; Stewardship does not). The atmosphere-to-bedrock integration work begun in 2021–25 (F.innov.1) continues at its current rate of growth — funded by external grants on the existing trajectory — rather than accelerating through campaign-funded permanent positions.

### Seeds in the present

The basin's longest continuous datasets (marmot demographic record [Blumstein, 2013]; meadow-warming experimental lineage [Harte et al., 1995]; snowmelt-phenology series; East River instrumentation suite [Hubbard et al., 2018; Carroll et al., 2018]); the in-house catalytic capacity produced by the innovation-to-infrastructure flywheel (the geospatial-data-science position, the GIS infrastructure, this Knowledge Commons itself); the mechanistic phenology tradition (Anderson et al., 2012; Inouye, 2008) which depends on the long records the Stewardship Fund protects.

## Setting

This scenario takes as its central contingency three coordinated near-term choices. RMBL positions the Centennial Campaign around continuity. The campaign closes at approximately $7.5M, near the ceiling of the realistic bracket. The resulting endowment and capital commitments are directed toward future-proofing what the basin has spent a century building — including the in-house catalytic capacity that recent years have grown.

None of these is automatic. The campaign could be positioned around innovation. It could close lower. The split between endowment and capital could favor visible near-term improvements over durable financial protection. The continuity-leaning choice is the quieter of two pitches and requires donors who value durability over launch. The scenario plays out under these choices going as articulated.

The decisive feature is not the dollar figure — at $7.5M, this is a meaningful but not transformative campaign. The decisive feature is what an endowed continuity commitment means for work that grant cycles cannot reliably support: the technical staff who maintain instruments, the observers trained to carry protocols across generations, the data systems that make a century of records accessible, the in-house capacity that has quietly become essential to how the basin's records are stewarded and used.

That last category is newer than the others. Over recent years RMBL has cultivated a productive externally-funded flywheel in which grants build in-house capacity (sensing, cyberinfrastructure, data systems), which catalyzes the broader guest-scientist community's work, which supports the next round of grants. This Knowledge Commons is one product of that flywheel. The Centennial Stewardship scenario protects this capacity rather than expanding it — preserving what the flywheel has built and ensuring its continuity through the period regardless of grant-cycle volatility.

The campaign exists in a fundraising environment shaped by federal contraction. The past year's disruptions have made diversified funding bases more strategically necessary than they were a decade earlier. Stewardship is responsive to that environment without being defined by it.

## The arc — three phases across the primary horizon

### Phase 1: Campaign and immediate implementation (2026–2030)

The Centennial Campaign launches in 2026 with the continuity emphasis articulated to potential donors. The pitch leans on what the basin has uniquely built — the longest continuous mountain ecological records in North America, augmented by in-house data and cyberinfrastructure capacity that has matured over recent years — and on the proposition that the campaign's role is to make that durable.

The campaign closes in 2029 with approximately $7.5M committed. Endowment dollars are placed into investment vehicles by 2030. Capital projects begin almost immediately on selective Gothic refurbishment, with the timeline running through 2031. The Stewardship Fund's first annual draw of approximately $175K reaches the long-running programs in 2030. The Continuity Fellowships begin recruitment in 2029 with first cohort in residence summer 2030.

The in-house data-science capacity preservation matters most subtly in this phase. The cyberinfrastructure investment that has produced the Knowledge Commons and the data systems on which centennial-record accessibility depends had been operating on a series of competitive grants. Endowing a portion of that capacity at this point means it survives whatever happens to federal data-science funding through the period. A small piece of the basin's catalytic capability transitions from grant-funded to durable.

By 2030, a meaningful early signal arrives: the first grant proposals from basin-affiliated guest scientists are written knowing that the long records they cite, and the data systems they query, are financially insulated through the campaign. The basin's research culture begins absorbing a new assumption — that the foundational measurements and the in-house capacity that maintains them will be there next year, regardless of which external grants land.

### Phase 2: Consolidation and succession (2031–2035)

The campaign's commitments mature during this window. Capital projects complete by 2031. The Stewardship Fund's annual draw is steady. The Continuity Fellowships cycle through their first generation — the fellows who arrived in 2030 complete their three-year embedded positions by 2033, with second-cohort overlap planned to maintain continuity into the next phase.

The most consequential institutional resolution happens in this window: the centennial-protocol succession problem. The cohort of researchers and observers who carried the marmot record, the meadow-warming experiment, and the snowmelt-phenology series through the 1990s and 2000s begins to retire on a schedule the basin can absorb. By 2035, each of the long-running programs has identified its successor cohort, documented its protocols at standards the next generation can follow, and built the institutional memory transfer the campaign was designed to fund. The records continue. This is not dramatic. It is the resolution of a problem that, in the absence of the campaign, would have become a crisis sometime in the 2030s.

Through this window, the archival and digitization investment reaches productive use. Field notebooks from the 1960s onward become accessible in digital form by 2033; plot maps for the meadow experiments link to current spatial records by 2034. The basin's pre-digital decades become legible to AI synthesis tools and to researchers who never had physical access to the originals. The in-house data-science capacity — preserved by the endowment — operates on the newly-accessible archive and supports guest scientists doing the analyses.

A subtler effect compounds through this window: guest scientists from peer institutions begin choosing to bring their work to the basin partly because the data infrastructure RMBL maintains is genuinely good and rare. The catalytic flywheel that brought the in-house capacity into being now compounds the basin's appeal as a place to do work. Federal contraction, which had reduced flexibility elsewhere, makes the basin's relatively-protected infrastructure more strategically valuable.

### Phase 3: Compounding and maturation (2036–2040)

The campaign's compound effects become visible during this window. The endowment, performing at typical institutional rates over a decade, has grown meaningfully above its 2029 establishment value. The annual draw — held to a sustainable rate — has produced reserves that begin to enable secondary investments: a small adjacent program here, a methodological upgrade there, contingency capacity against unexpected shocks. By 2040, the campaign's quiet contribution is no longer just continuity of the protected programs; it is institutional resilience of a kind RMBL has not previously possessed.

By the late 2030s, climate stress in the basin is intensifying in ways the founding observers anticipated but did not directly measure. Snowpack-loss thresholds begin to be crossed in some basin sub-systems. The phenology mismatch question (Anderson et al., 2012; Inouye, 2008) takes on different texture: the long records the campaign protected are now measuring transformations rather than baselines. The in-house data-science capacity, by this point, has produced retrospective analyses of those records that contribute substantially to the global understanding of how mountain ecosystems respond to changing climate. The cyberinfrastructure that started as a Knowledge Commons project has become the durable platform on which basin-derived knowledge reaches researchers, decision-makers, and the wider public.

The marmot demographic record approaches its centennial in the late 2030s — the actual centennial milestone falls just inside the coda — and is among the campaign's most visible long-term contributions. Its continuity through the period, including across the founding observers' retirement, is what the Stewardship Fund and the Continuity Fellowships were designed to ensure.

By 2040, RMBL approaches a next-horizon decision: whether the campaign's success justifies a second campaign focused on capacity expansion (to complement what Stewardship has consolidated), whether to continue inside the campaign's existing endowment, or whether the climate trajectory is forcing a fundamental reconsideration of what basin research should become. The Centennial Stewardship scenario is consistent with any of these paths; the decision is the boundary moment, not a moment within the scenario.

## Lines of inquiry that mature

The phenology tradition matures through the long records the campaign protects. By the late 2030s, basin researchers can describe snow-melt timing, soil chemistry, microbial dynamics, plant traits, and pollinator response as a coupled system — partly because the campaign's archival investment makes integrating decades of separate-tradition records possible, and partly because the in-house data-science capacity produces the integrative analyses that span the records.

The marmot demographic record approaches a century and becomes an irreplaceable comparative anchor for individual-based modeling of climate-vulnerable hibernating species. Its continuity through the period — including across the original observers' retirement — is among the campaign's most visible long-term contributions.

The in-house data-science and cyberinfrastructure capacity, preserved by the endowment, matures into routine availability for guest scientists. By 2035 the question "is the data infrastructure I need available?" is answered structurally in the affirmative for basin work — a different default than guest scientists experience at most peer field stations. This becomes part of why the basin attracts continued collaborations.

The mechanistic phenology work (Anderson et al., 2012) continues through the period but does not converge during the horizon. The plasticity-vs-evolution question remains open in 2040, awaiting either longer experimental records (which the campaign's continuity protects) or methodological advances (which the campaign does not directly fund). This is an honest articulation of what Stewardship does not do.

## Moments of choice within the primary horizon

Six inflection points, all within the 15-year window. Several recur across multiple scenarios in the set — those are tagged with their shared inflection identifier.

1. **2028 endowment-vs-capital split.** *Shared inflection: `endowment-capital-split-2028`.* Made by RMBL leadership and board during the campaign itself. Whether to commit closer to $5M endowment / $2.5M capital (as assumed) or different splits. More endowment increases long-term resilience and program insulation. More capital makes near-term physical improvements more visible to donors and visitors, and reduces deferred-maintenance debt that would otherwise compound. The 65/35 endowment/capital split assumed in this scenario is the central case.

2. **2030 fellowship-design choice.** *Shared inflection: `fellowship-design-2030`.* Made at endowed-program launch. Whether the Continuity Fellowships fund career-track positions (building institutional depth) or graduate-student rotations (broader downstream training at the cost of less institutional continuity per dollar). The scenario assumes career-track positions; the rotating-student version would describe a different Stewardship scenario with broader workforce impact and less institutional anchoring.

3. **2032 stewardship-priority sequence.** *Shared inflection: `stewardship-priority-2032`.* Made by RMBL leadership in consultation with the senior observers and the in-house technical staff. Which programs are stewarded first under the Stewardship Fund, and in what timeline. The Fund's annual draw is meaningful but not unlimited; not every long-running program can be fully insulated immediately. Forces explicit prioritization: the marmot record and meadow-warming experiment are likely first-priority; the snowmelt-phenology series and East River instrumentation are second-priority; smaller continuous datasets are addressed as resources allow. The sequence affects which programs reach the centennial-protocol resolution comfortably and which need to defend continuity through other means.

4. **2034 archival-scope choice.** *Distinctive to this scenario.* Made when the archival digitization investment matures. Whether digitization is comprehensive (all historical notebooks, photographs, plot maps, specimens) or selective (the materials most likely to feed ongoing research). Comprehensive is more durable across unforeseen future research questions; selective is more cost-controlled. The basin's pre-digital records are sufficiently irreplaceable that the comprehensive choice is defensible; the selective choice is realistic. The scenario assumes a mostly-comprehensive choice with selective phasing.

5. **2037 supplementary-direction decision.** *Shared inflection: `supplementary-direction-2037`.* Made when first endowment compounding produces visible reserves. Whether to use those reserves to expand the continuity work into adjacent programs (extending the campaign's reach within Stewardship's framework), to invest in modest capacity expansion (more in-house cyberinfrastructure, more sensing/instrumentation development) that the campaign's continuity focus had deliberately set aside, or to bank reserves as institutional shock-absorption against unexpected future challenges. Reasonable arguments for each.

6. **2040 next-horizon framing.** *Shared inflection: `next-horizon-2040`.* Made at the primary-horizon boundary. How the campaign's results — substantial endowment, resolved succession, intact long records, preserved in-house capacity — should shape the next visioning cycle.

## Audience lens — research

A scientist starting graduate work at the basin in 2027 (the campaign launch year) takes their first long-record-dependent class in 2028 knowing the records will be there. They complete a thesis in 2032 anchored in part by a Continuity Fellowship-supported observer's data. They access decades of archival material that became digitally available during the stewardship period. They use in-house data systems and AI-assisted analysis tools that the campaign preserved as durable capacity. By 2040 they are an established mid-career researcher, taking the basin's stewardship infrastructure for granted in the way their predecessors took grant-funded fragility for granted — a different career experience from the one the founding observers had.

A senior guest scientist who has been bringing collaborative work to the basin for fifteen years sees in 2030 that the data infrastructure their projects depend on is now financially insulated. They are more willing to commit multi-year research programs to the basin because the technical foundation they require is durable.

## Audience lens — institution

RMBL through the primary horizon is the institution it has been, made durable. The campaign's continuity commitments mean the institution does not have to invent a new identity in the 2030s; it has to inhabit its existing identity well. Staff plan in longer cycles than they previously could. Succession planning for the long-running programs becomes routine rather than crisis-driven. The in-house technical capacity that the flywheel produced over recent years is preserved through endowment rather than perpetually defended through grants. By 2040 the institution's distinctive shape — guest-scientist research model anchored by catalytic in-house capacity and a community-priority commitment — is preserved as an active choice rather than maintained by inertia.

## Audience lens — donor

A donor contributing to Centennial Stewardship joins a multi-generational commitment to mountain ecological measurement. The specific work their gift enables is identifiable: a particular line in the Stewardship Fund, a particular fellowship in the early-career program, a particular wing of the archival digitization, a portion of the data-science capacity that operates this Knowledge Commons and the basin's broader data systems. Their gift is to durability, not to launch. They become part of a small group of donors collectively responsible for ensuring that the basin's century of records — and the in-house capacity that makes those records continually useful — reaches its second century intact through the 2030s. The visible impact of their gift is measurable across the primary horizon: which records continued, which observers were trained, which infrastructure was maintained, which in-house capacity survived to keep producing the kind of platform the Knowledge Commons represents.

This is a public scenario read by general readers as well as donors. To a reader who is not contemplating a gift, the Stewardship case describes one form a successful Centennial Campaign could take — a quieter form that protects what the basin has built and lets the institution inhabit its existing identity well across the coming generation.

## Overlay robustness

Federal funding × AI economy × climate trajectory — three external uncertainties that shape how the scenario actually plays out.

**Central case (gradual federal contraction continuing × gradual AI evolution × RCP6.0 climate trajectory).** The scenario as described. Stewardship Fund insulates the long records from grant volatility; in-house capacity preservation insulates the data systems from federal cyberinfrastructure-funding fluctuation; the basin reaches 2040 with its core intact and its catalytic capacity durable.

**Stress case: sharp federal contraction.** Federal grant pipeline collapses below currently-contracted levels. The Stewardship Fund's protective effect becomes more valuable, not less — the long records continue when they otherwise might lapse. The flywheel's external-grant input contracts but the in-house capacity preservation ensures the existing capability survives even if the flywheel slows. Stewardship is the most resilient of the four scenarios in this set under sharp federal contraction.

**Stress case: severe climate trajectory (RCP8.5-like).** Basin systems change faster than the scenario assumes. The long records measure transformation rather than baseline earlier than 2035. The campaign's contribution shifts toward documenting what is changing and what is replacing it, rather than maintaining what was. The campaign's value does not diminish — it changes character. The in-house data-science capacity becomes particularly important under severe climate trajectories because the analytic demands of documenting transformation exceed what guest scientists can produce on grant cycles alone.

**Stress case: AI economy disruption.** If the AI economy reorganizes how field stations operate (fundamentally changes the economics of in-house data-science capacity, or makes synthesis-by-AI displace in-situ measurement), the Stewardship scenario's investment in in-house capacity becomes either more strategically valuable (if AI displaces guest-scientist analyses, the basin's in-house capacity becomes the rare in-situ-anchored analyst) or potentially redundant (if AI tools become so cheap that any institution can produce equivalent analysis). The scenario assumes the former; the latter is a real plausibility caveat below.

## Plausibility caveats

The scenario is **contingent** on the campaign actually closing at $7.5M (it could close lower) and on the continuity emphasis being articulated clearly enough to attract donors who value durability over launch. The endowment performance assumption is standard institutional, not exceptional. The Continuity Fellowship cohort assumption depends on RMBL's ability to attract and retain qualified early-career researchers in the basin under the institutional conditions of the late 2020s and 2030s, which has dimensions beyond the financial.

The "in-house capacity preservation" component depends on the geospatial-data-science position and adjacent capabilities being retainable through the period. Salary and career-path competitiveness for in-house science capacity at a small nonprofit is non-trivial; the campaign funds the position but the institution's ability to hold it depends on factors the campaign cannot fully control.

The scenario assumes a moderate climate trajectory through the primary horizon — basin systems change but remain recognizable through 2040. Under a more severe trajectory the basin's long records measure transformations rather than baselines earlier than this scenario assumes; the campaign's contribution shifts toward documenting what is changing rather than maintaining what was, but its value does not diminish. The scenario assumes no major federal-funding catastrophe that would overwhelm the endowment's protective effect, no wildfire that destroys Gothic infrastructure, no AI-driven economic disruption that fundamentally changes the economics of field-station in-house capacity. Any of these would require the campaign's plans to adapt; the campaign's commitments themselves remain valuable across most plausible disruptions because they are designed for institutional resilience rather than specific predictions.

The scenario's structural blind spot: it does not address what RMBL becomes if guest-scientist demand for basin work declines for reasons independent of the campaign — generational shifts in graduate-student priorities, geographic redistribution of academic ecology, or AI-driven changes in field methods that make the basin's measurements less central. These are real possibilities the scenario implicitly bets against.

The "continuity is what donors will fund at scale" assumption is plausible but not certain. Continuity is a quieter pitch than innovation. The campaign's success at $7.5M depends on identifying donors whose values align with durability — a real but bounded population, possibly smaller than the donor pool aligned with the Capacity scenario's transformation pitch.

## Looking further out: 2040–2050

*Speculative. Lower resolution than the primary horizon.*

By the mid-2040s, the basin's centennial datasets pass their hundred-year marks under conditions of compounding endowment value. The marmot record reaches its centennial around 2046, having continued through the founding observers' retirement under the campaign's combined Stewardship Fund and Continuity Fellowship mechanisms. The meadow-warming experiment passes 50 years of continuous run in 2041. Whether these records continue to measure the basin's classical ecosystem or document its transformation under intensifying climate stress depends on trajectories the campaign itself does not control. The campaign's contribution across this longer arc is the institutional and technical capacity to continue measuring through that period — whatever the systems are doing.

The in-house catalytic capacity that the campaign preserved continues operating as durable infrastructure. The data systems and platforms — direct descendants of this Knowledge Commons — have by 2050 become standard tooling on which guest-scientist research routinely depends. New campaign cycles in the 2040s may or may not extend this capacity beyond preservation into expansion; the Stewardship scenario's contribution is that the capacity exists to extend from.

What basin science looks like in 2050 is shaped by decisions not yet visible in 2040, including the next campaign cycle and second-generation succession problems. The Foundation scenario's contribution to whatever 2050 looks like is the institutional foundation on which the next set of choices gets made — a foundation that, in the scenario's primary horizon, did not previously exist at the scale the campaign provides.
