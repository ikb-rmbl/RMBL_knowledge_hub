# Centennial Stewardship

*A Future Scenario in the `centennial-2027` set. Target magnitude, continuity-leaning. v3.0 under [Future Scenarios Framework v0.7](../../Future_scenarios_framework.md).*

---

## Synopsis

Centennial Stewardship assumes the campaign comes in near the upper end of what RMBL might raise, and that the institution makes a single concentrated bet: protect the basin's longest continuous records through the next fifteen years, above all else. The marmot study reaches its hundredth year in the early 2040s. The long meadow phenology series and snowmelt-driven plant work continue without lapse. A small in-house data team grows enough to make a century of accumulated records newly readable. Around all of this, the campaign builds the savings and broader funding base that protect RMBL's independence through funding shocks. About two-thirds of the work is continuity stewardship; the rest serves it. The scenario forgoes new atmospheric instrumentation, broad community co-production work, large mechanistic experiments, and the East River watershed buildout. It asks donors to value continuity over visible expansion — durable protection of a few load-bearing things as a more honest expression of mission than growth.

### Campaign deliverables

- **primary.** Permanent protection of the basin's longest continuous records — the marmot demographic study, the meadow phenology series, the snowmelt-driven plant work. This means stewarded field protocols, plot infrastructure that doesn't degrade between seasons, archival systems that don't depend on any one person's memory, and steady support for the guest scientists who carry the records year by year. Sized as the largest share of the campaign.
- **primary.** Growth of RMBL's small in-house data team — adding data scientists, an archivist, and tools-developers who can digitize a century of field notebooks, plot maps, and observation records and make them machine-readable. This capacity exists to serve the records: to make them durable, to make them searchable, and to let guest scientists ask questions of them that prior generations couldn't attempt.
- **secondary.** Savings and broader funding base that buffer RMBL against federal funding shocks and protect the institution's freedom to keep asking the questions it wants to ask. Sized to cover several years of essential records-stewardship costs even under sharp grant disruptions. This is the financial expression of the bet: continuity means continuity through the bad years, not just the good ones.
- **supporting.** A small dedicated capacity inside RMBL to win and execute the external grants that have historically built RMBL's tools — the recent pattern where outside funding builds RMBL's own technical capacity, which then attracts more funding. Protecting this pattern matters because the records' long-term home depends on it.
- **continuity_residual.** Baseline maintenance of the Gothic field site, housing, and seasonal operations that the guest-scientist research community depends on. Not glamorous, but the records cannot continue if the place that hosts them cannot.

### Frontier support strategies

- **F.cont.1.** The campaign's primary share goes here. RMBL stewards the protocols, archives, plot infrastructure, and observer-succession support that the long records require. The data team digitizes and protects the historical materials. Guest scientists carry the field observations forward; RMBL ensures the conditions under which they can.
- **F.cont.2.** The marmot study is the singular anchor. Campaign investments protect the trapping and observation infrastructure on the slopes above Gothic, archive the demographic records, and keep the field housing and logistical support that the guest scientists carrying the study depend on. The hundredth year of continuous observation arrives during the period covered by this campaign's consequences.
- **F.innov.3.** RMBL's data team grows enough to digitize a century of field records and build the tools that let guest scientists analyze them at scales prior generations could not attempt. This is the one place where this scenario invests in new capacity — and it does so because the new capacity serves the records rather than departing from them.
- **F.innov.5.** The campaign builds the savings, governance, and broader funding base that protect RMBL's freedom to keep stewarding the records through funding shocks. Independence is treated as the precondition for continuity, not a separate program. Less dependence on federal grants means the records survive a bad funding decade.

### Forgone

This scenario does not fund new atmospheric instrumentation in the East River watershed after the federal SAIL campaign winds down. It does not build broad community co-production programs, although the existing Crested Butte building continues to operate at current scale. It does not launch new mechanistic experimental platforms, common-garden facilities, or cross-mountain partnership infrastructure. It does not invest in stakeholder-facing translation work for water districts and land-management agencies beyond what the records themselves provide. It does not staff a portfolio of new research directions. The bet is narrow on purpose: protect what cannot be rebuilt if it lapses, and let other parts of the basin science landscape develop through other vehicles, other institutions, and other future campaigns. A reader weighing this scenario should understand that several genuinely worthwhile directions are deliberately set aside.

### Seeds in the present

The marmot study at Gothic has been running since the 1960s and is approaching its centennial. The long meadow phenology records and snowmelt-driven plant demography work, anchored by decades of subalpine ecology in the basin, continue to be the empirical backbone for questions about mismatch and adaptive change. RMBL's existing technical core — a geospatial data scientist, a GIS manager, and instrumentation-focused technicians — already operates the data systems that guest scientists depend on. The Knowledge Commons itself is one product of the recent pattern where outside grants build RMBL's tools, which then attract more grants. The federal funding contraction that began before 2026 is already shaping how RMBL-associated researchers seek support. The 2025 acquisition of the RMBL365 building in Crested Butte gives the institution a year-round in-town presence that this scenario maintains but does not expand.

## Setting

This scenario takes as its starting assumption that protecting the basin's longest continuous records is the highest-leverage thing RMBL can do in the next fifteen years. The campaign comes in near the upper end of what RMBL might raise. But rather than spreading that capacity across many fronts, the institution concentrates it.

The reason is specific. The marmot study reaches its hundredth year in the early 2040s. The meadow phenology series and the snowmelt-driven plant work are entering decades where a single lapse in observation would be irreparable. These records exist because successive generations of guest scientists carried them, supported by a small RMBL technical staff and stewarded archives. They cannot be rebuilt from satellite data or modeled from first principles. Once interrupted, they end.

The scenario chooses to recognize this plainly. Most of the campaign goes to protecting what already exists — the field protocols, the plot infrastructure, the archives, the housing and logistical support, the savings that buffer all of it against the federal funding shocks that are now the operating reality. A smaller share grows RMBL's data team enough to digitize the historical materials and make a century of records readable in ways prior generations could not attempt.

What the scenario forgoes is visible expansion. There is no new atmospheric instrumentation in the watershed, no broad community partnership program, no portfolio of new research directions. The bet is that durable protection of a few load-bearing things is a more honest expression of RMBL's mission than growth would be — and that donors will recognize this when it is named plainly.

## The arc — three phases across the primary horizon

### Phase 1: Naming the bet (2026–2030)

The campaign opens by articulating its concentration clearly. RMBL leadership and the board commit publicly to records protection as the central priority, and shape donor conversations around that choice rather than around a broader menu. The early years go to stabilizing the field protocols and plot infrastructure that the long records depend on, and to a first round of data-team hiring — an archivist and a second data scientist join the existing technical core. Digitization of the marmot study's historical records begins in earnest. The federal SAIL campaign winds down in the watershed during this phase, and RMBL chooses not to take on its instrumentation; the scenario accepts that the watershed-as-coupled-testbed will continue through other institutions or not at all. By 2030, the first tranche of campaign savings is in place, sized to cover essential records work through several years of disrupted federal funding.

### Phase 2: The records become readable (2031–2035)

The data team's work compounds. A century of marmot demographic records, decades of meadow phenology observations, and the long snowmelt-plant series are digitized and made machine-readable. Guest scientists begin to use them in ways that were not possible before — running analyses across the full span of the records, asking questions about decade-to-decade variation that earlier paper-and-spreadsheet methods could not support. RMBL's broader funding base diversifies meaningfully; foundation and individual-donor support grows to a steady share of operations. The board navigates a mid-campaign decision about whether to expand into adjacent priorities as the savings target is met early, and chooses to deepen the records work instead. The marmot study passes its ninety-fifth year of continuous observation. The Gothic field site, the housing system, and the small technical staff operate with the quiet reliability that the records require.

### Phase 3: Approaching the centennial (2036–2040)

The campaign's consequences settle into place. The records have continued without lapse through a period of significant external strain. The data team is established at its target size — modest by university standards, transformative for what RMBL can offer guest scientists. The marmot study approaches its hundredth year, and the institution begins preparing for the centennial as a public moment that names what has been protected and how. Guest scientists publish syntheses drawing on the now-readable century of records. The broader funding base and savings have held through at least one significant federal funding shock. RMBL's independence — its freedom to keep asking the questions it wants to ask — is materially more secure than it was in 2026. The institution begins to consider what the next horizon asks of it, with the records intact as the foundation any future campaign builds on.

## Lines of inquiry

The research that matures during the period is the research the long records make possible. The marmot work continues to weave behavioral, demographic, and now molecular records — methylation-based age clocks, fitness consequences of social structure — into accounts of how a long-lived mammal navigates a changing climate. The phenology work continues to test plasticity against evolution as the basin's wildflower meadows shift earlier. The snowmelt-driven plant demography work documents what happens to subalpine plants as the snow regime they evolved with thins. Through the data team's work, guest scientists begin to ask questions of the full century-long records that prior decades' tools could not support — patterns across multiple decades, comparisons between climate regimes, syntheses that draw on field notebooks dating back to the 1950s and 1960s. What does not mature, in this scenario, is mechanistic experimental work at expanded scale, atmospheric and watershed-coupled science after SAIL winds down, and mechanistic-translation work for water and land-management decisions. Other institutions and other future campaigns may carry those forward; this campaign does not. The honest framing is that RMBL is choosing depth over breadth — accepting that several real frontiers go unaddressed here because the records cannot be allowed to lapse.

## Moments of choice within the primary horizon

1. **2027 — Whether to frame the campaign publicly as a concentrated bet on records protection, or as a broader portfolio that includes records alongside other priorities.** *Shared inflection: `campaign-framing-decision`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and board
   - Alternatives: Frame as concentrated and accept that some donors who want to fund expansion will give elsewhere; frame as broader and risk diluting the strategic claim that makes the scenario work.
   - Scenario assumption: The leadership commits publicly to the concentrated framing and shapes donor conversations around it.

2. **2028 — How to split campaign proceeds between long-term savings (protecting independence and records through future shocks) and near-term capital and operations (digitization, data-team hiring, field infrastructure).** *Shared inflection: `endowment-capital-split-2028`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership, board, and Development & Advancement team
   - Alternatives: Weight more heavily toward savings, accepting slower near-term capacity growth; weight more toward near-term work, accepting thinner buffers against future shocks.
   - Scenario assumption: A substantial savings tranche is established early to protect the records' long-term home, with near-term capital phased over the campaign period.

3. **2029 — Whether to take on any portion of the SAIL atmospheric and snow instrumentation as the federal campaign winds down.** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and technical staff
   - Alternatives: Take on a subset of instruments to preserve coupled watershed science capacity; decline entirely and concentrate on records.
   - Scenario assumption: RMBL declines the instrumentation transfer and concentrates on the records bet.

4. **2032 — Whether to expand into adjacent priorities (community partnership work, watershed instrumentation, mechanistic experiments) as mid-campaign success creates donor enthusiasm for more.** *Shared inflection: `stewardship-priority-2032`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and board
   - Alternatives: Deepen records and data work with the additional capacity; open a second priority and accept the dilution.
   - Scenario assumption: Leadership deepens the records work rather than opening adjacent priorities.

5. **2034 — How to govern access to the now-digitized century of records — what is open, what requires partnership, what protects observer credit and the guest-scientist research community's interests.** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL data team and guest scientists
   - Alternatives: Maximally open access with light governance; partnership-based access that protects the scientists carrying the records; tiered access depending on data type.
   - Scenario assumption: A partnership-based access model is established, balancing openness with credit and continuity for the scientists carrying the records.

6. **2038 — How to mark the marmot study's approaching centennial as a public moment and what to ask of the next campaign horizon.** *Shared inflection: `next-horizon-2040`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and board
   - Alternatives: Treat the centennial as a quiet milestone internal to the science; use it as the anchor for a broader public moment that names what has been protected.
   - Scenario assumption: The institution prepares the centennial as a public moment and begins shaping the next horizon around what the records make possible.

## Audience lens — research

Scientists working on the basin's long-record questions get something specific during this period: stability. The marmot trapping schedule continues, the meadow phenology plots are maintained, the snowmelt-driven plant work has the field support it needs. Archival access to a century of field notebooks, plot maps, and demographic records becomes practical for the first time. Guest scientists can ask questions across the full sweep of the records — questions about decade-to-decade variation, about the deeper history of an unusual year, about patterns only visible at multi-decade scales. What scientists working on adjacent questions do not get is the coupled atmospheric and watershed instrumentation that SAIL provided, or new mechanistic experimental infrastructure. The basin remains a place where long-record ecology can be done at world-class scale, and where some of the questions that long records cannot answer will need to be carried elsewhere.

## Audience lens — institution

RMBL through this period feels concentrated rather than expansive. The Gothic field site operates with the quiet reliability that the records require. The small technical staff grows by a few people — an archivist, additional data scientists — focused on making the records durable and readable. The Crested Butte building continues to host the workshop and community functions it currently does, without major renovation. Board conversations turn on whether the concentration is holding — whether the savings target is on track, whether the records are intact, whether the data work is compounding. The institution's identity becomes more clearly tied to long-record stewardship and to the independence that protects it. Working at RMBL during this period means being part of an institution that has chosen depth over breadth and named the choice plainly.

## Audience lens — donor

Donors are invited to be part of protecting something that cannot be rebuilt. The basin's longest continuous records — the marmot study reaching its hundredth year, the meadow phenology series, the snowmelt-driven plant work — exist because they have been carried forward across generations of scientists and staff. Your contribution joins the conditions that let them continue: the field protocols, the archives, the small technical team, the savings that buffer the work against funding shocks. It joins the effort to make a century of records newly readable through the data team's work. And it joins the broader funding base that protects RMBL's freedom to keep doing this work whatever happens in Washington. The campaign asks you to value continuity over visible expansion — to invest in the durable protection of a few load-bearing things rather than in growth. This is a quieter ask than some campaigns, and a more honest one.

## Overlay robustness

**Central case.** Federal funding continues to contract gradually; AI tools mature steadily; climate continues on a moderate warming trajectory. Under these conditions the scenario performs as designed. The records continue, the data team's work compounds, the savings hold, and the broader funding base diversifies enough to make RMBL meaningfully less dependent on any single source. The marmot study approaches its centennial intact. The concentration RMBL chose in 2027 looks, by 2040, like a defensible reading of what the institution could most usefully protect.

**Stress case — Sharp federal funding shock mid-campaign.** A sudden federal funding disruption hits in the early 2030s, sharper than the gradual contraction the central case assumes. The savings tranche RMBL established early absorbs the shock as designed. Field operations continue. Some guest scientists lose their own federal grants and reduce time in the basin, but the institutional infrastructure they depend on does not falter. The scenario's bet on savings as the precondition for continuity is vindicated. The cost is that some of the savings is drawn down earlier than planned, and the broader funding base must work harder in the campaign's later years.

**Stress case — AI tools advance faster than expected.** AI tools mature rapidly through the late 2020s, beyond what the central case assumes. The data team's work becomes more leveraged than planned: a century of records becomes deeply readable earlier, and external interest in the basin's archives grows. The institution faces pressure to expand the data and synthesis work beyond its planned scale. The board's 2032 choice — whether to expand or deepen — becomes harder. In the scenario as written, leadership chooses to stay concentrated; in this stress case, that choice is more costly because the opportunity is more visible.

**Stress case — Harder climate trajectory.** Climate warming proceeds faster than the central case. Snowpack in the basin thins more rapidly; the systems the long records track shift more sharply than expected. The records become more valuable, not less, because the rate of change is what makes baseline comparison possible. But field conditions become harder: shorter seasons, more variable access, occasional fire impacts near Gothic. The campaign's investment in field infrastructure proves essential rather than incidental. The bet holds, but the work of protecting it is more demanding than the central case assumes.

## Plausibility caveats

This scenario assumes that RMBL's donor base will respond to a concentrated framing — that enough donors find protecting a few load-bearing things compelling without a broader menu. This is not certain. The Development team's read of the donor base could plausibly show that broader framings raise more, and that the concentration in this scenario would close at a lower magnitude. The scenario also assumes that the guest-scientist community carrying the long records remains intact through the federal funding contraction — that the scientists who would carry the marmot study, the phenology series, and the snowmelt-plant work can keep coming to the basin even as their own grant landscape thins. If that community frays badly enough, RMBL's infrastructure investments cannot substitute. The characteristic failure mode of this scenario is success-by-the-letter and failure-by-the-spirit: RMBL protects the protocols, the archives, and the plot infrastructure, but the scientists who give those things life drift away. A second characteristic failure mode is that concentration becomes rigidity: by 2035, the bet on records protection forecloses adjacent moves that would have served the records' long-term home better than continued concentration. The scenario's structural blind spot is community priorities: it treats the existing Crested Butte building's current scale as adequate and does not invest in deepening community partnership work that §3.2 identifies as more institutionally central than it once was. A reader weighing this scenario should weigh that omission carefully.

## Looking further out: 2040–2050

*Speculative. Lower resolution than the primary horizon.*

Looking past 2040, the further future is hazier and the framing here is explicitly speculative. The marmot study passes its hundredth year in the early 2040s, and the centennial becomes a public moment that names what RMBL and the basin science community have protected. The data team's work compounds further; guest scientists publish syntheses across a now-readable century of records that prior generations could only have dreamed of. The broader funding base, established through the campaign, carries RMBL through whatever the federal landscape looks like by 2050. The bet RMBL made in 2027 — that durable protection of a few load-bearing things mattered more than visible expansion — settles into the basin's longer arc. RMBL matters in 2050 for the same reason it mattered in 2040: because the century-long records continued without interruption through a constrained period, because the small in-house data team made all of it newly readable, and because the basin remains the rare global asset it has been. Not because expansion happened, but because protection did.
