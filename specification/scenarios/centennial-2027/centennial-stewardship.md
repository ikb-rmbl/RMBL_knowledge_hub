# Centennial Stewardship

*A Future Scenario in the `centennial-2027` set. Target magnitude, continuity-leaning. v2.1 under [Future Scenarios Framework v0.6](../../Future_scenarios_framework.md).*

---

## Synopsis

Centennial Stewardship assumes that protecting the basin's century of continuous records is the single most consequential thing RMBL can do in the next fifteen years, and that doing so requires sustaining RMBL's independence as federal funding stays tight. The campaign organizes near the upper end of what RMBL might raise around four priorities: stewardship of the marmot demographic record and the long meadow and snowmelt series; growth of RMBL's data-science and AI capacity so a century of basin records can be re-read at scales prior generations could not attempt; archival digitization that makes those records machine-readable; and the savings and governance work that buffer RMBL against funding shocks. Roughly two-thirds of the work is continuity. What the scenario forgoes is new atmospheric and watershed instrumentation, new mechanistic experimental platforms, and broad community co-production work. It asks donors and the institution to invest in records and independence — durable, undramatic things that are hard to rebuild once lost.

### Campaign deliverables

- **primary.** Stewardship of the basin's longest continuous records — the marmot demographic study approaching its centennial, the multi-decade meadow phenology and snowmelt-driven plant series, and the pollinator censuses. This means protocol stewardship, plot infrastructure, archival systems that survive observer transitions, and the on-the-ground support that lets guest scientists carry the records forward. The primary share of campaign work sits here.
- **primary.** Substantial growth of RMBL's in-house data-science and geospatial team, paired with archival digitization of a century of field notebooks, datasheets, photographs, and specimens. New staff capacity in AI-assisted synthesis lets the long records be re-analyzed at scales prior generations could not attempt. This builds directly on the technical staff RMBL already has and on the data systems the recent pattern of outside grants has produced.
- **secondary.** Savings and governance work that buffer RMBL against funding shocks — the financial reserves, board capacity, and grant-management capacity that let RMBL keep stewarding records and hosting guest scientists through years when federal funding is thin. This is the institutional independence priority: less dramatic than new instruments, but what makes the rest durable.
- **supporting.** Modest renovation at RMBL365 to support year-round archival work, data-team space, and meetings with guest scientists and regional partners during the off-season. This is a supporting investment, not a major capital build.
- **continuity_residual.** Baseline operating support for the housing, lab, and field infrastructure at Gothic that the guest-scientist community depends on every summer. The campaign protects this floor; it does not expand it.

### Frontier support strategies

- **F.cont.1.** The campaign treats the century-scale records as the irreplaceable thing. Investments go to protocol stewardship, archival systems that survive personnel turnover, plot and equipment continuity, and the staff time it takes to coordinate observer succession with guest-scientist teams. The records are protected as global infrastructure — among the few long enough to separate climate-driven change from natural variability.
- **F.cont.2.** The marmot study approaches its centennial during the campaign horizon. The campaign sustains the field infrastructure and archival access the project depends on, supports the technician capacity that bridges generations of guest scientists, and ensures the molecular tools now layered onto the demographic record (methylation clocks, social-network analysis) have the data systems they need.
- **F.innov.3.** This is where new in-house RMBL capacity matters most. The campaign funds digitization of a century of field notebooks and datasheets, growth of the data-science and geospatial team, and the AI tools that let guest scientists ask synthesis questions across records no individual could read in a lifetime. The synthesis depends on systems RMBL builds and operates — not only on outside researchers using them.
- **F.innov.5.** Independence is treated as a real campaign target, not background language. The campaign builds the savings, board capacity, and diversified fundraising that let RMBL keep hosting unrestricted inquiry when federal funding is thin or politically constrained. The records and the data team are the visible products; the financial buffer is what keeps them going.

### Forgone

This scenario does not fund new atmospheric or watershed instrumentation. The SAIL-era integration of clouds, snow, soil, and groundwater carries forward through existing partnerships with the Department of Energy and partner institutions, not through new RMBL-led capacity. It does not fund mechanistic experimental ecology platforms — common gardens, reciprocal transplants, factorial warming experiments — at the scale some scenarios contemplate. It does not build out community co-production work with water districts, tribal natural-resources offices, and stakeholder groups beyond what current partnerships already carry. It does not pursue cross-mountain comparative infrastructure with peer field stations. These are real losses. The campaign chooses the records and the independence to keep stewarding them, and accepts that other frontiers advance through guest scientists' own grants and partnerships rather than through RMBL investment.

### Seeds in the present

The scenario builds on what already exists. The marmot study at Gothic has run since the 1960s and is approaching its centennial. The meadow phenology and snowmelt-driven plant records span half a century. The pollinator censuses anchor a tradition of basin pollination ecology going back decades. RMBL's technical core — a geospatial data scientist, a GIS manager, and instrumentation technicians as of 2026 — already maintains the data systems guest scientists depend on. The Knowledge Commons itself is a product of the recent pattern where outside grants have built RMBL's own data and archival tools. The RMBL365 building in Crested Butte, acquired in 2025, gives the institution a year-round footprint. The federal contraction that began before this scenario opens is the operating environment from which it departs, not a future risk.

## Setting

This scenario assumes that the next fifteen years are the window in which the basin's century of continuous records becomes either newly readable at scales prior generations could not attempt, or quietly stranded as observers retire and data systems age out. It also assumes that federal funding stays roughly where it is now — tight, uneven, sometimes politically constrained — through the horizon, with no return to the sustained investment of the 2010s.

Under those conditions, the campaign chooses records and independence over new instruments. The marmot study approaches its centennial in the early 2030s. The meadow phenology series passes fifty years of continuous observation. The snowmelt-driven plant demography records, now read through a climate lens that did not exist when they began, become some of the few datasets long enough to distinguish climate-driven change from background variability. None of these records survive on their own. They depend on protocol stewardship, on archival systems that outlast individual careers, on field infrastructure that keeps working, and on the guest scientists who carry the protocols forward summer after summer.

The campaign organizes near the upper end of what RMBL might realistically raise. Most of the work is continuity — protecting what already exists. The innovation share concentrates in one place: a substantial growth of RMBL's in-house data-science team, paired with archival digitization, so that AI tools can be used to ask synthesis questions across a century of basin records. The independence priority — savings, governance, diversified fundraising — runs underneath everything, less visible but what makes the rest durable.

## The arc — three phases across the primary horizon

### Phase 1: Securing the records (2026–2030)

The first five years concentrate on protocol stewardship and archival work. RMBL leadership commits early to the marmot centennial as a campaign anchor and works with the guest scientists carrying the study to formalize succession protocols and archival deposit. The meadow phenology and snowmelt-driven plant records get similar treatment — written protocols, equipment continuity plans, and clear handoff agreements with the academic teams that have run them.

The data team grows. RMBL hires a senior data scientist focused on AI-assisted synthesis, a second GIS staffer, and an archival digitization technician. By 2028 the team is roughly doubled from its 2026 size. Digitization of a century of field notebooks, datasheets, and photographs begins in earnest, prioritizing the marmot, phenology, and pollinator records. RMBL365 sees modest renovation: archival workspace, a working room for the data team during off-season, and meeting space for visiting guest scientists.

The board and leadership formalize the independence priority. Operating reserves grow. A standing fundraising committee shifts toward foundations and major donors. The first AI-synthesis papers using digitized basin records appear from guest-scientist collaborations by 2030.

### Phase 2: Centennial and synthesis (2031–2035)

The marmot study reaches its centennial in the early 2030s. RMBL marks the milestone publicly — a scientific symposium, a major synthesis volume, regional press coverage — but the substantive work is what the centennial enables: a hundred years of demographic data, now fully digitized and analyzable, available to guest scientists asking questions about climate, sociality, and population persistence that earlier generations could not pose.

The data team produces tools that let guest scientists query across records that previously sat in separate notebooks. Methylation-clock work on the marmots extends into questions the demographic record alone could not answer. Phenology syntheses across the meadow records, the pollinator censuses, and digitized weather records yield findings on lagged climate effects that confirm and sharpen what the 2020s literature could only suggest.

The independence priority pays off in less visible ways. When federal funding tightens further mid-decade — as the central case assumes — RMBL's reserves let it keep stewarding the records and hosting guest scientists without cutting core staff. A handful of guest scientists who lost federal grants find that RMBL remains a place where they can keep working.

### Phase 3: Durability (2036–2040)

The final phase consolidates what the campaign built. The records are protected through protocols that have now survived at least one observer transition. The data team is established as core staff, not grant-funded contingent capacity, and produces tools and synthesis products that guest scientists across the broader mountain-science community use. The marmot centennial volume has reframed how the long record is read in the global literature.

Leadership turns to what the next horizon should look like. The questions that surface — whether to invest in new instrumentation infrastructure, whether to expand community-partnership work, whether to take on cross-mountain comparative roles — are real because the campaign chose not to address them. The board and leadership face a deliberate decision about what the next campaign should be, with the records and the data team already secure.

By 2040 the basin's century-scale records are among the most heavily used long-term ecological datasets in the world. RMBL remains a small nonprofit with a small staff. Its independence is intact. Federal funding has not recovered. The campaign's bet — that records and independence were the right things to protect — looks correct, though the things it did not fund are visibly absent.

## Lines of inquiry

Phenology matures through the period as the central synthesis frontier. Guest scientists, using the digitized records and the AI tools RMBL builds, produce work on lagged climate effects, plasticity-versus-evolution questions, and pollinator-plant rewiring that the 2020s literature could only frame. The marmot project's transition from demography to integrated demographic-genetic-molecular work continues, anchored in the centennial volume.

The long pollinator censuses feed into the global insect-decline conversation with the rare authority that comes from continuous, century-spanning observation. Snowmelt-driven plant demography work matures into mechanistic accounts of how thresholds get crossed in subalpine systems.

What does not mature through this scenario is atmospheric and watershed integration at the scale SAIL began. That work continues — the Department of Energy investment in the East River persists in some form — but RMBL is not the institution leading its next phase. Mechanistic experimental ecology depending on new common-garden or factorial-warming infrastructure does not advance through RMBL investment. Community co-production work with water districts and tribal natural-resources offices stays at roughly its current scale.

The AI-assisted synthesis work, by contrast, becomes a genuine RMBL signature. The institution is recognized as one of the places where a century of ecological records can be asked questions that no individual researcher could have asked.

## Moments of choice within the primary horizon

1. **2027 — How to split the campaign between endowment-style commitments (records stewardship, savings) and capital projects (data-team expansion, archival digitization, RMBL365 renovation)** *Shared inflection: `endowment-capital-split-2028`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and board
   - Alternatives: Tilt strongly toward endowment to lock in long-term protection of records and independence; tilt toward capital to build visible new capacity; balance the two
   - Scenario assumption: The campaign tilts toward endowment-style commitments for records and savings, with a substantial but bounded capital investment in the data team and digitization

2. **2029 — Whether to formalize observer-succession protocols and archival-deposit agreements during the campaign window** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the guest-scientist teams running the marmot and phenology studies
   - Alternatives: Continue informal succession as it has worked historically; formalize protocols and deposit agreements while the senior generation is still active; defer the question
   - Scenario assumption: Protocols and deposit agreements are formalized during the first phase, while the senior generation can shape them

3. **2031 — How to scope the AI-synthesis program — narrow tools for digitized records, or broader infrastructure that other field stations could also use** *Shared inflection: `stewardship-priority-2032`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the data-team lead
   - Alternatives: Build tools narrowly for RMBL records; build broader infrastructure that becomes a shared resource; partner with a peer institution that already has infrastructure
   - Scenario assumption: RMBL builds tools shaped to its own records but designs them so peer institutions can adopt them, without taking on the cost of being a general platform

4. **2033 — How aggressively to grow operating reserves as federal funding tightens further** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: Board, leadership, and the fundraising committee
   - Alternatives: Hold reserves at modest levels and absorb shocks year by year; grow reserves substantially during the campaign to buffer the next decade; shift more of the campaign toward reserves and away from capital
   - Scenario assumption: Reserves grow substantially during the campaign, accepting that this is visible to donors as savings rather than as new capacity

5. **2036 — Whether to extend the data team's role into producing public-facing synthesis products (briefings for water managers, accessible visualizations for community audiences)** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the data team
   - Alternatives: Keep the team focused on internal infrastructure and guest-scientist support; extend into limited public-facing synthesis; build a dedicated translation function
   - Scenario assumption: The team takes on limited public-facing synthesis as a side product of the guest-scientist work, without building a dedicated translation function

6. **2039 — What the next campaign should address, given what this one chose not to fund** *Shared inflection: `next-horizon-2040`.*
   - Actors: Board and leadership
   - Alternatives: Pivot toward atmospheric and watershed integration; pivot toward community co-production; pivot toward cross-mountain comparative work; consolidate further around records and independence
   - Scenario assumption: Leadership opens a deliberate planning process about the next horizon, with the records and data team already secure as a foundation

## Audience lens — research

If you are a guest scientist working on phenology, pollination ecology, marmot demography, or any question that depends on long basin records, this is a period when the records get easier to use. By the early 2030s a century of field notebooks, datasheets, and photographs is digitized and queryable. RMBL's data team builds tools that let you ask synthesis questions across decades without spending years on data wrangling. The protocols and field infrastructure you depend on are stewarded actively rather than left to drift. If your work depends on new atmospheric or watershed instrumentation at RMBL, or on new common-garden or factorial-warming platforms, this scenario is harder for you — those frontiers carry forward through your own grants and partnerships, not through new RMBL investment. If you work in unrestricted-inquiry areas where political constraints are tightening elsewhere, RMBL stays a place where you can work.

## Audience lens — institution

RMBL through this scenario remains a small nonprofit with a small staff. The technical core roughly doubles by 2030 — a second data scientist, a second GIS staffer, an archival digitization technician — and stabilizes there. The institution becomes recognizably more focused: records stewardship and AI-assisted synthesis are the work everyone knows it for. RMBL365 hosts year-round archival and data work but does not become a major in-town campus. The board spends more time on financial reserves and independence than in previous decades. Federal funding stays tight throughout, and the campaign's reserves matter precisely because of that. The institution feels durable rather than expansive. What you work on is clear. What you have chosen not to do is also clear, and the questions about whether that was right come up regularly at board meetings.

## Audience lens — donor

Your contribution joins an effort to protect things that are hard to rebuild once lost: a century of continuous basin records, the institutional independence to keep stewarding them, and the technical capacity to read them at scales prior generations could not attempt. This is not a campaign for new buildings or new instruments. It is a campaign for the data team that makes a hundred years of marmot observation queryable, for the archival digitization that rescues field notebooks from filing cabinets, for the protocols that survive observer transitions, and for the savings that let RMBL keep doing this work when federal funding is thin. Joining this campaign means investing in durability rather than expansion, in records rather than headlines, and in the conditions under which independent inquiry continues.

## Overlay robustness

**Central case.** Under gradual federal contraction, gradual AI evolution, and a mid-range climate trajectory, the scenario plays out roughly as written. Federal funding stays tight but does not collapse. AI tools become useful for synthesis without dramatic disruption. Climate change continues to make the long records more valuable, not less. RMBL's reserves buffer the worst years. The data team produces tools that guest scientists adopt. The marmot centennial is marked successfully. By 2040 the records and the team are durable, and the questions about what was forgone are real but answerable.

**Stress case — Sharper federal collapse.** If federal funding collapses further mid-decade rather than holding tight, the independence priority becomes the campaign's most consequential bet. The reserves buffer the data team and core staff. Some guest scientists lose grants and reduce their basin work. The records continue but at lower throughput. The AI-synthesis work matures more slowly because there are fewer guest scientists producing new analyses. RMBL's signature becomes archival stewardship more than active synthesis. The scenario's choice to forgo new instrumentation looks especially correct in retrospect; the choice to limit community-partnership investment looks more questionable as those partners increasingly look to RMBL for support.

**Stress case — AI moves faster than expected.** If AI tools advance more rapidly than the central case assumes, the data team's role shifts. Off-the-shelf tools handle more of the synthesis work, and RMBL's investment in custom infrastructure looks less distinctive. The team's value moves toward curation, archival quality, and the basin-specific context that general tools cannot provide. The digitization work becomes more valuable, not less. By the mid-2030s RMBL is recognized less as a builder of synthesis tools and more as a steward of the records the tools run on. The marmot centennial volume is produced partly with AI assistance the campaign did not specifically plan for.

**Stress case — Climate change crosses thresholds faster.** If climate change crosses snowpack and ecological thresholds faster than the central case assumes, the long records become more valuable but also more difficult to interpret. The phenology and snowmelt-driven plant work documents transitions in real time. Guest scientists ask increasingly urgent questions about whether the historical baselines still apply to the systems they are observing. The scenario's choice to forgo new instrumentation infrastructure for studying transformed systems looks more painful. RMBL's records remain irreplaceable, but the institution's inability to study what comes after them becomes visible by the late 2030s.

## Plausibility caveats

This scenario assumes federal funding stays roughly at its current contracted level through 2040 — neither recovering substantially nor collapsing further. A sharper collapse stresses the scenario in the ways the stress case describes; a recovery would make some of the forgone investments look like missed opportunities. It assumes the guest-scientist teams running the marmot and phenology studies stay engaged through observer-succession transitions, which depends on academic-institution decisions RMBL does not control. It assumes AI tools become useful for synthesis at roughly the pace the central case describes; faster or slower trajectories change what the data team produces.

It assumes RMBL can grow its in-house data and archival capacity by roughly the amount described without straining the small-nonprofit operating model. Hiring senior data scientists in a competitive market may be harder than the scenario assumes. It assumes donors find records-stewardship and independence compelling as campaign priorities, which is a real bet — these are less visible than new buildings and new instruments.

The structural blind spot the scenario most clearly carries is its narrow community-engagement posture. The planning themes describe substantial stakeholder pull from water managers, tribal natural-resources offices, and land-management agencies that this scenario does not engage with new investment. If that pull becomes urgent during the horizon — through a water crisis, a regulatory deadline, or a community partnership opportunity — the scenario's choice to forgo that work may look harder to justify than it does in 2026.

It also assumes that protecting the records and the independence is the right bet for the moment, rather than investing in the next generation of basin science infrastructure. Reasonable people would weigh this differently.

## Looking further out: 2040–2050

*Speculative. Lower resolution than the primary horizon.*

Looking out toward 2050, the scenario gets hazier. If the records and the data team prove as durable as the campaign intends, RMBL in mid-century is recognized as one of a small number of institutions in the world stewarding ecological records long enough to anchor planetary-scale climate questions. The marmot study is well past its centennial. The phenology records cross seventy-five years. The basin remains a place where guest scientists come because the records are here and accessible.

Whether RMBL takes on new instrumentation, new community-partnership infrastructure, or new cross-mountain roles by 2050 depends on what the next campaign chooses. Leadership in the late 2030s opens that question deliberately. If federal funding has recovered, the next campaign could be expansive. If it has not, the next campaign may look much like this one. The scenario does not predict which. What it does claim is that a century of records and the independence to keep stewarding them are foundations from which many futures remain possible — and that without them, fewer do.
