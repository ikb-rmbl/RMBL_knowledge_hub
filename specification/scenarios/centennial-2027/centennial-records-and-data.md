# Centennial Records and Data

*A Future Scenario in the `centennial-2027` set. Floor magnitude, continuity-leaning. v1.0 under [Future Scenarios Framework v0.7](../../Future_scenarios_framework.md).*

---

## Synopsis

Centennial Records and Data assumes the campaign comes in modest, and that RMBL responds with a focused two-part bet: protect the basin's longest records, and grow a small data team that can finally read those records at scales prior generations could not attempt. The marmot study passes its seventy-fifth year in this period. The long meadow phenology series and snowmelt-driven plant work continue without lapse. Alongside that stewardship, RMBL adds a few data scientists and archivists, digitizes a century of field notebooks and plot records, and builds the tools to make them machine-readable. About two-thirds of the work is continuity; the rest is the digital and analytical capacity that turns archival depth into living research. The scenario forgoes new atmospheric instrumentation, broad community partnership programs, and large financial reserves. It asks donors and the institution to believe that a narrow, well-chosen pair of investments at the campaign floor can matter more than a wider spread.

### Campaign deliverables

- **primary.** Stewardship of the basin's longest continuous records — the marmot demographic study, the long meadow phenology series, and the snowmelt-driven plant work. This means protocol continuity, plot infrastructure, archival systems, and the technical staff who support the guest scientists carrying the records forward through observer transitions.
- **primary.** Growth of RMBL's in-house data team — adding a few data scientists, an archivist, and AI-tool capacity to the existing technical core. This is the distinctive bet of the scenario: a small, focused team whose work is to make a century of basin records readable, searchable, and analyzable in ways that were not possible before.
- **secondary.** Digitization of the basin's archival record — field notebooks, plot maps, hand-written census sheets, photographs, and decades of correspondence that exist only on paper. The work is unglamorous and slow, but it is the raw material the data team and the broader research community need.
- **supporting.** Tool development and data infrastructure that lets guest scientists work with the digitized records — extending the Knowledge Commons platform, building search and synthesis tools, and developing AI-assisted methods in collaboration with researchers using the archive.
- **continuity_residual.** Modest sustaining investment in existing facilities and operations to keep the Gothic site functional through the horizon. No major new construction; no renovation of RMBL365 beyond what is already underway.

### Frontier support strategies

- **F.cont.1.** The campaign protects the marmot, meadow, and snowmelt records as continuous datasets. It pays for the protocol stewardship, plot infrastructure, and archival systems that keep observation unbroken through the horizon, and supports the small technical staff who help guest scientists carry the records forward. No new long-term observation lines are launched; the bet is on protecting what already exists.
- **F.innov.3.** The campaign grows RMBL's in-house data team and digitizes the archival record so that a century of basin science becomes machine-readable. The data scientists build tools, develop AI-assisted methods, and partner with guest scientists on retrospective syntheses the original observers could not have attempted. This frontier depends on the records frontier for its source material, and the two together form a coherent pair at modest scale.

### Forgone

The scenario forgoes most of what a larger campaign might pursue. There is no new atmospheric or watershed instrumentation built on basin grounds — the SAIL-style integrated mountain Earth-systems work continues only as guest scientists bring their own equipment. There is no broad community-science or stakeholder co-production program; partnerships with water districts, tribal nations, and land-management agencies continue informally rather than through a dedicated team. RMBL365 is not renovated into laboratory space during the horizon. The campaign does not build the financial reserves that would buffer RMBL against a sharp funding shock. No new mechanistic experimental platforms are launched. The scenario asks the institution to accept a narrow scope as the price of doing two things well at a modest scale.

### Seeds in the present

The scenario builds on capacities that already exist. RMBL's technical core — a geospatial data scientist, a GIS manager, and instrumentation technicians — is the foundation the data team grows from. The Knowledge Commons itself is a product of the recent pattern in which outside grants build RMBL's own tools, which then attract more grants; this scenario extends that pattern at modest scale. The marmot demographic record, the long meadow phenology series, and the snowmelt-driven plant work are the records the campaign protects. The planning-pipeline themes around long-term integrated demographic and genetic time series, and around the integration infrastructure that makes existing records usable, both point at the same gap this scenario addresses. Recent landmark work — the universal DNA methylation clock applied to marmots, the cross-taxon phenology synthesis — shows what becomes possible when long records meet new analytical tools.

## Setting

This scenario assumes the Centennial Campaign comes in modest. Federal science funding stays tight through the horizon. The donor base supports stewardship but does not stretch to a broad expansion. RMBL responds by narrowing rather than spreading: two frontiers, chosen because they reinforce each other, funded at the campaign floor.

The first is the basin's longest records. The marmot study at Gothic now in its eighth decade passes 75 years during the horizon. The long meadow phenology series, the snowmelt-driven plant work, and the pollinator censuses are all entering decades where a single lapse in observation would be irreparable. The campaign protects them — the protocols, the plot markers, the archival systems, the technical staff who support the guest scientists doing the work.

The second is a small data team. RMBL adds a few people — data scientists, an archivist, AI-tool capacity — to the existing technical core. Their job is to make a century of basin science readable. Field notebooks come off shelves and into databases. Hand-written census sheets become searchable. The tools they build let guest scientists ask questions of the archive that the original observers could not have framed.

The bet is that these two investments compound. Protected records are the raw material; the data team is what turns archival depth into living research. Neither would be as valuable alone. At the campaign floor, doing both at modest scale is what the scenario claims is possible.

## The arc — three phases across the primary horizon

### Phase 1: Stewardship and the first hires (2026–2030)

The campaign closes in this phase at the campaign floor. RMBL leadership and the board commit to the two-frontier focus and accept what is not funded. The first new data-team hires arrive — a data scientist and an archivist join the existing technical core by 2028. The archivist begins the long work of digitizing field notebooks, starting with the marmot demographic record and the meadow phenology series. The data scientist works alongside guest scientists to build search and analysis tools on top of the Knowledge Commons platform. On the records side, observer succession in the marmot study moves through a planned transition as long-serving field workers hand off to the next generation. Protocols are documented in greater detail than ever before, partly because the digitization work surfaces gaps. The community of guest scientists notices the data tools first; the broader research community notices the protected continuity. By 2030, the basic shape of the bet is visible: small team, large archive, intact records.

### Phase 2: The archive becomes readable (2031–2035)

The digitization work crosses a threshold. By 2032, the marmot record, the meadow phenology series, and the long pollinator censuses are all available as structured, searchable data. The data team grows by one or two more positions — an AI-tools specialist joins, and the team begins partnering with guest scientists on retrospective syntheses that would not have been possible before. The marmot study passes its seventy-fifth year in this phase. The seventy-fifth-year milestone is marked quietly — a workshop, a synthesis paper drawing on the now-digitized record, attention from regional press. Guest scientists begin using the archive in new ways: a researcher asks how floral traits have shifted across fifty years of meadow records; another links the methylation-clock work to the now-digitized demographic record. RMBL's role shifts subtly. The institution is no longer only a place where field work happens; it is also a place where a century of accumulated field work can be read at scale. The data team is small — five or six people by 2035 — but their leverage is large.

### Phase 3: The bet's consequences (2036–2040)

The compounding effect of the two-frontier bet becomes visible by the end of the horizon. The records are intact. The archive is digital, structured, and increasingly cross-linked. The data team has matured into a stable group whose work attracts external grants on its own terms — the same flywheel pattern that built the Knowledge Commons in the prior decade, now operating around the archive and the synthesis tools. Guest-scientist publications drawing on the digitized records appear regularly; a noticeable share name RMBL data-team members as collaborators. The constraints of the modest campaign are also visible. RMBL has no in-town laboratory space. Community partnerships remain informal. The institution carries less buffer than it would prefer against funding shocks. A federal-funding shock in this phase would be felt sharply, because the campaign did not build the reserves to absorb one. The scenario ends with a question: was the narrow bet right? On the evidence inside the horizon, the records and the readable archive are the lasting things. Whether the institution could have done more with a wider scope is unprovable.

## Lines of inquiry

The phenology work matures into what it has been pointing toward for two decades: a record long enough, and now structured enough, to separate plastic responses from evolutionary change at the species level. The data team's tools let researchers cross-reference the meadow series with the marmot record, with the pollinator censuses, and with the molecular work that has accumulated since the early 2020s. Retrospective syntheses become a recognizable mode of basin science — papers that ask what a century of observation actually shows, drawing on records that earlier generations could see only in fragments. The marmot work integrates demographic, behavioral, and molecular data on the same individuals across decades. The pollinator records support reanalyses that respond to the broader scientific concern about insect decline.

What does not mature is the atmosphere-to-bedrock integration that defined the prior era. SAIL-style coupled mountain Earth-systems work continues only as guest scientists bring their own equipment to the basin; RMBL does not build new instrumentation infrastructure. Mechanistic experimental platforms — common gardens, factorial manipulations at scale — are not added. The translation work that connects basin science to water management and land-use decisions stays informal. These are real lines of inquiry the scenario does not advance.

## Moments of choice within the primary horizon

1. **2027 — Whether to organize the campaign around two frontiers at modest scale or to attempt a wider portfolio at the same magnitude.** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the board
   - Alternatives: A wider campaign covering three or four frontiers, each at thinner support; or the narrow two-frontier focus this scenario chooses.
   - Scenario assumption: The board commits to the narrow focus, accepting that the campaign forgoes community partnerships, new instrumentation, and reserves.

2. **2028 — How to balance permanent endowment against immediate capital deployment within the campaign.** *Shared inflection: `endowment-capital-split-2028`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the Development team
   - Alternatives: Heavier endowment to sustain the data team in perpetuity; heavier near-term capital to digitize the archive faster; or a balance between them.
   - Scenario assumption: RMBL chooses a balance that funds the first hires immediately and builds modest endowment to sustain them through the horizon.

3. **2029 — How to sequence the first data-team hires — archivist first, data scientist first, or in parallel.** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the technical core
   - Alternatives: Hire the archivist first and begin digitization before tool-building; hire the data scientist first and build tools before the archive is ready; or hire both in parallel and accept the coordination cost.
   - Scenario assumption: RMBL hires both in parallel by 2028, accepting that the archivist's work moves faster than the data scientist's tool-building in the first two years.

4. **2032 — Which of the basin's long records receives the strongest stewardship investment as observer transitions accelerate.** *Shared inflection: `stewardship-priority-2032`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and guest-scientist community
   - Alternatives: Concentrate stewardship resources on the marmot study as it passes its seventy-fifth year; spread support across the marmot, meadow, and pollinator records; or let the records community set priorities.
   - Scenario assumption: Stewardship is spread across the marmot, meadow, and pollinator records, with the seventy-fifth year marked through a workshop and synthesis rather than dedicated funding.

5. **2034 — Whether to open the digitized archive broadly to outside AI research groups or to keep tool development inside RMBL's data-team partnerships.** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the data team
   - Alternatives: Open the archive widely and accept that some uses will not credit or partner with RMBL; keep tighter control through collaboration agreements; or develop a tiered access model.
   - Scenario assumption: RMBL develops a tiered access model that preserves credit and collaboration norms while keeping the underlying records broadly available to the research community.

6. **2038 — How to plan the next horizon — whether to extend the narrow-focus model or to use the matured data team as the basis for a wider second campaign.** *Shared inflection: `next-horizon-2040`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the board
   - Alternatives: Continue the focused two-frontier model into the 2040s; mount a wider second campaign that adds instrumentation, community work, and reserves; or wait until external conditions clarify.
   - Scenario assumption: RMBL leadership begins planning a wider second campaign using the matured data team as one of several anchors, but defers the decision until the federal-funding environment is clearer.

## Audience lens — research

If you work on phenology, pollination, demography, or any line of inquiry that depends on the basin's long records, this scenario is the one that makes your work easier through the period. The records you rely on stay intact. The archives you might once have visited in person become searchable from your desk. The data team at RMBL will partner with you on retrospective syntheses, help you cross-reference your observations against decades of prior work, and build tools that match the questions you bring. If your work depends on atmospheric instrumentation, new mechanistic experiments, or community partnerships, this scenario offers less — those threads continue through whatever you and your home institution can sustain on your own. The trade is sharp and visible. What the scenario gives you is a century of basin science you can actually read.

## Audience lens — institution

RMBL through this period stays small, focused, and quiet. The Gothic site looks much as it does now. The technical core grows by four or five people — data scientists, an archivist, an AI-tools specialist — and that growth is the most visible change. The data team becomes a recognizable part of the institution, the way the GIS and instrumentation staff are now. Day-to-day work in summer feels familiar: guest scientists arrive, fieldwork happens, the records continue. What changes is what happens around the records the rest of the year. Archives that lived in file cabinets are increasingly online. Synthesis papers drawing on the digitized record appear regularly in journals. The institution is doing less than a wider campaign would have funded, and the constraints show. But the work it does is durable and visibly its own.

## Overlay robustness

**Central case.** Under the central case — gradual federal contraction, gradual AI evolution, and an RCP6.0 climate trajectory — the scenario plays out roughly as written. The records continue. The data team grows at modest pace. Digitization completes on the core records by the middle of the horizon. AI tools mature alongside the data team's work, so the synthesis capacity matches what the archive can support. The constraints of the campaign floor are real throughout but not crippling.

**Stress case — Sharp federal funding shock.** A deeper federal contraction in the early 2030s tests the scenario hard. Because the campaign did not build financial reserves, RMBL absorbs the shock through operating-budget compression. The data team holds, but barely; one position goes unfilled, and the pace of digitization slows. The records continue because the field-season investments are protected first. The scenario ends the horizon with its core bet intact but with less institutional cushion than would be prudent.

**Stress case — Rapid AI capability acceleration.** Faster-than-expected AI capability shifts the leverage of the small data team upward. The synthesis tools the team builds in the late 2020s are overtaken by general-purpose models within a few years; the team's work pivots toward curation, validation, and domain-specific tooling rather than building analytical methods from scratch. The archive becomes valuable to a wider research community than the scenario anticipated, including groups outside ecology. RMBL's tiered-access model is tested by demand it was not designed for.

**Stress case — An observer-succession failure in a core record.** A core record — the marmot study or the long meadow series — loses key personnel without a planned successor in place. The campaign did not fund redundant observer capacity. The record gaps for a season or two; partial coverage continues through guest-scientist effort, but the continuity that justified the whole scenario is compromised in one line. The data-team work continues regardless, but the scenario's central claim — that protected records plus readable archive compound — is weakened in the affected line.

## Plausibility caveats

The scenario assumes the campaign reaches the campaign floor rather than falling short of it. A campaign that closes well below this floor would not support the data team at the size the scenario describes; the digitization work would slow and some hires would not happen. The scenario also assumes that federal funding stays tight but does not collapse entirely. A sharp federal-funding shock would test the scenario hard because it does not build the financial reserves that would buffer one — this is the characteristic failure mode of the narrow bet. The scenario assumes AI tools mature steadily enough to support the synthesis work without overtaking the small team's distinctive contribution; if general-purpose models advance much faster, the team's work pivots toward curation and validation rather than methods development.

A structural blind spot: the scenario takes the donor base as supporting stewardship and modest in-house growth without testing whether donors will fund the unglamorous work of digitization and tool-building at the scale required. If donors prefer visible capital projects — buildings, instrumentation, community spaces — the campaign may close at this magnitude but with deliverables that do not match the scenario's strategic shape. A second blind spot: the scenario assumes observer succession in the long records proceeds without crisis, but the specific people who carry institutional knowledge of these records are not fungible. The scenario's continuity claim depends on succession planning the campaign supports but does not fully control.

## Looking further out: 2040–2050

*Speculative. Lower resolution than the primary horizon.*

Through the 2040s, the consequences of the narrow bet become clearer. The digitized archive matures into a resource that defines what basin science means to a generation of researchers who never visited Gothic in paper-and-pencil years. The marmot study, now in its eighth decade, becomes one of a small handful of mammal records long enough to read evolutionary change directly. Retrospective syntheses drawing on the basin's full archival depth appear regularly in major journals. The small data team that built the tools is, by 2050, one of the most consequential additions to RMBL's small-scale infrastructure in decades — not because it grew large, but because its leverage on a century of accumulated observation turned out to be durable. Whether RMBL by 2050 has also rebuilt the breadth the narrow campaign forwent — community partnerships, integrated instrumentation, financial reserves — depends on a second campaign the scenario does not attempt to describe. The further future is hazier than the horizon, and intentionally so.
