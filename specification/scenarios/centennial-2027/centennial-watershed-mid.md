# Centennial Watershed-Anchored

*A Future Scenario in the `centennial-2027` set. Mid-range magnitude, balanced. v1.0 under [Future Scenarios Framework v0.7](../../Future_scenarios_framework.md).*

---

## Synopsis

Centennial Watershed-Anchored assumes the campaign comes in mid-range, and that RMBL bets the bulk of it on keeping the East River watershed operating as a coupled-systems testbed after the federal SAIL campaign winds down. The atmospheric instruments, snow and soil moisture stations, and biogeochemistry sampling that SAIL brought to the basin become permanent RMBL infrastructure, run by an expanded technical staff. The long records — the marmot study reaching its hundredth year, the meadow phenology series, the snowmelt-driven plant work — continue without lapse, because those records are what give the watershed measurements their depth. A small data team grows alongside, enough to weave the instrument streams together. The scenario forgoes broad community co-production work, large financial reserves, and most mechanistic experimental platforms outside the watershed. It tests something specific: whether the watershed turn that defined basin science after 2016 can be made permanent at mid-magnitude, rather than waiting for a target-level campaign that may never come.

### Campaign deliverables

- **primary.** Permanent operation of the atmospheric, snowpack, and biogeochemistry measurement systems that the federal SAIL campaign installed across the East River watershed, transitioned from campaign infrastructure to RMBL-operated infrastructure. This is the distinctive bet: the radars, radiometers, flux towers, and stream chemistry stations stay running past the campaign window, maintained by RMBL technicians rather than dispersed when federal funding ends.
- **primary.** Growth of RMBL's in-house technical staff — instrumentation technicians, a watershed data engineer, and added geospatial capacity — to operate and integrate the watershed measurement systems. This is also distinctive: the staff needed to keep coupled atmosphere-to-bedrock measurements running is larger and more specialized than the current technical core.
- **secondary.** Continued stewardship of the basin's longest records — the marmot demographic study reaching its hundredth year, the meadow phenology series, the snowmelt-driven plant demography work — through protocol support, plot infrastructure, archival systems, and the technical staff that supports the guest scientists carrying the records forward.
- **secondary.** Data infrastructure that lets the watershed instrument streams, the long biological records, and the basin's archival depth be read together. Includes archival digitization of historical field records and modest in-house AI capacity for synthesis across the combined data.
- **supporting.** Facility investments that host the guest-scientist collaborations the watershed work depends on — wet-lab and staging space at Gothic, modest renovation at the year-round RMBL365 building in Crested Butte for off-season analysis and coordination.
- **continuity_residual.** Baseline operating support for the housing, facilities, and core staff that allow the seasonal community at Gothic to function each summer, on which all the above depends.

### Frontier support strategies

- **F.cont.1.** The campaign protects the marmot, meadow, and snowmelt records by funding protocol stewardship, plot infrastructure, archival systems, and the technical staff that supports the guest scientists who carry the records forward. The marmot study crosses its hundredth year inside the horizon. The records are also the deep-time spine that gives the watershed measurements their meaning — a soil moisture station reads differently when it sits next to a fifty-year flowering record.
- **F.innov.1.** This is where the scenario's distinctive weight lands. The atmospheric, snowpack, and biogeochemistry instruments that SAIL installed transition from a federal campaign into permanent RMBL-operated infrastructure. RMBL hires the technicians and data engineers needed to keep them running. The campaign funds the year-round operating cost, the equipment replacement cycle, and the integrated data systems that let guest scientists from atmospheric science, hydrology, soil microbiology, and ecology share the same measurements.
- **F.innov.3.** A small data team grows — a few staff added to the current technical core — to make the watershed instrument streams and the long biological records readable together. The team digitizes historical field notebooks and plot records, builds tools that let guest scientists query a century of basin data alongside live watershed measurements, and develops AI-assisted methods for finding patterns across the combined record. The work is sized to support the watershed integration rather than to stand alone.

### Forgone

The scenario does not fund broad community co-production work. Partnerships with the Ute Indian Tribe, Gunnison Valley water districts, and county schools continue at their current modest scale rather than expanding into a defining feature of the campaign. RMBL365 is renovated only enough to support off-season watershed coordination, not staffed as a year-round community hub. The scenario does not build large financial reserves; institutional independence rests on the diversified grant base the watershed infrastructure attracts rather than on substantial endowed savings. It does not fund new mechanistic experimental platforms outside the watershed — no large common-garden infrastructure, no factorial warming arrays, no expanded pollinator manipulation experiments. The translation work that would carry basin findings into Mountain West water management happens informally through guest scientists, not through dedicated RMBL staff. These are real costs. A donor who reads the scenario as community-first or as resilience-first will find it does something else.

### Seeds in the present

The seed is the SAIL campaign described in Feldman et al. 2023, which from 2021 onward installed radars, radiometers, and flux instrumentation across the East River watershed. The East River testbed described in Hubbard et al. 2018 and the snow-fed groundwater work in Carroll et al. 2018 established the watershed as a Department of Energy community research site. The recent Kerins et al. 2025 work showing how snow, soil, and steepness drive diverging streamflow trends is exactly the kind of integrated question the watershed infrastructure was built to ask. The hillslope clustering framework from Wainwright et al. 2022 gives the watershed analytical units that ecologists and biogeochemists can share. RMBL's existing technical core — a geospatial data scientist, a GIS manager, and instrumentation technicians — is the staff this scenario expands. The Knowledge Commons itself, a product of the recent pattern where outside grants build RMBL's own tools and capacity, is the model for how watershed data infrastructure would be built.

## Setting

This scenario takes as its starting point a specific bet about what mattered most in the basin's recent past and what is most at risk of being lost. From 2021 onward, the Surface Atmosphere Integrated Field Laboratory transformed the East River watershed into a place where atmospheric scientists, hydrologists, soil microbiologists, and ecologists could share instruments and study reaches. The radars and radiometers and flux towers, combined with the basin's long biological records, made it possible to ask integrated questions that no other mountain site could support. SAIL was a federal campaign, time-limited by design.

The central question the scenario answers is what happens when that campaign winds down. The default outcome is dispersal: instruments are removed or transferred elsewhere, the scientific community that formed around the watershed scatters, and the integration retreats to the level of conference panels and review papers. This scenario chooses something different. The campaign comes in mid-range, and most of it goes toward keeping the watershed infrastructure operational past the federal window. RMBL takes on the instruments. RMBL hires the technicians who maintain them. RMBL builds the data systems that let the measurements be used.

The basin's long records — the marmot study, the meadow phenology series, the snowmelt-driven plant work — continue at current intensity. They are protected because they are part of what makes the watershed measurements valuable; a soil chemistry reading next to a fifty-year flowering record is a different kind of evidence than a soil chemistry reading alone. What the scenario does not do is expand into community partnership work, build large financial reserves, or launch new experimental platforms outside the watershed. The bet is concentration, not balance.

## The arc — three phases across the primary horizon

### Phase 1: Transition from campaign to infrastructure (2026–2030)

The campaign's first phase is dominated by the question of what stays. SAIL's federal funding cycle winds down across this period. RMBL leadership negotiates with the Department of Energy and partner institutions about which instruments transfer to RMBL ownership, which stay with their home institutions, and which are decommissioned. The campaign funds the technician hires needed to operate the inherited instruments — initially two new positions added to the existing technical core, with a third by the end of the phase. The basin's long records continue without disruption. The marmot study completes its hundredth year of continuous observation in 2027. A small data engineering position is added to begin building the integrated platform that will let watershed instrument streams be queried alongside the long biological records. The renovation at RMBL365 is modest — winterized workspace for off-season data analysis and coordination meetings, not a community hub. By 2030 the inherited watershed instruments are running under RMBL operation and the first guest-scientist projects using them as permanent rather than campaign infrastructure are underway.

### Phase 2: Watershed infrastructure as standard (2031–2035)

By the early 2030s the basin's watershed measurements are no longer a federal campaign with an expiration date. They are RMBL infrastructure that guest scientists plan multi-year work around. The technical staff has grown by about four positions from its 2026 baseline. The data platform is operational and lets a soil microbiologist, an atmospheric scientist, and an ecologist share the same measurement context. The first wave of guest-scientist publications using the post-SAIL infrastructure appears. The integrated questions the SAIL campaign opened — how snow microphysics, soil moisture, bedrock chemistry, and plant communities co-vary across the watershed — now have a sustained empirical base rather than a campaign-bounded one. Equipment replacement becomes a routine cost the campaign anticipated. The long records continue. The meadow phenology series enters its sixth decade. Archival digitization of historical field notebooks accelerates, and the data team begins building AI-assisted tools that let researchers query the digitized archive alongside live instrument streams. The scenario's bet is now visible: the watershed turn that defined basin science after 2016 has become permanent.

### Phase 3: Coupled-systems testbed at depth (2036–2040)

The final phase of the primary horizon is about what the sustained infrastructure produces. By 2036 the watershed has been operating as an RMBL-run integrated testbed for nearly a decade. Guest scientists who could not have proposed their work in 2026 — because the federal campaign was ending and the infrastructure's future was uncertain — now run multi-year projects using the coupled measurements. The basin's contribution to mountain-system science is no longer a campaign chapter but a sustained record. The long records remain the deep-time spine. The marmot study, the meadow phenology series, and the snowmelt-driven plant work continue under their existing partnerships, with archival and protocol support from RMBL staff. The data team's AI-assisted synthesis tools, modest by national standards, are tuned to the specific problem of reading a century of basin records alongside watershed instrument streams. The campaign closes in 2040 having done one thing well: it kept the East River operating as a coupled-systems testbed past the moment when, by default, it would have dispersed.

## Lines of inquiry

The integration of atmospheric, hydrological, and biological measurement deepens across the horizon. Questions that SAIL opened — how snow microphysics shapes soil moisture, how bedrock chemistry contributes to stream nitrogen, how shifting snowmelt cascades through plant communities — gain the sustained record that lets them be answered rather than only formulated. The recent finding that snow, soil, and steepness drive diverging streamflow trends across the watershed is the kind of work that now has a multi-decade arc rather than a single campaign window. The basin's long records remain active research objects. The marmot study, having crossed its hundredth year, continues to feed work on social structure, climate vulnerability, and molecular age estimation. The meadow phenology series and the snowmelt-driven plant demography work continue to anchor questions about plasticity, evolution, and the timing of mountain biology. Plant-pollinator network research continues at current intensity through guest-scientist partnerships, without the expansion that a different scenario might bring. What does not mature here is mechanistic experimentation outside the watershed frame — large common-garden installations, factorial warming arrays at landscape scale, expanded pollinator manipulation infrastructure. Community co-production work continues at modest scale rather than becoming a research line in its own right. The translation of basin findings into Mountain West water management happens through guest scientists and informal channels, not through a dedicated RMBL function.

## Moments of choice within the primary horizon

1. **2027 — What happens to the SAIL instrumentation when the federal campaign winds down** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership, the Department of Energy program offices, and the partner national laboratories that ran SAIL
   - Alternatives: Let the campaign end on its federal schedule and accept that most instruments leave the basin; negotiate transfer of a subset to RMBL operation; pursue a successor federal campaign
   - Scenario assumption: RMBL negotiates transfer of the core atmospheric, snowpack, and biogeochemistry instruments to RMBL operation, with partner institutions retaining specialized equipment

2. **2028 — How the campaign splits between operating reserves and the capital and staffing needed to inherit watershed infrastructure** *Shared inflection: `endowment-capital-split-2028`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership, the board, and the Development team
   - Alternatives: Heavier endowment to buffer against future shocks; heavier capital and staffing investment to make the watershed transition succeed; balanced split
   - Scenario assumption: The split tilts heavily toward capital and staffing for the watershed transition, with operating reserves built only modestly

3. **2029 — How large the technical staff grows and how specialized those new positions are** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL technical leadership and the board
   - Alternatives: Hire generalists who can support multiple infrastructure types; hire deep specialists in atmospheric instrumentation and watershed data engineering; outsource specialized maintenance to vendors
   - Scenario assumption: Hire deep specialists — instrumentation technicians and a watershed data engineer — accepting that this raises long-term salary commitments

4. **2031 — Whether to build an integrated data platform in-house or rely on partner-institution platforms for the watershed data** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the data team
   - Alternatives: Adopt a partner institution's platform and contribute basin data to it; build an RMBL-operated platform that integrates watershed instrument streams with the basin's long records; do both at partial cost
   - Scenario assumption: Build an RMBL-operated platform, on the model of the Knowledge Commons, that makes the watershed data and the long records queryable together

5. **2032 — How to prioritize stewardship of the long biological records against the expanded watershed work** *Shared inflection: `stewardship-priority-2032`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the guest-scientist community carrying the long records
   - Alternatives: Let the long records receive whatever staff attention remains after watershed needs; carve out protected stewardship capacity for the records; pull staff from records to watershed during transition years
   - Scenario assumption: Carve out protected stewardship capacity for the long records, treating them as part of the watershed value proposition rather than a competing demand

6. **2035 — How to fund equipment replacement and instrument upgrades as the inherited SAIL hardware ages** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership, the board, and the Development team
   - Alternatives: Allow capability to degrade gradually; pursue federal grants for replacement cycles; build a dedicated equipment replacement reserve within the campaign
   - Scenario assumption: Pursue federal and foundation grants for replacement cycles, treating the watershed infrastructure as the platform that attracts that funding

7. **2039 — How to frame the next horizon as the centennial campaign closes** *Shared inflection: `next-horizon-2040`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the board, in dialogue with major donors
   - Alternatives: Position the watershed infrastructure as the foundation for a larger atmospheric and biogeochemistry expansion; pivot toward community partnership or resilience priorities the campaign forgoes; consolidate and protect what was built
   - Scenario assumption: Position the watershed infrastructure as the foundation for the next phase, with explicit recognition that what was built is also what must continue to be funded

## Audience lens — research

A scientist working on snow physics, watershed hydrology, soil biogeochemistry, or mountain ecology gets something through this scenario that other basin scenarios do not provide: a coupled measurement context that continues past the federal campaign window. The radars and radiometers, the soil moisture stations, the stream chemistry sampling — these run as standing infrastructure that you can plan multi-year work around rather than racing to publish before the campaign ends. The basin's long biological records remain active, which means your watershed measurements sit next to a century of context. The trade is real. If your work depends on large common-garden experiments, expanded pollinator manipulation, or community co-production partnerships, this scenario gives you less than a sibling would. If your work depends on the East River operating as a coupled testbed, this is the scenario where it does.

## Audience lens — institution

Through this scenario, RMBL becomes a place that operates standing watershed infrastructure on the model that national laboratories and federal facilities used to. The technical staff is larger than it is in 2026 — four or so additional specialists, including instrumentation technicians and a watershed data engineer. The seasonal rhythm at Gothic continues, but a meaningful share of staff now works year-round on instrument maintenance, data integration, and equipment replacement cycles. The institution carries more recurring operating cost than it did before, balanced by the grant flow that the watershed infrastructure attracts. RMBL365 in Crested Butte is renovated modestly, used for off-season analysis and coordination rather than as a community hub. The institution's distinctive shape — small staff, guest-scientist research model, broad responsive infrastructure investment — holds, but the technical core is meaningfully larger and more specialized than it was at the campaign's start.

## Overlay robustness

**Central case.** Under gradual federal contraction, the Department of Energy maintains some watershed science funding but at reduced scale, and the SAIL successor campaigns that might have replaced this scenario's bet do not materialize at the size needed. RMBL's inherited infrastructure becomes the basin's continuity. AI tools mature steadily, and the modest in-house data team uses them effectively for integration across the watershed and long records. Climate tracks an intermediate warming path: snowpack continues to thin, the foresummer dries further, and the questions the watershed infrastructure was built to ask remain urgent. The scenario plays close to its design intent.

**Stress case — Sharper federal contraction with watershed funding cut.** If federal funding for watershed science contracts more sharply than the central case assumes, the grants that this scenario expects to fund equipment replacement and ongoing operation do not arrive. RMBL is left operating instruments with a recurring cost burden that the campaign was not sized to carry alone. The institution must either let infrastructure degrade selectively, reduce technical staff, or draw from the modest reserves the campaign built. The watershed bet partly fails: the instruments stay in the basin but operate at reduced intensity, and the integrated questions are still asked but at lower data density.

**Stress case — Rapid AI maturation outpaces the scenario's data investment.** If AI tools mature faster than the central case assumes, the modest in-house data team the scenario funds is undersized for the synthesis work the watershed infrastructure makes possible. Guest scientists with their own AI capacity extract more value from the data than RMBL's own platform supports, which is acceptable but means the institution captures less of the synthesis function than it could. RMBL becomes more a data provider and less a synthesis partner. The scenario's central watershed bet still holds; the data dimension is the weak point.

**Stress case — Climate threshold crossed in the basin.** If snowpack thins past a threshold that fundamentally changes the watershed's hydrology — multiple low-snow years in succession, or sustained early melt-out — the questions the SAIL-era infrastructure was designed to ask shift. The instruments remain useful but are now measuring a post-snowpack system rather than the snow-dominated one they were built around. The scenario's coupled-systems bet still pays off, because the integration is what reveals the threshold crossing, but the research program the watershed supports reorients toward the transformed basin.

## Plausibility caveats

The scenario assumes that the Department of Energy and partner institutions will agree to transfer core SAIL instruments to RMBL operation rather than removing them or transferring them to another site. This negotiation is not guaranteed, and a less favorable outcome would partly hollow out the scenario's central bet. It assumes that grant flow tied to the watershed infrastructure continues at a level that funds equipment replacement and ongoing operation across the horizon. If federal watershed science funding contracts more sharply than the central case assumes, the campaign's mid-range scale is not sufficient to carry the recurring cost alone. It assumes that the technical specialists the scenario hires can be recruited and retained at competitive salaries in a mountain location with limited housing — a real challenge that the scenario addresses only modestly through RMBL365.

The characteristic way this scenario could go wrong is specific: the campaign succeeds in inheriting the instruments but fails to sustain the operating cost across the full horizon. RMBL ends 2040 holding infrastructure it cannot fully maintain, having forgone the community partnership work and the reserves that a different scenario would have built. This is the cost of concentration.

The structural blind spot is community. By choosing the watershed bet at mid-range, the scenario leaves community engagement at its current modest scale during a period when local water and climate concerns are intensifying. If community salience rises faster than the scenario anticipates, RMBL may find itself missing a strategic moment that a different scenario would have caught. The scenario also assumes that the guest-scientist community that formed around SAIL stays engaged with the basin after the campaign ends. If those scientists disperse to other sites despite the inherited infrastructure, the bet's value falls.

## Looking further out: 2040–2050

*Speculative. Lower resolution than the primary horizon.*

By 2050 the watershed measurements have been running as RMBL infrastructure for roughly two decades. The first generation of guest scientists who started multi-year projects under the post-campaign infrastructure has been replaced by a second generation who never knew the East River as a federal campaign — only as a place where coupled measurements were available. The marmot study has crossed a hundred and twenty years. The meadow phenology series is approaching seven decades. The data platform that the campaign built is older and the AI tools that read across the records are deeper than what the 2030s could support. What the scenario cannot say is whether the recurring operating cost remained sustainable across the full quarter-century, or whether the institution eventually had to choose between watershed maintenance and the long records. That choice may not have been forced. It may have been. The scenario's coda is honest about the unknowability of that further future, while naming what it bet on: that depth in one place, sustained past the moment of natural dispersal, was worth the things it did not pursue.
