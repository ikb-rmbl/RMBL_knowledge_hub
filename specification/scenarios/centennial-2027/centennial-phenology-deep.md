# Centennial Phenology-Deep

*A Future Scenario in the `centennial-2027` set. Target magnitude, balanced. v1.0 under [Future Scenarios Framework v0.7](../../Future_scenarios_framework.md).*

---

## Synopsis

Centennial Phenology-Deep assumes the campaign comes in near the upper end of what RMBL might raise, and that the institution makes one focused intellectual bet: use the basin's century of phenology and pollinator records, read with new AI tools, to finally resolve whether plasticity within generations or evolution across them dominates how mountain ecosystems respond to climate change. The meadow phenology series, the long pollinator censuses, and the snowmelt-driven plant work continue without lapse — they are the source material. A larger in-house data team grows around them, and the campaign funds the common-garden plots, the digitized archives, and the partnerships with quantitative geneticists needed to push from description into mechanism. About fifty-five percent of the work is continuity; the rest builds the data tools and experimental infrastructure that turn the records into mechanistic answers. The scenario forgoes atmospheric instrumentation, broad community partnership programs, and deep financial reserves. It asks donors and the institution to bet on intellectual depth rather than scope.

### Campaign deliverables

- **primary.** Protect and extend the long phenology and pollinator records — the meadow flowering plots, the pollinator censuses across decades, the snowmelt-driven plant work — through plot infrastructure, archival systems, protocol stewardship, and the technical staff that supports the guest scientists carrying the records forward. These records are the source material for the scenario's central question and cannot lapse.
- **primary.** Build a substantially larger in-house data team — data scientists, archivists, and a geospatial analyst added to the existing technical core — dedicated to digitizing a century of field notebooks, plot records, and pollinator data, and to developing AI tools that let researchers read the records mechanistically rather than only descriptively. This is the load-bearing innovation of the scenario.
- **secondary.** Fund permanent common-garden and reciprocal-transplant infrastructure in the meadows around Gothic, designed for multi-generation experiments that separate plastic responses from evolutionary change. Plots, irrigation and snowmelt-control hardware, and the technical staff to maintain them across decades. This is the experimental complement to the archival work and is distinctive to this scenario.
- **secondary.** Support sustained methodological partnerships with quantitative geneticists, evolutionary ecologists, and AI-methods researchers who bring the analytical tools the basin records have never been read with. RMBL hosts these collaborations rather than directing them, with workshop space and staging capacity for visiting teams.
- **supporting.** Renovate parts of RMBL365 to host the year-round working sessions, archival work, and visiting-team residencies that the mechanistic synthesis requires. Modest scope — laboratory and meeting space rather than full community-facing build-out.
- **continuity_residual.** Modest growth in the savings and broader funding base that protect RMBL's independence through funding shocks. Smaller than in sibling scenarios that anchor on independence, but enough to keep the long records intact if a single grant cycle goes badly.

### Frontier support strategies

- **F.cont.1.** The campaign protects the basin's longest continuous records as the source material for the scenario's central question. Plot infrastructure, archival systems, and observer-succession capacity are funded at the level needed to keep the meadow phenology series, the pollinator censuses, and the snowmelt-driven plant work intact across the horizon. Without this base the mechanistic work has nothing to read.
- **F.cont.4.** The long pollinator censuses are protected and deepened through census continuity infrastructure and network data systems. Within this scenario, the pollinator records are treated not only as a continuity asset but as one half of the plasticity-versus-evolution question — pollinator timing and behavior are read alongside plant phenology as a coupled system.
- **F.both.2.** This is the scenario's defining frontier. The campaign funds permanent common-garden and reciprocal-transplant infrastructure, supports methodological partnerships with quantitative geneticists, and builds the data tools needed to read decades of phenology records mechanistically. Continuity (the long records) and innovation (the experimental and analytical infrastructure) are deliberately coupled in service of resolving the mismatch question.
- **F.innov.3.** RMBL grows in-house data-science and geospatial capacity beyond its current technical core, digitizes a century of field notebooks and plot records, and develops AI tools that let researchers extract mechanistic findings from accumulated basin records at scales prior generations could not attempt. The data team is load-bearing — the synthesis depends on tools RMBL builds and operates.

### Forgone

This scenario does not fund the atmospheric and watershed instrumentation that SAIL brought to the East River; if that work continues at RMBL it does so through other funding channels. It does not build a broad community co-production program with tribal nations, water districts, and county schools — community engagement happens, but it is not a campaign-defining feature. It does not fund a standing translation team that brings basin science to Mountain West water managers and Forest Service planners. It does not build deep financial reserves; the institution accepts more exposure to funding shocks than sibling scenarios that anchor on independence. It does not pursue cross-mountain network coordination among peer field stations. Each of these is a real choice, and a reader can fairly ask whether the scenario's intellectual bet justifies setting them aside.

### Seeds in the present

The scenario rests on work already in the ground. The meadow phenology series begun in the 1970s is now approaching its sixth decade. The long pollinator censuses, including the CaraDonna 2017 rewiring tradition, have built network records few other systems can match. The 2012 Anderson paper on plasticity and adaptive evolution in flowering phenology, and the 2022 Inouye synthesis placing basin records on the global stage, are the intellectual frame the campaign extends. The 2023 Prather multi-taxon phenology paper and the 2021 Campbell work on floral-trait selection show how mechanistic the questions have become. RMBL's existing technical core — a geospatial data scientist, a GIS manager, instrumentation technicians — and the Knowledge Commons itself are products of the recent pattern where outside grants build RMBL's own tools and capacity. The scenario grows that pattern in one direction: depth on a defining question.

## Setting

This scenario takes as its starting point a specific bet: that the basin holds the records, and is acquiring the tools, to answer a question that has shaped phenology research for two decades. The question is whether the advances in flowering, leafing, and pollinator activity observed across decades reflect plastic responses within individual lifetimes or evolutionary change across generations. The 2012 Anderson paper showed both were operating; what no one has yet been able to do is read the long records mechanistically enough to say how much of each, in which systems, on what timescales.

The campaign comes in near the upper end of what RMBL might raise, and the institution chooses depth over breadth. Federal contraction is the operating environment, not a future risk. Some basin researchers have already lost grants. The campaign exists partly because that environment makes a focused intellectual bet more important, not less — RMBL's independence as a nonprofit means it can commit to a multi-decade question that federal program cycles would struggle to sustain.

The shape of the work is concrete. The long phenology plots in the meadows around Gothic continue without lapse. A century of field notebooks and pollinator records gets digitized and made machine-readable. The data team grows. New common-garden plots go in, designed for multi-generation experiments that separate plasticity from evolution. Quantitative geneticists and AI-methods researchers visit as collaborators, hosted at RMBL365 in town. The atmospheric instruments SAIL brought to the East River are not the campaign's concern. Nor is broad community partnership work. The scenario chooses one thing and tries to do it well.

## The arc — three phases across the primary horizon

### Phase 1: Building the depth infrastructure (2026–2030)

The first phase establishes the conditions for the scenario's bet. The campaign launches with a clear public message: the basin will spend the next fifteen years reading its longest records mechanistically. RMBL hires two additional data scientists and an archivist, expanding the technical core. Digitization of field notebooks begins in earnest — the meadow phenology records first, then the pollinator censuses, working backward from the present. Plot infrastructure for the long records gets a quiet upgrade: new markers, weather-resilient data loggers, redundant observation protocols. The first new common-garden plots go in during 2028, sited to capture the snowmelt-timing gradient that the basin's natural meadows already span. Methodological partnerships with three quantitative-genetics groups and two AI-methods labs are formalized through visiting-researcher residencies hosted at RMBL365. The pattern of work becomes legible by 2029: long records read in new ways, new experiments designed to test what the records suggest. The first publications emerge late in the phase, showing that AI-assisted analysis can extract phenological signals from notebook records that prior methods missed.

### Phase 2: First mechanistic results (2031–2035)

The middle phase is where the bet begins to pay off — or to show its limits. The digitized phenology archive reaches roughly seventy years of plot-level records by 2032, paired with pollinator network data of comparable depth. The common-garden plots have run for several growing seasons; the first papers separating plastic and evolutionary components of flowering-time change appear around 2033, focused on three or four well-studied species. Reciprocal-transplant work begins in 2034, designed to push the mechanistic question further. The data team becomes a recognized hub for AI-assisted phenology synthesis, hosting visiting researchers from beyond the basin who want to apply the tools to their own systems. RMBL365 hosts standing working groups twice a year. The marmot study and the snowmelt-driven plant work continue without lapse, and the marmot record reaches its hundredth year toward the end of the phase. Federal funding remains contracted; the campaign's role in keeping the records intact through this period becomes visible. The first major synthesis paper — reading a century of basin records through the plasticity-evolution lens — appears in 2035.

### Phase 3: The question meaningfully answered (2036–2040)

The final phase consolidates what the bet has produced. By 2037 the mechanistic results have accumulated across enough species and enough years to support a sustained answer to the plasticity-versus-evolution question for the systems the basin studies. The answer is not simple — plasticity dominates in some species, evolution in others, and the partition depends on snowmelt regime and pollinator network position — but it is mechanistic, defensible, and grounded in records no other system can match. The work reshapes how phenology under climate change is taught and understood. Publications from the basin appear in venues that previously cited basin work only as natural history; they now cite it as the empirical anchor for theory. Toward 2040 the institution faces a question about the next horizon: whether to extend the mechanistic work into less-studied taxa, whether to apply the methods to other long records, whether to broaden the portfolio. The campaign closes near the upper end of the realistic bracket. The marmot record passes its centennial in the early 2040s, just outside the campaign horizon.

## Lines of inquiry

The defining line of inquiry is phenology read mechanistically. The long meadow records, the pollinator censuses, and the snowmelt-driven plant work are extended without lapse, and read through tools the records' originators could not have imagined. The plasticity-versus-evolution question — which the 2012 Anderson paper opened and the field has not closed — is pursued with common-garden plots, reciprocal transplants, and AI-assisted analysis of accumulated archives. Pollinator timing and behavior are studied alongside plant phenology as a coupled system, so the question is not only about plants. AI-assisted retrospective synthesis matures as a methodological program in its own right, anchored in the basin's data team but exported to other long-record systems through collaborations. The yellow-bellied marmot study continues, supported by the same archival and data infrastructure that serves the phenology work, and its centennial approaches near the end of the period. What does not mature in this scenario: the atmospheric and subsurface integration that SAIL began, the watershed-scale biogeochemistry that the East River testbed has anchored, broad community co-production work, translation to water and land management at scale, and cross-mountain comparative programs. These remain present at the basin through guest-scientist work and other funding, but they are not what the campaign builds.

## Moments of choice within the primary horizon

1. **2027 — Whether to frame the campaign around a single intellectual question or around a broader portfolio of basin priorities** *Shared inflection: `campaign-framing-decision`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and board, in consultation with the Development team
   - Alternatives: A broader frame would spread the campaign across continuity, watershed instrumentation, community engagement, and independence — appealing to a wider donor base but diluting focus. A narrower frame anchors on a single defining question and accepts that some donors will look elsewhere.
   - Scenario assumption: The board chooses the narrower frame, accepting that depth on a defining question is the campaign's identity and that some donor segments will be less engaged as a result.

2. **2028 — How the campaign balances investments that draw returns over time against capital build-out** *Shared inflection: `endowment-capital-split-2028`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership, board, and Development team
   - Alternatives: Heavier endowment weighting protects the long records and data team across decades but slows visible build-out. Heavier capital weighting accelerates plots, archives, and renovations but leaves operating exposure higher.
   - Scenario assumption: The institution accepts moderate endowment weighting, enough to sustain the data team and plot infrastructure beyond the horizon, while funding the common gardens, digitization, and RMBL365 renovations as capital.

3. **2029 — Whether to build AI tools as basin-specific instruments or as exportable methods** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the growing data team
   - Alternatives: Basin-specific tools would be tuned to the meadow phenology records and pollinator censuses, deeper but narrower. Exportable methods would generalize to other long-record systems, attracting broader collaborations but slowing immediate mechanistic results.
   - Scenario assumption: The team builds basin-first but designs for export, so the tools mature on the records they were built for and then spread to other systems through collaborations.

4. **2031 — Whether to design the common-garden program as a closed RMBL-run platform or as a hosted facility open to multiple guest-scientist groups** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the principal investigators leading the common-garden work
   - Alternatives: A closed platform would let RMBL direct the experimental design more tightly and produce a cleaner mechanistic answer. A hosted facility would attract more guest scientists, more questions, and a broader collaborative footprint, but with less coherence.
   - Scenario assumption: RMBL hosts the facility on its responsive-infrastructure model, supporting a small set of long-running guest-scientist groups with shared protocols rather than directing the science itself.

5. **2033 — How to respond when SAIL-derived atmospheric work loses its federal anchor and partner researchers ask whether RMBL will pick it up** *Shared inflection: `sail-transition-2027`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership, board, and the technical core
   - Alternatives: Taking on the atmospheric instrumentation would broaden the campaign's footprint and answer a pressing partner need. Declining keeps the campaign focused on the phenology bet but accepts that the East River as an atmosphere-to-bedrock system will not be sustained through this campaign.
   - Scenario assumption: The institution declines, with public acknowledgment of the cost. The campaign's identity is intellectual depth on the phenology question; the atmospheric work is for another campaign or another institution.

6. **2036 — Whether to extend the mechanistic work into the marmot record as its centennial approaches** *Shared inflection: `stewardship-priority-2032`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the data team
   - Alternatives: Extending would bring the AI-assisted methods to the marmot demographic record and pair behavioral, demographic, and molecular data in ways the marmot work has been pointing toward. Not extending preserves campaign focus on phenology.
   - Scenario assumption: The institution makes a partial commitment — the data team supports the marmot work through shared tools and infrastructure, but the campaign does not anchor on extending the mechanistic frame to marmots. That work waits for a later campaign or different funding.

7. **2039 — What the next horizon looks like after the phenology question is meaningfully answered** *Shared inflection: `next-horizon-2040`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership, board, and the broader guest-scientist community
   - Alternatives: Apply the methods to other long records — marmots, the watershed, broader mountain systems. Hold focus on phenology and deepen into less-studied taxa. Pivot to a different defining question entirely.
   - Scenario assumption: Planning for the next horizon begins late in the campaign and is deliberately left open, so the institution does not foreclose options before the current bet has fully paid out.

## Audience lens — research

For phenology researchers, evolutionary ecologists, and quantitative geneticists working on climate response, this period is unusual. The basin's long records get digitized and made machine-readable on a timeline that lets a researcher reach back through a century of phenology and pollinator data in a single afternoon. New common-garden and reciprocal-transplant plots are available for multi-generation experiments, with the technical support and snowmelt-control infrastructure that such work requires. AI-assisted analysis tools, built around the basin records but designed for export, become available for use on other long-record systems. RMBL365 hosts working sessions where quantitative geneticists, phenology modelers, and field ecologists meet across disciplines that rarely share a room. For researchers whose work depends on atmospheric or watershed instrumentation, this scenario offers less — the basin remains a place where that work happens through guest-scientist partnerships, but the campaign does not anchor it.

## Audience lens — institution

RMBL through this period is a place with a sharper intellectual identity than it has carried in a generation. The institution is known publicly for one thing: it is the place where the plasticity-versus-evolution question for mountain ecosystems gets answered. Staff numbers grow modestly — three or four additional technical positions in the data team, an archivist, support for the common-garden infrastructure — but the institution does not become large. The guest-scientist community remains the engine of the research. The trade-off is real: RMBL does not become the community-anchored translator that other scenarios imagine, nor the watershed observatory, nor the broadly networked field station. It becomes a place that bet on intellectual depth and is recognized for it. Working at RMBL through this period feels focused. The conversations in the dining hall are about the same question, returned to from different angles, year after year.

## Audience lens — donor

Your contribution is part of a focused intellectual commitment: the basin will spend fifteen years reading a century of records to answer a question that has shaped phenology research for two decades. You join the work of protecting the long meadow phenology records, the pollinator censuses, and the snowmelt-driven plant work that are the question's source material. You enable the conditions for a growing data team to digitize and read those records mechanistically, and for new common-garden plots to test what the records suggest. This is not the broadest version of the campaign, and we are open about what it forgoes. It is the version that asks what RMBL might be remembered for in a generation, and answers: as the place where one of the field's defining questions was meaningfully answered.

## Overlay robustness

**Central case.** Under gradual federal contraction, gradual AI evolution, and roughly RCP6.0 climate, the scenario plays out close to its design. Federal grants for individual basin researchers thin further but do not collapse. AI tools mature steadily, neither leaping forward nor stalling. Snowmelt continues advancing, frost exposure continues to vary, pollinator networks continue rewiring. The long records remain interpretable as a coherent series. The mechanistic question gets meaningful answers across several species by the end of the horizon, with the partition between plasticity and evolution shown to depend on snowmelt regime and network position.

**Stress case — Sharper federal contraction.** If federal grants to basin researchers contract more sharply than expected, the guest-scientist community thins faster than the campaign anticipated. The long records depend on those guest scientists, and the campaign's continuity investments have to absorb more observer-succession work than planned. The common-garden experiments slow because fewer outside groups can host postdocs to run them. The mechanistic question is still pursued, but the timeline stretches and the synthesis paper that this scenario expects in 2035 arrives closer to 2038. RMBL's choice to forgo deep financial reserves becomes more visible as a cost.

**Stress case — AI tools advance faster than expected.** If AI methods leap forward in the late 2020s — large foundation models that can read field notebooks at scale with little tuning — the digitization and synthesis work compresses dramatically. The first mechanistic results arrive earlier, perhaps by 2031. The data team becomes a methodological hub more quickly than planned, and the basin tools spread to other long-record systems by mid-horizon. The risk shifts: the institution must decide whether to expand into a methodological-export role, which is not what the campaign was built for, or to keep focus on the basin question and let others run with the tools.

**Stress case — A climate threshold crosses earlier.** If snowpack thins past a threshold that disrupts the meadow systems being studied — earlier and more variable than projections suggest — some of the long phenology series become discontinuous with their own past, and the comparability that makes the question answerable is partly lost. The scenario's bet absorbs this badly. The mechanistic work pivots toward documenting the threshold itself, which is valuable, but is not the question the campaign organized around. RMBL would face a hard choice about whether to reframe the campaign mid-stream.

## Plausibility caveats

This scenario assumes several things that a careful reader should weigh. It assumes the long phenology and pollinator records remain interpretable as coherent series across the horizon — that snowmelt regimes do not cross thresholds that disrupt the meadow systems past the point of comparability with their own past. It assumes AI tools mature enough to extract mechanistic signals from a century of field notebooks without introducing biases that quietly distort the conclusions; the methods literature on this is young and the standards are still being set. It assumes the guest-scientist community for phenology and quantitative-genetics work remains active enough through federal contraction to staff the common-garden experiments the campaign builds. It assumes donor interest in a focused intellectual question rather than a broader portfolio — a real bet about the donor base that the Development team will test in practice.

The characteristic way this scenario could go wrong is specific to its bet: the question itself could turn out to be less tractable than the field has assumed. If the plasticity-evolution partition depends on factors the basin records cannot resolve — gene-environment interactions, multi-generational epigenetics, pollinator-mediated selection on traits the censuses did not measure — the mechanistic answers may remain partial, and the campaign closes having advanced the question without resolving it. That would not be a failure of execution, but a finding about the question. A structural blind spot: the scenario invests heavily in two species groups (subalpine plants and their pollinators) and accepts that aquatic, microbial, and vertebrate phenologies in the basin get less attention. The scenario's portrait of mountain phenology is partial by design.

## Looking further out: 2040–2050

*Speculative. Lower resolution than the primary horizon.*

Past 2040 the picture is necessarily hazier. If the bet has paid out, the basin enters the 2040s known as the place where one of phenology's defining questions was meaningfully answered. The marmot record passes its centennial in the early 2040s and a successor campaign — distinct from this one — may organize around extending the mechanistic frame to mammals. The AI tools the data team built spread to other long-record systems, and basin methods become standard in mountain phenology research worldwide. If the bet has paid out only partially, the institution faces a choice in the mid-2040s about whether to continue or to pivot, and the partial answers themselves become a contribution. By 2050 the meadow systems the basin has watched for eighty years may have changed past recognition; what endures is the mechanistic understanding of how they responded — read out of records no other system can match, by tools built for the purpose.
