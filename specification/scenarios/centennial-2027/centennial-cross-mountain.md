# Centennial Cross-Mountain

*A Future Scenario in the `centennial-2027` set. Target magnitude, balanced. v1.0 under [Future Scenarios Framework v0.7](../../Future_scenarios_framework.md).*

---

## Synopsis

Centennial Cross-Mountain assumes the campaign comes in near the upper end of what RMBL might raise, and that the institution makes a deliberate bet on becoming a networked anchor among mountain field stations across western North America and beyond. The basin's longest records — the marmot study reaching its hundredth year, the meadow phenology series, and the snowmelt-driven plant work — continue without lapse, and serve as the basin's distinctive contribution to a wider comparative science. A larger in-house data team builds shared tools and protocols that peer stations can use, and reads a century of basin records at scales prior generations could not attempt. The campaign also builds the savings and broader funding base that protect RMBL's independence. The scenario forgoes broad community co-production work, large mechanistic experiments outside the basin, and a major build-out of the East River as a permanent watershed observatory. It asks whether a place-based field station can strengthen rather than dilute its identity by anchoring a network.

### Campaign deliverables

- **primary.** Substantial growth of RMBL's in-house data and geospatial team, with explicit responsibility for building shared data infrastructure, common protocols, and analytical tools that peer mountain field stations can adopt. This is the load-bearing investment of the scenario: the team is sized to serve guest scientists in the basin and to maintain shared systems used by partner stations elsewhere.
- **primary.** Partnership coordination capacity — modest dedicated staff and travel support — that sustains formal collaboration agreements with peer mountain field stations in western North America and at least two international sites. The work is convening, protocol harmonization, and joint hosting of guest scientists who move between basins.
- **secondary.** Protocol stewardship and archival systems for the basin's longest continuous records — the marmot study, the meadow phenology series, the snowmelt-driven plant work — at the level needed to keep them intact and to make them comparable with parallel records at peer stations.
- **secondary.** Digitization of a century of field notebooks, plot records, and observational archives, structured from the start to be readable by collaborators at other mountain stations rather than only by basin researchers.
- **supporting.** Savings and broader funding base that protect RMBL's independence through funding shocks, with particular attention to the operating costs of the shared infrastructure RMBL will be maintaining on behalf of a network.
- **supporting.** Modest renovation of RMBL365 to support year-round hosting of visiting scientists from peer stations, with space configured for cross-station working meetings rather than primarily for local community programming.

### Frontier support strategies

- **F.cont.1.** The basin's longest records are protected as the basin's distinctive contribution to a comparative science. Protocol stewardship, plot infrastructure, and archival systems are funded at the level needed to keep records intact, with the added requirement that protocols be documented in forms peer stations can adopt or compare against.
- **F.innov.3.** RMBL's in-house data team grows substantially. Digitized archives, machine-readable records, and analytical tools are built so that a century of basin observations can be read at scales prior generations could not attempt — and so that the same tools work on parallel records at peer mountain stations.
- **F.both.3.** This is the scenario's organizing frontier. Partnership coordination, shared data infrastructure, and visiting-scientist hosting are funded as durable capacities rather than one-off projects. The basin contributes its long records; the network contributes comparative range.
- **F.innov.5.** Savings and a broader funding base protect RMBL's independence — important here because anchoring a network means other institutions depend on RMBL being there. The campaign also makes RMBL attractive to guest scientists seeking unrestricted venues, by guaranteeing that shared infrastructure will not be subject to outside research-direction restrictions.

### Forgone

This scenario does not fund a permanent atmosphere-to-bedrock watershed observatory in the East River after SAIL winds down. It does not build broad community co-production programs with the Ute Indian Tribe, Gunnison Valley water districts, and county schools at the scale that a community-anchored scenario would. It does not stand up large mechanistic experimental platforms — common gardens, factorial warming arrays, basin-scale paired-treatment networks. RMBL365 is renovated for visiting-scientist hosting rather than as a community-facing hub. The campaign does not build deep financial reserves beyond what is needed to protect the shared infrastructure RMBL will be maintaining for a network. Some basin-specific frontiers — phenology mechanism, plant-pollinator network resilience, post-snowpack ecology — continue through guest-scientist work but receive no dedicated campaign investment. The bet is that distinctive value comes from comparative reach, not from doing everything well in one valley.

### Seeds in the present

The Knowledge Commons itself is the most visible product of the recent pattern in which outside grants build RMBL's own tools and capacity, which then attract more grants. The basin's geospatial and GIS staff are already maintaining data systems that other mountain stations could use. The SAIL campaign and the East River watershed work have given RMBL a decade of experience hosting large multi-institution collaborations on basin grounds. The phenology synthesis work and the marmot project's molecular turn have made the basin's long records newly legible to scientists at other sites. Planning themes around scale and integration friction, coupled mountain observatories, and long-term integrated time series all point toward the same diagnosis: many independent research lines are bottlenecked on shared infrastructure that no single site has incentive to build alone. A networked anchor role is the response.

## Setting

This scenario assumes the campaign comes in near the upper end of what RMBL might raise, and that the institution chooses to spend much of that range on becoming a networked anchor rather than a self-contained field station. The bet is specific: the basin's distinctive contribution to mountain science in the coming decades comes less from what happens in this valley alone than from what happens when basin records, protocols, and tools are shared with peer stations across western North America and beyond.

The operating environment is the contracted federal funding base that has shaped the past several years, with continued pressure on individual researchers to diversify their support. Some federal-adjacent institutions face restrictions on inquiry that RMBL, as an independent nonprofit, does not. Both conditions make a networked role more valuable: peer stations need a partner that can build shared infrastructure without strings, and researchers need places where comparative work can happen freely.

The campaign protects the basin's longest records — the marmot study reaching its hundredth year, the meadow phenology series, the snowmelt-driven plant work — as the basin's contribution to that comparative science. It grows RMBL's data and geospatial team substantially, with explicit responsibility for tools and protocols that travel beyond the basin. It funds the coordination work that sustains formal collaboration agreements with peer mountain stations, and the savings that protect RMBL's independence as other institutions come to depend on the shared infrastructure RMBL maintains. The scenario is asking whether place identity and network identity can reinforce each other rather than compete.

## The arc — three phases across the primary horizon

### Phase 1: Anchoring the network (2026–2030)

The campaign closes near the upper end of what RMBL might raise. The first hires expand the data and geospatial team, with two new positions explicitly responsible for shared infrastructure that peer stations will use. By 2028, RMBL signs formal collaboration agreements with three peer mountain field stations in western North America, with a shared data platform as the first concrete deliverable. The basin's long records continue without lapse; protocol stewardship is reorganized so that documentation is legible to outside collaborators from the start. Digitization of a century of field notebooks begins in earnest in 2027. RMBL365 is renovated by 2029, with space configured for cross-station working meetings and visiting-scientist hosting rather than primarily local community programming. The first cross-station visiting researcher cohort arrives in summer 2029. SAIL has wound down by this phase; the basin does not attempt to make its instruments permanent, which is the scenario's defining tradeoff and is visible in this phase as the choice not taken.

### Phase 2: Shared infrastructure in use (2031–2035)

The shared data platform is in routine use by 2032 across the founding partner stations, with formal collaboration agreements adding a fourth North American partner and the first international partner — most likely a long-running European alpine station whose records run nearly as long as RMBL's. Guest scientists begin to publish comparative work that would not have been possible without harmonized protocols. The marmot study reaches its hundredth year in this phase, marked as a centennial milestone for basin science rather than a basin-only event; peer stations contribute their own long-record reflections. The in-house data team makes a century of digitized basin records available in machine-readable form by 2034, with parallel work underway to ingest comparable records from partner stations. The campaign's savings cover the operating costs of the shared infrastructure when one partner station loses its core federal grant in 2033 — the first concrete test of whether RMBL can carry the network through funding shocks elsewhere.

### Phase 3: Network identity (2036–2040)

By the late 2030s the network has its own identity — joint working groups, shared graduate training, cross-station synthesis papers — and RMBL is recognized within it as the institutional anchor. The basin's long records are routinely read in comparative context with parallel records from a half-dozen peer sites. Guest scientists move between stations using shared protocols and data tools that the basin team built and maintains. The in-house data team is roughly twice its 2026 size, with a clear split between basin-facing and network-facing responsibilities. The savings and broader funding base have weathered at least one further federal shock. RMBL365 is established as a year-round venue for cross-station working meetings, and several international partner stations send researchers there each winter. By 2040 the question the scenario set out to test — whether place identity and network identity can reinforce each other — has a working answer in the form of an active network whose anchor is a small Colorado field station with a century of records and the tools to share them.

## Lines of inquiry

The phenology work matures through comparative analysis. Basin records of bloom timing, frost exposure, and pollinator activity are read alongside parallel records from peer sites, allowing questions about mismatch resolution to be tested across mountain systems rather than within one. The marmot record's transition into molecular and social-structure work continues, and is increasingly compared with long mammal records at other stations. Snow ecology becomes a shared frontier across the network, with the basin contributing its long snowmelt-phenology series. AI-assisted retrospective synthesis matures in this scenario because the source material is broader: a century of basin records plus parallel records from partner stations, all in shared formats. Cross-mountain comparative ecology — the basin's contribution to questions about which mountain dynamics are general and which are local — becomes a defining theme of basin science by the late 2030s. What does not mature in this scenario: a permanent atmosphere-to-bedrock watershed observatory in the East River; mechanistic plant-microbe-soil experiments at scale; broad community co-production with valley stakeholders; new instrumentation development beyond what shared protocols require.

## Moments of choice within the primary horizon

1. **2027 — Whether to anchor the campaign around a networked role or around place-based depth in the basin.** *Shared inflection: `campaign-framing-decision`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and board
   - Alternatives: RMBL could spend the same range on a permanent East River watershed observatory, on deep community partnerships in the valley, on broad financial reserves with modest growth, or on a balanced spread across all of these.
   - Scenario assumption: Leadership chooses the networked anchor role, accepting that some basin-specific frontiers will be served only through guest-scientist work rather than dedicated campaign investment.

2. **2028 — How to split the campaign's resources between endowed capacity and one-time capital.** *Shared inflection: `endowment-capital-split-2028`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership, board, and Development team
   - Alternatives: Heavier endowment protects the network role across decades but slows the build-out of shared tools. Heavier capital builds the platform faster but leaves operating costs more exposed to future fundraising.
   - Scenario assumption: The split leans toward endowment for the data team and partnership coordination, with capital concentrated on digitization and the RMBL365 renovation.

3. **2029 — Whether the shared data platform is built as RMBL-hosted infrastructure that partners use, or as a federated system where each station hosts its own with shared standards.** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the data team lead
   - Alternatives: RMBL-hosted is faster to build and easier to control but concentrates dependency on a small Colorado nonprofit. Federated is slower and harder to govern but distributes risk and respects partner autonomy.
   - Scenario assumption: The platform is built as federated infrastructure with shared standards, with RMBL hosting the reference implementation. This is slower but more durable.

4. **2031 — Whether to admit a first international partner station to the network, and on what terms.** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and partner-station directors
   - Alternatives: Stay North American to keep coordination tractable, or expand to international partners and accept the additional complexity in governance, data sovereignty, and travel.
   - Scenario assumption: The network admits its first international partner in 2032, accepting the complexity in exchange for genuinely comparative range.

5. **2032 — Which long records receive the strongest stewardship investment as the marmot study approaches its hundredth year.** *Shared inflection: `stewardship-priority-2032`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the board
   - Alternatives: Concentrate stewardship on the marmot record alone; spread it across all three of the basin's longest records; or treat stewardship as a comparative obligation that includes documentation legible to peer-station scientists.
   - Scenario assumption: Stewardship is treated as a comparative obligation, with protocol documentation, archival access, and observer-succession capacity all reorganized to support cross-station use.

6. **2033 — How to respond when a partner station loses its core federal grant and asks the network to carry shared infrastructure costs through the gap.** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and board
   - Alternatives: Refuse and let the partner station drop temporarily; underwrite the shared costs from RMBL's savings; or restructure the network's funding model to share infrastructure costs more formally across partners.
   - Scenario assumption: RMBL underwrites the shared costs from its savings, and the experience prompts a more formal cost-sharing model by 2035. This is the first concrete test of the scenario's bet on independence as a strategic asset.

7. **2039 — What the next horizon for the network looks like as the campaign period closes.** *Shared inflection: `next-horizon-2040`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership, board, and partner-station directors
   - Alternatives: Consolidate at current scale; expand to a second international region; deepen integration with national-laboratory partners; or hand significant coordination responsibility to a partner station.
   - Scenario assumption: The network consolidates at current scale and begins planning a second campaign horizon focused on integration depth rather than further expansion.

## Audience lens — research

Scientists working on basin frontiers during this period get something they have not had before: routine comparative range. A researcher studying snowmelt phenology in the basin can pull parallel records from peer stations using the same data tools, compare patterns across mountain systems, and ask which dynamics are general and which are local. A researcher whose home institution faces restrictions on inquiry can come to the basin and find an unrestricted venue with shared infrastructure that connects their work to a wider network. Visiting cohorts move between stations using common protocols. The basin's long records are no longer read only as basin records; they are read as one mountain system's contribution to a larger comparative science. What is less available in this scenario is dedicated support for new basin-specific instrumentation outside the shared-protocol effort.

## Audience lens — institution

RMBL becomes a small institution with an outsized role in a wider field. Working at RMBL in the 2030s means working at a place that other mountain field stations depend on for shared infrastructure, that hosts cross-station working meetings year-round in town, and that is recognized in mountain science as the network's anchor. The technical core roughly doubles, with a clear split between basin-facing and network-facing work. Staff find themselves in regular contact with peers at other stations. The basin's identity as a place-based field station is not weakened but reframed: the basin is where the longest records live, the meetings happen, the tools are built. The institution carries new responsibility — other stations depending on RMBL means that funding shocks at RMBL ripple outward — and the campaign's savings exist partly to absorb that.

## Audience lens — donor

A contribution to this campaign joins an effort to give a small Colorado field station a role it could not have on its own: anchoring a network of mountain field stations across western North America and beyond. Your contribution is part of protecting the basin's longest records as one mountain system's contribution to a wider comparative science, of building the shared data tools and protocols that let researchers move between basins, and of sustaining the savings that let RMBL carry shared infrastructure through funding shocks. It is part of building the conditions under which a small independent nonprofit can be the connective tissue that a wider science needs. The work is quieter than visible expansion. What it asks of you is the belief that place identity and network identity can reinforce each other — that a hundred years of basin records become more valuable, not less, when they are read alongside parallel records elsewhere.

## Overlay robustness

**Central case.** Under gradual federal contraction, gradual AI evolution, and an RCP6.0 climate trajectory, the scenario plays out roughly as described. Federal contraction makes shared infrastructure more valuable to peer stations that cannot build it alone. Gradual AI evolution lets the in-house data team build useful tools without requiring exotic capabilities. RCP6.0 climate produces the kinds of comparative questions — which mountain dynamics are general, which are local — that the network is built to answer. The basin's long records remain distinctive contributions; the network's value compounds across the period.

**Stress case — Sharp federal contraction.** If federal support contracts faster and deeper than the central case, peer stations face existential pressure earlier. RMBL's savings absorb more cost-sharing than planned, and the partner-station rescue in 2033 becomes the first of several. The network consolidates around fewer, more deeply integrated stations rather than expanding to international partners on schedule. The scenario's independence frontier becomes more central. The basin's long records remain protected, but the data team's ambitions narrow.

**Stress case — Rapid AI evolution.** If AI capabilities advance faster than the central case, the value of shared digitized records compounds quickly. Cross-station synthesis that would have taken years becomes tractable in months. The data team's role shifts toward curation and quality control rather than tool-building. The risk is that the network becomes valuable enough that larger institutions try to absorb its coordination function, and RMBL must defend its anchor role on grounds other than tool sophistication.

**Stress case — Network coordination fails.** If formal collaboration agreements prove harder to sustain than expected — through governance disputes, data-sovereignty conflicts with international partners, or partner-station leadership turnover — the network thins. RMBL ends the period with strong basin records, a substantial data team, and shared infrastructure that fewer partners use than planned. The bet does not pay off in full. The basin's long records remain valuable in their own right, but the comparative reach the scenario was built to enable is partial.

## Plausibility caveats

This scenario assumes several things that could fail. It assumes peer mountain field stations will accept RMBL — a small Colorado nonprofit — as a network anchor rather than coordinating among themselves or under a larger institution. There is no guarantee of this. Several peer stations are embedded in universities or national laboratories with more institutional weight than RMBL has, and could plausibly view a federated network as competition rather than infrastructure. It assumes that shared data standards and protocols can be agreed across stations whose research traditions developed independently for decades. Protocol harmonization is genuinely hard and often fails. It assumes that international partner stations can join under data-sovereignty arrangements compatible with U.S. law, which is increasingly fraught. It assumes that RMBL's independence — important here because partners are trusting RMBL with shared infrastructure — remains protected through the period.

The characteristic failure mode of this scenario is specific: the network coordination work absorbs more institutional energy than expected, and the basin's place-based science thins as staff and leadership attention flow outward. A field station that becomes mostly a coordinator stops being a field station. The scenario's bet is that place and network identity reinforce each other, but the genuine risk is that they compete and the place loses. A structural blind spot worth naming: this scenario assumes that comparative mountain science is the question the next two decades will most need answered. If the basin's distinctive value turns out to lie in depth rather than breadth — in coupled atmosphere-to-bedrock observation of one watershed, or in mechanistic ecology of one community — the scenario will look like a wrong bet in hindsight even if it executes well.

## Looking further out: 2040–2050

*Speculative. Lower resolution than the primary horizon.*

By 2050, if the scenario's bet has paid off, the basin's century-and-a-quarter of records are read routinely alongside parallel records from a network of mountain stations spanning western North America and at least two other continents. RMBL is recognized as the network's anchor — a small Colorado nonprofit whose distinctive contribution to mountain science is connective tissue as much as observation. The marmot record, the meadow phenology series, and the snowmelt-driven plant work are still continuous, and are now part of a comparative library that has changed what mountain science can ask. Researchers move between basins using tools the basin team built. Place identity and network identity reinforce each other; the basin is where the longest records live and where the meetings happen. If the bet has failed in the ways the caveats name, the basin still has its records and its data team, but the wider network is thinner than hoped, and the next campaign horizon asks a different question.
