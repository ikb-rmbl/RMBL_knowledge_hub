# Centennial Watershed

*A Future Scenario in the `centennial-2027` set. Target magnitude, balanced. v1.0 under [Future Scenarios Framework v0.7](../../Future_scenarios_framework.md).*

---

## Synopsis

Centennial Watershed assumes the campaign comes in near the upper end of what RMBL might raise, and that the institution makes one decisive bet: the East River becomes a permanent atmosphere-to-bedrock observatory rather than a project that ends when federal campaigns end. The instruments, towers, and sampling that SAIL brought to the basin between 2021 and 2025 stay running, owned and operated by RMBL. A larger technical staff keeps them coupled — atmosphere, snow, soil, water, microbes, biology — as one integrated record. The basin's long biological records continue without lapse, because they give the watershed measurements their depth. The campaign also builds the savings and broader funding base that protect this work from political weather. The scenario forgoes broad community partnership programs, large mechanistic experiments outside the watershed, and a major build-out of community-facing space in town. It asks donors to fund something specific: the chance to make permanent what may otherwise be remembered as a campaign that came and went.

### Campaign deliverables

- **primary.** Make the East River observatory permanent. The atmospheric radars, snow and soil moisture stations, flux towers, and stream chemistry sampling that the federal SAIL campaign brought to the basin transition to RMBL ownership and operation. This is the largest commitment in the scenario, covering instrument purchase or transfer, site infrastructure, calibration capacity, and long-term operating support. It is the deliverable that no other scenario in the set makes at this scale.
- **primary.** Grow RMBL's technical staff to run the observatory. The current four-person technical core expands meaningfully, adding instrument technicians, a watershed data engineer, and additional geospatial capacity. This is the team that keeps atmosphere, snow, soil, water, microbes, and biology readable as one coupled system rather than separate streams. Distinctive to this scenario in scale.
- **secondary.** Protect the basin's longest biological records. The marmot study, the meadow phenology series, and the snowmelt-driven plant work continue without lapse through their next decades. Plot infrastructure, protocol stewardship, and archival systems are maintained at the level needed to keep the records intact and to give the watershed measurements their biological depth.
- **secondary.** Build data systems that join the watershed streams to the long biological records. AI-assisted tools let researchers ask questions that span a century of biology and a decade-plus of coupled atmosphere-to-bedrock measurement on the same plots. Modest growth of RMBL's data science capacity supports this.
- **supporting.** Build savings and a broader funding base that protect the observatory from funding shocks. Permanent infrastructure needs operating support across political cycles. The campaign sets aside reserves, diversifies foundation and private support, and strengthens the staff capacity that wins and manages outside grants.

### Frontier support strategies

- **F.innov.1.** This is the scenario's anchor. The campaign transitions SAIL-era instruments to RMBL operation, builds permanent site infrastructure across the East River, and staffs the technical team that keeps the measurements coupled across atmosphere, snow, soil, water, microbes, and biology. The deliverables that build the observatory and grow technical staff are the frontier.
- **F.cont.1.** The basin's long biological records continue without lapse because they are what give the watershed measurements their depth. Protocol stewardship, plot infrastructure, and archival systems are maintained. The records are protected here as part of the watershed bet, not separately from it.
- **F.innov.3.** RMBL's data scientists build tools that join the new watershed streams to the digitized biological archive. The work is scoped to what serves the coupled-systems questions — letting a researcher ask how a particular flowering season relates to that year's snow microphysics, soil moisture, and stream chemistry. Smaller in scope than in sibling scenarios where AI synthesis is the anchor.
- **F.innov.5.** Permanent infrastructure needs durable independence. The campaign builds the savings, governance practices, and broader funding base that let RMBL operate the observatory across political cycles, including when federal support contracts further. Independence is sized to what protects the watershed bet, not pursued as a separate anchor.

### Forgone

This scenario does not fund broad community co-production programs. The Ute Indian Tribe partnerships, water-district co-design work, and county-school engagement that anchor other scenarios in the set are not built out here beyond what existing relationships sustain. RMBL365 is maintained as it is but does not receive a major renovation. The scenario also forgoes large mechanistic experimental platforms outside the East River — no new common-garden facilities, no factorial warming experiments at scale. Cross-mountain comparative partnerships with peer institutions are not formally built out. Financial reserves are sized to protect the observatory rather than to maximize institutional buffer, so the savings cushion is smaller than in scenarios that make independence the anchor. The bet is concentrated. If the watershed work does not become the basin's defining contribution to global mountain science, the scenario will have spent the campaign on something narrower than its breadth justified.

### Seeds in the present

The seeds are already in the ground. The 2021–2025 era at RMBL was defined by the SAIL campaign — radars, radiometers, and flux instruments installed across the East River, coupled with hillslope-clustering frameworks and bedrock-to-surface modeling that treated the watershed as a single system. The federal investment that built this — the Department of Energy's watershed program and SAIL itself — is exactly the kind of infrastructure that ends when federal campaigns end. The long biological records sit on top of this terrain: the marmot study above Gothic, the meadow phenology series, the snowmelt-driven plant work documented across more than four decades. RMBL's recent pattern of growing its own technical staff to run shared data and instruments — the team that built this Knowledge Commons among other tools — is the precedent for the larger technical team this scenario builds. The integration is already happening. The scenario asks whether it can be made permanent.

## Setting

This scenario starts from a specific worry. The SAIL campaign that reshaped basin science between 2021 and 2025 was, by design, a campaign. Federal earth-systems campaigns come, run for a defined period, and leave. The instruments are removed or transferred. The integration that took years to build dissolves back into separate disciplinary streams. The scenario assumes the campaign comes in near the upper end of what RMBL might raise, and that the institution uses the bulk of it to make the East River observatory permanent rather than letting it end.

The choice is sharper than it sounds. The basin's distinctive contribution to global science has changed shape over the last decade. It is no longer only the long biological records, important as those are. It is the pairing — flowers and snow microphysics, marmots and groundwater chemistry, pollinators and atmospheric flux — measured on the same landscape, on overlapping timescales, by teams that share instruments. No other place has this depth across these coupled systems. The campaign locks it in.

The operating environment makes the bet harder and the rationale stronger. Federal funding for this kind of long-horizon mountain science has already contracted from what it was a few years ago. The institutions that might otherwise run permanent atmosphere-to-bedrock observatories face restrictions on inquiry that an independent nonprofit does not. RMBL can do this. The question is whether donors will fund the operating reality of a permanent observatory — the technicians, the calibration trips, the unsexy work of keeping a stream gauge accurate for thirty years — rather than only the science it produces.

## The arc — three phases across the primary horizon

### Phase 1: Transition (2026–2030)

The first phase is about not losing what SAIL built. As the federal campaign winds down across 2026 and 2027, RMBL negotiates the transfer or replacement of the instruments that matter most for coupled-systems science — the radars and radiometers, the flux towers, the snow and soil moisture stations across the East River. Some are purchased outright. Some are replaced with RMBL-owned equivalents. A few are let go. By 2028 the campaign is far enough along that RMBL can hire the technical staff who will run the observatory: instrument technicians, a watershed data engineer, two additional geospatial scientists. The long biological records continue without disruption. The marmot study runs its annual census. The meadow phenology plots are read. The data team begins the slower work of joining the new watershed streams to the digitized biological archive. By 2030 the observatory is operating under RMBL stewardship for the first time, with the integration intact.

### Phase 2: Integration (2031–2035)

The middle phase is where the bet starts to pay or not. With the instruments running and the team in place, basin researchers begin asking questions that require all the streams together. A 2032 paper traces a single early-snowmelt year through atmospheric moisture, soil chemistry, microbial activity, flowering time, and pollinator response — the kind of analysis that was not possible when these measurements lived in different projects. By 2033 the East River is being cited as one of a small number of places worldwide where this depth of coupling can be measured at all. The data systems mature enough that a guest scientist can arrive in spring and have working access to a decade of coupled records by the end of their first field season. The long biological records reach important markers — the marmot study, now in its eighth decade, passes seventy-five years, the meadow phenology series passes fifty. Around 2034 the institution faces a real decision about whether to extend the observatory upslope into the alpine, or to deepen what it already runs.

### Phase 3: Permanence (2036–2040)

The final phase is about whether what was built actually persists. By 2036 the observatory has run under RMBL operation for nearly a decade. Federal funding has continued to be uneven; the savings and broader funding base built in the first phase have absorbed at least one significant shock. The marmot study passes its seventy-fifth year in the late 2030s and is read as a record of mammal life history nested inside a fully measured mountain Earth system rather than as a standalone study. A 2038 synthesis of the basin's coupled records is published — atmosphere through bedrock through biology, one continuous account of how a mountain watershed changed across the first four decades of the 21st century. By 2040 the institution faces the next horizon question: extend the observatory another generation, or fold it into a broader cross-mountain network. The campaign closes with the observatory operating, the records intact, and the integration that defined SAIL made durable.

## Lines of inquiry

Several lines of research mature through this period and several do not. The atmosphere-to-bedrock work that began with SAIL deepens substantially. Questions about how snow microphysics, soil moisture, and bedrock weathering jointly set the conditions for biological activity become tractable in ways they were not in the 2020s. The phenology work matures inside this coupled context — flowering shifts are read against simultaneous records of atmospheric moisture, snowpack persistence, and soil chemistry, not against weather data alone. The marmot study, now in its eighth decade, has its social and demographic record now nested in a fully measured habitat. The pollinator and floral-trait work continues, with floral chemistry now readable against the watershed's nutrient and water records. Microbial and biogeochemistry research at the East River matures into one of the most complete records of mountain subsurface activity anywhere.

Other lines do not mature here. Community co-production work — partnerships with the Ute Indian Tribe, water districts, county schools — continues at the level existing relationships sustain but does not become a defining feature of basin research. Large mechanistic experimental platforms outside the watershed are not built. Cross-mountain comparative work happens through individual guest-scientist projects rather than through a formal RMBL-led network. The retrospective AI synthesis of a century of records progresses but is scoped to what serves the coupled-systems questions rather than as a standalone program.

## Moments of choice within the primary horizon

1. **2027 — Which SAIL-era instruments RMBL takes on, replaces, or lets go as the federal campaign concludes.** *Shared inflection: `sail-transition-2027`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership, board, and federal program officers managing the wind-down of SAIL-era infrastructure
   - Alternatives: RMBL could take on the full instrument suite, betting that the integration is the asset. It could take on a minimum subset, accepting reduced coupling in exchange for lower operating cost. It could decline the transition and let the campaign end as designed.
   - Scenario assumption: RMBL takes on the full coupled suite — atmospheric, snow, soil, and stream — and commits to operating it permanently. This is the scenario's defining choice.

2. **2028 — How to weight endowment-style support against capital deployment within the campaign as it matures.** *Shared inflection: `endowment-capital-split-2028`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the Development & Advancement team
   - Alternatives: The campaign could front-load capital purchases of instruments and infrastructure, accepting thinner long-term operating support. It could prioritize endowment building, accepting slower physical buildout. It could pursue a balance.
   - Scenario assumption: The campaign favors capital and near-term operating support to lock in the instrument transition while the opportunity exists, with endowment growth scaled to the operating reality the observatory needs.

3. **2029 — How fast to grow the in-house technical team and what mix of skills to hire.** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the technical staff
   - Alternatives: RMBL could hire slowly and rely on guest-scientist time to run instruments. It could hire quickly with a focus on instrumentation technicians. It could hire with a focus on data engineering and integration.
   - Scenario assumption: RMBL hires substantially across both instrumentation and data engineering, recognizing that the observatory's value is the coupling and the coupling requires staff who can keep streams readable together.

4. **2032 — How much of any unexpected scientific opportunity to absorb against the cost of the long records.** *Shared inflection: `stewardship-priority-2032`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership, board, and the stewardship of the long biological records
   - Alternatives: RMBL could expand into newly funded lines of work and let some long records contract. It could refuse new work to protect every record. It could find a middle position.
   - Scenario assumption: The long records remain protected at the level needed to keep them intact. New scientific opportunities flow through the observatory rather than around it.

5. **2034 — Whether to extend the observatory's coupled measurements upslope into the alpine, or deepen what is already running across the existing East River footprint.** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership, the science staff, and guest scientists doing alpine work
   - Alternatives: Extending upslope would broaden the elevation range of coupled records, capturing the systems most exposed to warming. Deepening at the current footprint would let the existing records become denser and more analytically powerful. A partial extension could attempt both at lower depth.
   - Scenario assumption: RMBL deepens at the existing footprint, treating analytical depth as more distinctive than elevational range and reserving upslope extension for a later horizon.

6. **2037 — How to respond to a second wave of federal funding contraction that hits the watershed sciences specifically.** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and federal program officers managing reduced earth-systems funding
   - Alternatives: RMBL could draw aggressively on reserves to maintain full operation. It could reduce sampling intensity and protect the longest streams. It could partner more deeply with peer institutions and share operating cost.
   - Scenario assumption: RMBL draws on the reserves built earlier in the campaign and protects full coupling, treating the integration itself as the asset most at risk if reduced.

7. **2040 — What the next horizon looks like — extend the observatory another generation, fold it into a broader cross-mountain network, or open new directions alongside it.** *Shared inflection: `next-horizon-2040`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership, board, and the broader basin-science community
   - Alternatives: Continuing on the current footprint preserves what the campaign built. Folding into a cross-mountain network amplifies impact but dilutes RMBL's distinctive position. Opening new directions would mean returning to a more diversified portfolio.
   - Scenario assumption: The scenario does not foreclose this choice. It hands the institution an intact observatory and the credibility to make the choice well.

## Audience lens — research

If you work on mountain Earth systems, the basin during this period is a place where the measurements you need are already running and already coupled. You can arrive in spring, request access to a decade of overlapping atmospheric, snow, soil, hydrologic, and biological records on the same landscape, and have working data by the end of your first field season. The technical staff to support new instrument deployments is in place. If you work on the long biological records — flowers, pollinators, marmots — your study site is now embedded in one of the most fully measured mountain watersheds in the world, and the questions you can ask sharpen accordingly. If your work is mechanistic at scales smaller than the watershed, or if you need community co-production infrastructure, this is not the period when RMBL leans hardest toward you. The watershed bet shapes what is easiest to do here.

## Audience lens — institution

RMBL through this period feels like an institution that has chosen something specific and is doing it. The technical staff is larger than it has ever been, weighted toward the instrumentation and data engineering the observatory needs. The Gothic site hosts a denser set of permanent installations than at any time in its history. The atmosphere around campaign decisions is one of operating discipline rather than expansion — what does the observatory need to keep running, how does the savings buffer hold up against the next federal cycle. The community-facing footprint in Crested Butte is maintained at current scale rather than grown. The institution becomes legible to outside partners as the place where coupled mountain Earth-systems science is done permanently. That legibility is the strategic asset, and also the constraint — RMBL is recognizably one kind of place during this period rather than several.

## Audience lens — donor

Your contribution joins an effort to make permanent what may otherwise be remembered as a campaign that came and went. The atmospheric, snow, soil, and stream measurements that federal investment brought to the East River between 2021 and 2025 are the kind of integration that disappears when federal campaigns end. This scenario takes the position that the basin's distinctive contribution to global mountain science is exactly this coupled-systems capability paired with a century of biological records on the same landscape — and that an independent nonprofit is the kind of institution that can keep it running across political cycles. You are part of building the operating reality of a permanent observatory: the instruments themselves, the technicians who keep them calibrated, the data systems that hold the streams together, and the savings that protect the work when federal weather turns. It is a quieter offer than expansion, and a more durable one.

## Overlay robustness

**Central case.** Under gradual federal contraction, gradual AI evolution, and a moderate climate trajectory, the observatory transitions to RMBL operation across the late 2020s and runs continuously through 2040. The savings built in the early phase absorb the federal contractions of the 2030s. The data systems benefit from steady AI tooling improvements without depending on transformative tools that may not arrive. The coupled records become one of the basin's defining contributions to global mountain science. The long biological records continue without lapse alongside.

**Stress case — Sharper federal withdrawal.** If federal earth-systems funding contracts faster than expected, the operating cost of the observatory becomes harder to cover from outside grants. RMBL draws more heavily on the reserves built early in the campaign, slows the alpine extension question, and may need to reduce sampling intensity on the lowest-priority streams to protect the core coupling. The bet still holds — the integration stays intact — but the institution finishes the period with thinner buffer than planned and a harder operating posture going into the next horizon.

**Stress case — Faster AI evolution.** If AI tools mature faster than the central case assumes, the data integration work becomes substantially cheaper than the scenario budgeted for. RMBL is able to do more with the streams it already runs, and the synthesis questions that would otherwise have waited become tractable earlier. The data team grows more slowly than planned because the tools amplify the existing team's capacity. Resources free up to deepen the alpine question or to expand microbial and chemistry sampling at the existing footprint.

**Stress case — Climate stress above central case.** If warming runs hotter than the moderate trajectory — a snow-free season substantially longer by the late 2030s — the observatory becomes more valuable, not less, because the questions about how mountain systems behave during the transition are the ones every downstream user needs answered. Demand on the observatory rises. RMBL faces pressure to extend upslope sooner than planned to capture the alpine systems crossing thresholds. The savings buffer is tested by the need to add capacity faster than the campaign budgeted for.

## Plausibility caveats

This scenario rests on several assumptions that may not hold. It assumes the federal partners managing the SAIL-era instruments are willing to transfer them, or that RMBL can replace them at reasonable cost. If the transfer negotiations fail or the replacement cost is higher than expected, the early phase is harder and the observatory may transition with thinner coverage than planned. It assumes that donors will fund the operating reality of permanent infrastructure — technicians, calibration trips, multi-decade data stewardship — rather than only the science it produces. This is a different ask than the campaigns that built the capital infrastructure in the first place, and the donor base's appetite for it is genuinely uncertain.

The scenario assumes the basin's guest-scientist community will continue to bring the coupled-systems questions the observatory was built to answer. If the broader research community shifts toward different mountain sites, different questions, or different institutional arrangements, the observatory could run technically well while becoming less central to the science than the bet imagines.

The characteristic way this scenario could go wrong is concentration risk. The campaign puts most of its strategic weight on one anchor. If atmosphere-to-bedrock integration turns out not to be the basin's defining contribution — if, for example, retrospective synthesis across the long biological records or community-anchored work proves more distinctive in retrospect — the institution will have spent a decade and a campaign on something narrower than its breadth justified. The scenario's structural blind spot is that it cannot evaluate this from inside its own bet. The judgment that the watershed integration is the basin's distinctive global contribution is a strategic claim, not a fact, and the scenario depends on it being right.

## Looking further out: 2040–2050

*Speculative. Lower resolution than the primary horizon.*

By the mid-2040s the observatory has run under RMBL operation for nearly two decades. The marmot study is in its ninth decade. The meadow phenology series approaches sixty years. Federal funding for mountain Earth-systems work has gone through at least two more contractions and partial recoveries, and the observatory has continued through all of them. The basin's century of biological records sit inside a fully measured coupled-systems context — one of a small number of places in the world where this depth of integration exists at all, and the only one operated by an independent nonprofit. What happens next is less clear. A 2046 conversation about whether to fold the East River into a broader North American mountain observatory network is underway. A new generation of guest scientists, trained inside the coupled framework, is asking questions about the alpine transition that the existing footprint cannot fully answer. By 2050 RMBL is recognizably the institution that made permanent what may otherwise have been remembered as a campaign that came and went — and is facing the question of what permanence asks of the next campaign.
