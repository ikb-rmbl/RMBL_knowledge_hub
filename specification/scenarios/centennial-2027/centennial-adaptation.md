# Centennial Adaptation

*A Future Scenario in the `centennial-2027` set. Target magnitude, balanced. v1.0 under [Future Scenarios Framework v0.7](../../Future_scenarios_framework.md).*

---

## Synopsis

Centennial Adaptation assumes climate change has crossed thresholds in the basin that make basin science directly useful to water managers, ranchers, Forest Service planners, and tribal natural-resources offices across the Mountain West. The campaign comes in near the upper end of what RMBL might raise, and the institution makes a deliberate bet: build a standing translation function that connects the basin's long records to the decisions those records can inform. The marmot study, the meadow phenology series, and the snowmelt-driven plant work continue without lapse. Around them, RMBL hires a small team whose job is to make basin findings usable to people making decisions about water, land, and adaptation, and renovates RMBL365 to host the working relationships that translation requires. About half the work is continuity stewardship; the other half builds translation capacity. The scenario forgoes new atmospheric instrumentation, a large data-science buildout, and deep financial reserves. It tests whether RMBL can do this work without compromising the basic-research identity that draws guest scientists in the first place.

### Campaign deliverables

- **primary.** Build a standing translation function — a small team of two to four staff whose work is connecting basin science to Mountain West water management, agricultural adaptation, Forest Service and BLM planning, and tribal natural-resources offices. Funds salaries, travel, partnership coordination, and the production of decision-ready summaries from long basin records. Sized as a small nonprofit function, not as an endowed program of independent investigators.
- **primary.** Renovate RMBL365 in Crested Butte as the year-round venue where translation work happens — a place for water districts, agency planners, tribal staff, ranchers, and basin scientists to convene off-season, when decisions actually get made. Renovations add workshop and small-group meeting space, a working library of basin records, and modest staging for visiting partners. This is a building that already exists; the campaign makes it fit for purpose.
- **secondary.** Protect the basin's longest records — the marmot study reaching its hundredth year, the meadow phenology series, the snowmelt-driven plant demography work — at the level needed to keep them intact. Funds protocol stewardship, plot infrastructure, archival systems, and the technical staff who support the guest scientists carrying these records forward. Sized to ensure continuity, not expansion.
- **secondary.** Grow the in-house data and geospatial team modestly — one or two additional positions — so the translation function has decision-ready data behind it. The data team's work is shaped by what partners need, not by what would be most novel scientifically.
- **supporting.** Strengthen the savings and broader funding base that protect RMBL's independence — so the translation function survives federal funding shocks and so partners can count on RMBL being there for the multi-year cycles their planning processes require.

### Frontier support strategies

- **F.cont.1.** The campaign protects the basin's longest records — marmot demography, meadow phenology, snowmelt-driven plant work — through protocol stewardship, plot infrastructure, archival systems, and continuity of the technical staff who support guest scientists. The records are also the raw material the translation function draws on: a hundred years of marmot data, half a century of phenology, decades of snowmelt observation are what make basin findings credible to agency partners who need defensible evidence.
- **F.cont.3.** Subalpine plant demography under climate stress continues through protected plot infrastructure and protocol continuity. The translation function turns this work into something land managers and ranchers can use — how meadow communities are changing under earlier snowmelt, what that means for forage, pollination, and the timing of management actions.
- **F.innov.2.** This is the scenario's anchor frontier. The campaign builds the translation function directly: a small team, renovated meeting space at RMBL365, partnership coordination capacity, and the modest data tools needed to deliver decision-ready outputs. The work runs at scales appropriate to a small nonprofit — durable relationships with a defined set of partners rather than a federal-style program serving everyone.
- **F.both.1.** Post-snowpack ecology is where the continuity records and the translation work meet. The basin's long records establish what classical mountain systems looked like; the translation function carries the implications of crossing snowpack thresholds to the people making decisions about water and land in real time. Modest in-house data capacity supports both sides.

### Forgone

The scenario does not fund new atmospheric instrumentation to replace SAIL when the federal campaign winds down. The East River does not become a permanent atmosphere-to-bedrock observatory under this scenario; that work either finds other support or pauses. The scenario does not build a large in-house data-science program of the kind that could read a century of basin records at scales prior generations could not attempt — the data team grows modestly and is shaped by partner needs rather than by retrospective synthesis ambitions. The campaign does not fund broad community co-production work in the F.innov.4 sense, where local communities and tribes become co-designers of research agendas; the translation function is narrower, focused on connecting existing basin science to existing decision processes. Financial reserves grow but do not reach the depth that other scenarios in this set prioritize. Large mechanistic experimental platforms are not built.

### Seeds in the present

The seeds are already in the ground. The 2021–25 era saw basin findings move into regional and national press at a rate a quarter higher than the prior five years, and the most-linked story cluster of the period was about the widening gap between snowpack and runoff — a question Mountain West water managers were asking directly. The SAIL campaign brought atmospheric measurement into the East River and produced findings (Feldman et al., 2023) that water districts and Forest Service planners need to understand. The cross-lens planning themes show stakeholder pull from BLM Resource Management Plans, GMUG and White River National Forest plan revisions, Colorado Water Conservation Board proceedings, and Colorado Parks and Wildlife harvest and closure decisions. The Knowledge Commons itself — one product of the recent pattern where outside grants build RMBL's own tools — gives the translation function a starting platform. RMBL365, purchased in 2025, is the in-town venue this work needs.

## Setting

This scenario assumes that by the late 2020s, climate change has crossed thresholds in the basin that make basin science directly useful to the people making decisions about water, land, and adaptation across the Mountain West. Snowpack-to-runoff gaps that surprised water managers in the early 2020s have become a recurring planning problem. Foresummer drought has become the normal condition rather than the exception. Forest Service ranger districts are rewriting plans that depend on understanding how subalpine meadows are changing. Tribal natural-resources offices are asking what the long records say about systems their staff are responsible for. Colorado Parks and Wildlife is making harvest and closure decisions on evidence thinner than the basin's records could provide.

RMBL is one of the few institutions positioned to translate this work. The long records exist. The independence to do the work without political constraint exists. The relationships with agencies and downstream communities have been building for years. What does not yet exist is the standing capacity — staff, space, sustained partnership coordination — to do the translation work as a durable function rather than as occasional extra-duty for scientists already stretched.

The campaign comes in near the upper end of what RMBL might raise, and the institution chooses to build that capacity. A small translation team. A renovated RMBL365 as the venue where partners and basin scientists actually sit together off-season. Protected continuity of the records the translation work draws on. A modest data team behind it. The bet is that doing this work well will make basic research at the basin more valuable to guest scientists, not less — that the records become more interesting when they are connected to decisions people care about.

## The arc — three phases across the primary horizon

### Phase 1: Building the translation function (2026–2030)

The campaign launches in 2027 and closes in 2030. The first hires for the translation team come on in 2028 — initially two staff, focused on water and on land management. RMBL365 renovations begin in 2028 and finish in 2029, adding workshop space, a working library of basin records, and meeting rooms designed for off-season convenings.

The SAIL campaign winds down in 2027. Under this scenario, RMBL does not pick up the atmospheric instrumentation; the choice is to let that work find other homes and focus the campaign on translation. This is a real cost. Some guest scientists who valued the integrated atmospheric measurement reduce their basin work or move elsewhere.

The long records are protected from the start. Marmot demography, meadow phenology, and snowmelt-driven plant work continue without lapse. A modest data position is added in 2029 to support the translation team with decision-ready outputs. First sustained partnerships are formalized in 2029 and 2030 — with two Gunnison Valley water districts, with the GMUG National Forest on its plan revision, and with the Ute Indian Tribe's natural-resources office on a smaller, longer-horizon collaboration.

### Phase 2: Translation work matures (2031–2035)

By 2031 the translation team is at four staff and the function is operating as a durable part of how RMBL works. The team produces an annual basin science briefing for water and land partners, hosts twelve to fifteen off-season convenings a year at RMBL365, and contributes to two Forest Service plan revisions and one BLM Resource Management Plan revision during this phase.

The stewardship question comes to a head around 2032. With the translation function maturing, leadership and the board face a real decision: how much of the campaign's catalytic capacity goes to deepening translation work versus protecting the records that feed it. The scenario assumes the institution holds the balance — neither lets translation crowd out continuity nor lets continuity starve translation.

The marmot study reaches its hundredth year in the early 2030s. The centennial is marked publicly, and the translation team uses it to anchor a Mountain West conversation about what a century of one species' records can tell adjacent management questions about climate vulnerability in mountain mammals more broadly. Guest-scientist numbers stabilize. Some basic-research scientists raise concerns that translation work is changing the institution's character; leadership commits to maintaining clear separation between the translation function and the broader scientific community's research agendas.

### Phase 3: Translation as a durable RMBL function (2036–2040)

By the late 2030s the translation function is established. Water districts plan around RMBL inputs. Agency planners cite basin records routinely in plan documents. The Ute Indian Tribe partnership has matured into a sustained collaboration on phenology and snowmelt records that connect to tribal cultural and management priorities. Two of the four translation staff are now mid-career and have developed the kind of standing relationships that the work depends on.

The basic-research identity question gets its real test in this phase. The basin still draws guest scientists at roughly the rates it did in the late 2020s — a sign the translation function has not displaced the research environment. Some guest scientists now actively use the translation function as a way to extend their work's reach; others ignore it and continue their basic science as before. Both patterns are healthy.

The scenario closes in 2040 with the translation function visibly working but with real costs visible too. The atmospheric integration that SAIL began is now happening elsewhere. Financial reserves are thinner than other scenarios would have built. The institution faces a decision about whether to grow the translation function further into the 2040s or hold it at its current scale and focus on protecting what has been built.

## Lines of inquiry

The lines of inquiry that mature through this period are shaped by what the translation work surfaces. The basin's phenology records become a tool for predicting how Mountain West meadow systems will respond to continued snowmelt advance, used in Forest Service planning. The marmot record matures into one of the few continuous mammal demographic series of its length, with translation work connecting it to broader questions about climate vulnerability in hibernating mountain species relevant to state wildlife agencies. The snowmelt-driven plant demography work feeds directly into rangeland management questions about forage timing under foresummer drought.

Post-snowpack ecology becomes a central research theme, because the basin is one of the few places with records long enough to characterize what's being lost as snowpack thresholds are crossed. The translation function makes this work visible to the people who need it.

What doesn't mature under this scenario: large mechanistic experimental platforms, deep retrospective synthesis using AI tools at the scales the in-house data capacity would otherwise allow, atmosphere-to-bedrock integrated watershed science as a permanent RMBL function. These remain possible through guest-scientist work and external grants but are not what the campaign builds.

## Moments of choice within the primary horizon

1. **2027 — How to frame the campaign to donors: as continuity stewardship, as scientific expansion, or as translation of basin science to decision-makers** *Shared inflection: `campaign-framing-decision`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the Development & Advancement team
   - Alternatives: Lead with the centennial framing of the marmot study and other long records; lead with the East River watershed and atmospheric integration story; lead with the translation-of-science-to-management story; lead with a balanced multi-priority framing
   - Scenario assumption: Leadership leads with the translation story — that climate change has made basin science directly useful to Mountain West decision-makers and that RMBL is positioned to do that translation without compromising its independence — while protecting the records as the foundation that makes translation credible

2. **2027 — What to do as the SAIL atmospheric campaign winds down — pick up the instrumentation as a permanent RMBL function, find partners to take it on, or let it end** *Shared inflection: `sail-transition-2027`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the board
   - Alternatives: Make the East River a permanent atmosphere-to-bedrock observatory under RMBL operation; partner with a peer institution to keep instrumentation running; let SAIL end and focus campaign resources elsewhere
   - Scenario assumption: Leadership lets SAIL end as a permanent RMBL function and focuses the campaign on translation capacity. The cost is real — some guest scientists move work elsewhere — but the scenario's thesis demands the focus

3. **2028 — How to balance endowment and capital within the campaign — protect long-term operating capacity for the translation function, or invest in renovation and near-term hires** *Shared inflection: `endowment-capital-split-2028`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership, the board, and the Development & Advancement team
   - Alternatives: Heavier endowment to protect the translation function's salaries for decades; heavier capital to renovate RMBL365 and hire faster; a balanced split
   - Scenario assumption: A balanced split, with enough endowment to make the translation positions credible to mid-career hires who will not take the jobs if they look like soft-money positions

4. **2029 — Which partner relationships to make the durable anchors of the translation function — water districts, agency planners, tribal natural-resources offices, ranching organizations, or some combination** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the translation team
   - Alternatives: Concentrate on Gunnison Valley water districts as the closest natural partner; concentrate on federal land managers with the largest decision footprint; concentrate on the Ute Indian Tribe and other tribal partners on longer-horizon terms; spread across several partner types
   - Scenario assumption: Leadership commits to a defined set — two water districts, two federal land-management partners, the Ute Indian Tribe, and Colorado Parks and Wildlife — with the discipline to say no to other asks until those relationships are working

5. **2031 — How to keep the translation function from changing the basic-research character of the basin** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and senior guest scientists
   - Alternatives: Treat translation as a separate institutional function with clear walls between it and guest-scientist work; integrate translation into how all basin science gets framed; let the boundary be informal
   - Scenario assumption: Leadership commits to clear separation — translation staff do not direct guest-scientist research agendas, and guest scientists are not asked to do translation work as a condition of basin access. The boundary is articulated explicitly to the research community

6. **2032 — Which long-term records to prioritize for stewardship investment as needs and costs evolve mid-campaign** *Shared inflection: `stewardship-priority-2032`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the board
   - Alternatives: Concentrate stewardship on the marmot centennial as the highest-visibility record; balance across the marmot, phenology, and snowmelt-plant lineages; let stewardship needs be set year-by-year by what's most fragile
   - Scenario assumption: Leadership maintains the balanced commitment, recognizing that the translation function depends on all three record lineages, not just the most visible one

7. **2038 — Whether to grow the translation function further into the 2040s or hold it at the scale established in this campaign** *Shared inflection: `next-horizon-2040`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership, the board, and the next generation of staff
   - Alternatives: Plan a follow-on campaign that doubles translation capacity; hold the function at current scale and direct future fundraising elsewhere; pull back if the basic-research identity test has not held
   - Scenario assumption: Leadership begins planning to hold the function at current scale and direct next-horizon energy toward other gaps the campaign forgoes, especially atmospheric integration and deeper data capacity

## Audience lens — research

If your work depends on long basin records — phenology, marmots, snowmelt-driven plant demography — this is a good period to be doing basin science. The records are protected. The technical staff that supports your fieldwork is intact. RMBL365 gives you somewhere to stage off-season work. If your work touches questions water managers, agency planners, or tribal natural-resources staff are asking, the translation function is a route by which your findings reach those audiences without you having to build the relationships from scratch. If your work depends on the atmospheric integration SAIL began, this period is harder. The instruments are not there in the same way. You may find yourself moving some work to other mountain sites. The basic-research character of the basin holds — there is no pressure on you to make your work applied — but the institution's center of gravity has shifted toward translation in ways some scientists welcome and some do not.

## Audience lens — institution

RMBL becomes an institution that does translation work as a durable function — not as occasional extra-duty but as a real part of how it operates. Four people on staff whose work is connecting basin science to decisions. RMBL365 humming off-season with water districts, agency planners, tribal staff, and basin scientists in the same rooms. The basic-research mission continues alongside, with clear separation between the translation function and guest-scientist research agendas. The institution feels both more grounded in the Mountain West and more exposed — more obviously useful, and more obviously accountable for whether the translation work actually helps. The independence that lets RMBL do this work without political constraint becomes more visible as a feature, not just a quiet operating fact. By 2040 the institution has shown that a small nonprofit can do this work without losing its scientific character, which is the test this scenario was set up to run.

## Audience lens — donor

Your contribution joins an effort to build something specific: a standing capacity at RMBL to translate basin science into decisions about water, land, and adaptation across the Mountain West. The basin's long records — the marmot study reaching its hundredth year, the meadow phenology series, the snowmelt-driven plant work — are protected, because they are what makes the translation credible. RMBL365 becomes the year-round venue where the people making decisions and the people who understand the records sit together off-season, when planning actually happens. Your contribution is part of testing whether a small nonprofit field station, independent of political pressure, can take on this role without compromising the basic-research identity that draws guest scientists in the first place. It is not a promise that the bet pays off. It is an invitation to be part of finding out.

## Overlay robustness

**Central case.** Under gradual federal contraction, the translation function is the right bet — federal program staff capacity to deliver basin findings to land managers has thinned, leaving more demand for what RMBL can provide. Gradual AI evolution lets the modest data team produce better decision-ready outputs without requiring a large in-house data buildout. An RCP6.0 climate keeps the translation work salient through the horizon: snowpack changes, foresummer drought, and phenology shifts are visible enough that partners stay engaged. The scenario plays out roughly as the phase arc describes.

**Stress case — Federal contraction intensifies sharply after 2028.** Demand for the translation function rises further as federal capacity thins. RMBL's role becomes more visible. The risk is that demand exceeds the small team's capacity, and partners come to expect more than four staff can deliver. Leadership has to be disciplined about saying no, which is harder when the institutional story has been about being useful. Financial reserves, which this scenario already underbuilds, become a real vulnerability if a federal grant cycle the institution depends on collapses.

**Stress case — Faster AI evolution than the central case.** The modest data team becomes more productive than planned — AI tools let two data staff do what three or four would have done. The translation function delivers richer outputs. But the scenario's forgone investment in deeper retrospective synthesis becomes more visible as a real opportunity cost — the records could be read at scales the institution chose not to pursue. Pressure mounts to add data capacity from external grants, which raises questions about whether the institution should have built more of this in the campaign.

**Stress case — Climate change tracks closer to RCP8.5 in the basin.** Threshold crossings come faster. Snowpack-to-runoff gaps widen sharply. Some classical basin systems change in ways that make the long records harder to interpret as continuous series. Translation demand rises further, and the work shifts from informing planning to informing emergency response in some cases. The scenario's bet becomes more obviously right, but the basic-research identity question gets sharper — guest scientists asking whether basin science can still be about basic questions when everything is changing fast around them.

## Plausibility caveats

This scenario assumes the campaign reaches near the upper end of its realistic range, which is a fundraising assumption that the Development & Advancement team's actual donor-base intelligence will validate or revise. It assumes that climate threshold-crossings make basin science more useful to Mountain West decision-makers, not less — there is a version of the same climate change where partners become too overwhelmed by near-term crisis to engage with multi-year planning inputs from basin science, and the translation function finds fewer takers than expected. It assumes that mid-career staff with the right combination of scientific credibility and partnership skills can be hired and retained at salaries a nonprofit can pay. It assumes that the basic-research community continues to value basin access enough to absorb the institutional shift toward translation without major attrition.

The scenario's characteristic failure mode is institutional drift. The translation function changes the institution's character more than leadership intends. The clear separation between translation work and guest-scientist research erodes informally, year by year, until basin scientists experience pressure to make their work applied. Guest-scientist numbers fall. The basic-research identity that made the translation function credible in the first place weakens, and the institution finds itself a smaller version of a federal program-managing organization rather than the nonprofit field station it set out to remain.

The scenario's structural blind spot is that translation is being treated as a function RMBL can do well at small scale because the institution is independent and trusted. The alternative reading — that translation is hard precisely because the institutional relationships, the legal and regulatory literacy, and the political navigation required are not what a basin field station has built capacity for — is real and is not fully addressed in the scenario. A four-person team may not be enough for the work the scenario asks it to do, and the campaign may underbuild rather than overbuild.

## Looking further out: 2040–2050

*Speculative. Lower resolution than the primary horizon.*

If the bet plays out, by the early 2040s RMBL has shown that basin science can become directly useful to Mountain West water management, agricultural adaptation, and Forest Service planning at scale — and that doing so makes basic research more valuable, not less. The marmot record passes its centennial visibly. The phenology and snowmelt-plant records continue. Translation work has become a durable part of how the institution operates, embedded in partnerships that have outlasted individual staff turnover.

The questions the 2040s open: whether the translation function should grow further or stay at the scale this campaign built. Whether the atmospheric integration this campaign forgoes can be picked back up through some combination of guest-scientist work and a follow-on campaign. Whether the basic-research identity holds for another generation, as the staff who built the translation function move on. By 2050 the answers depend on choices the next generation of leadership will make in conditions this scenario cannot foresee — federal funding environment, climate trajectory, the shape of mountain communities the institution serves. The bet was that the translation function would prove its value enough to be worth carrying forward. By 2050, that question has its first real answers.
