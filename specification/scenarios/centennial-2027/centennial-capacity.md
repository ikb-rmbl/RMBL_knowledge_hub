# Centennial Capacity

*A Future Scenario in the `centennial-2027` set. Target magnitude, innovation-leaning. v3.0 under [Future Scenarios Framework v0.7](../../Future_scenarios_framework.md).*

---

## Synopsis

Centennial Capacity assumes the campaign comes in near the upper end of what RMBL might raise, and that the institution makes a deliberate choice about where the new money goes. The basin's longest records — the marmot study now in its eighth decade, the meadow phenology series, the snowmelt-driven plant work — are protected at the level needed to keep them intact, but no more. The major investments go elsewhere: a substantially larger in-house data team that can read a century of accumulated records at scales prior generations could not attempt, and a renovated RMBL365 building that anchors year-round partnerships with the Ute Indian Tribe, Gunnison Valley water districts, and county schools. About a third of the work is continuity stewardship; the rest grows data capacity and community partnerships. The scenario forgoes new atmospheric instrumentation, large mechanistic experiments, and deep financial reserves. It asks donors to value building durable connective tissue rather than only protecting it.

### Campaign deliverables

- **primary.** A substantially larger in-house data and archival team — data scientists, archivists, and software engineers who digitize a century of field notebooks, plot records, and instrument streams, build the tools that make them machine-readable, and partner with guest scientists doing the analysis. This is the largest single area of growth in the scenario and represents a structural change in what RMBL's staff does.
- **primary.** Renovation and year-round staffing of RMBL365 as a community partnership hub — laboratory and meeting space for water districts, classrooms for county schools, and dedicated coordinators who carry the relationships with the Ute Indian Tribe, Gunnison Valley water managers, and Forest Service planners. Community partnership becomes a defining feature of RMBL's work rather than a quiet background commitment.
- **secondary.** Critical-path protection of the basin's longest continuous records — protocol stewardship for the marmot study, plot infrastructure for the meadow phenology series, archival systems for the snowmelt-driven plant work. These records are kept whole, but the campaign does not expand the field side of this work.
- **supporting.** Modest growth in the savings and broader funding base that protect RMBL's independence through funding shocks, sized to buffer the data team and community staff against the next round of federal contraction rather than to underwrite the institution at large.

### Frontier support strategies

- **F.cont.1.** The campaign protects the basin's longest records at the level needed to keep them intact across the next fifteen years. Protocols, plot infrastructure, and archival systems are sustained; observer succession is supported through continued partnership with the guest scientists who carry the records. The investment is real but narrower than in scenarios where stewardship is the central bet — the records are kept whole so they can be re-read by the data team, not expanded as a field program in their own right.
- **F.innov.3.** This is where the largest investment goes. A substantially expanded in-house data team — data scientists, archivists, software engineers — digitizes a century of field notebooks, plot records, and instrument streams, builds machine-readable archives, and develops the AI-assisted tools that let guest scientists ask questions of the accumulated record at scales prior generations could not attempt. The Knowledge Commons grows into a working research platform.
- **F.innov.4.** The renovated RMBL365 building anchors year-round partnerships with the Ute Indian Tribe, Gunnison Valley water districts, county schools, and Forest Service planners. Dedicated coordinators — a few full-time staff — carry the relationships, co-design research questions with partners, and translate basin findings into the formats agencies and tribal natural-resources offices actually use. Community work becomes structural rather than occasional.
- **F.innov.5.** The campaign builds modest savings and a broader donor base sized to protect the data team and community staff against funding shocks. RMBL's independence is supported not through large reserves but through the working partnerships and donor relationships the scenario itself develops — independence as a practical condition of the new work, not as a separately endowed deliverable.

### Forgone

This scenario does not fund new atmospheric instrumentation. The radars, radiometers, and flux towers that the SAIL campaign brought to the East River are not picked up by RMBL when federal support winds down; that infrastructure either finds another home or comes out. Large mechanistic experimental platforms — common gardens, factorial warming experiments, paired-treatment networks across the basin — are not built. Deep financial reserves that would protect the institution at large through a multi-year funding collapse are not accumulated; the savings the campaign does build are sized to protect the new data team and community staff, not RMBL as a whole. Field-side expansion of the long records — new plots, new sites, new instruments on the marmot slopes — is not pursued. The scenario protects the records at critical-path level and bets the rest on what RMBL can do with the records and with its community.

### Seeds in the present

Several things already in place make this scenario possible. The Knowledge Commons itself is a product of the recent pattern where outside grants build RMBL's own tools and capacity, which then attract more grants — the data team this scenario expands already exists at about four people. RMBL365, the year-round building in Crested Butte purchased in 2025, is the platform the community partnership work is built on. The basin's longest records — the marmot study, the meadow phenology series, the snowmelt work — are documented in the era primers and in landmark papers from Inouye, Anderson, Campbell, CaraDonna, and the Blumstein marmot lineage. The cross-lens planning themes that surfaced in 2026 — the agency-pull theme around land management decisions, the water-governance theme, the connective-tissue diagnosis across scale and integration gaps — describe exactly the kind of work this scenario funds.

## Setting

This scenario takes as its central assumption that the campaign comes in near the upper end of what RMBL might raise, and that the institution uses the extra room not to expand everything but to grow two things substantially: an in-house data team that can read a century of accumulated records, and a set of year-round community partnerships anchored at RMBL365. The long records are protected at the level needed to keep them intact. They are not the centerpiece.

The basin in 2026 is a place where atmospheric scientists, hydrologists, and ecologists have been sharing instruments for several years. The SAIL campaign brought radars and flux towers to the East River; the marmot study above Gothic is in its eighth decade; a Tier 1 shortage has been in effect on the Colorado River since 2021. Federal funding has contracted. Some partner institutions in the region face restrictions on what they can study; RMBL does not. Water districts and tribal natural-resources offices in the Gunnison Basin are making decisions about flows, contaminants, and habitat with less evidence than they would like.

Against that backdrop, this scenario makes a particular choice. The data team grows from about four people to something closer to ten, with new strength in archival digitization and AI-assisted analysis. RMBL365 is renovated to include laboratory and meeting space, and dedicated coordinators carry partnerships with the Ute Indian Tribe, Gunnison Valley water districts, and county schools. The fieldwork that has defined RMBL for a century continues, but the institution starts to feel as much like a working data archive and a community hub as a summer field station.

## The arc — three phases across the primary horizon

### Phase 1: Building the team and the building (2026–2030)

The campaign closes in 2027 and 2028. RMBL begins hiring against the data team plan: two new data scientists in 2028, an archivist and a software engineer in 2029, two more roles by 2030. The team starts the slow work of digitizing a century of field notebooks, plot records, and instrument logs. RMBL365 enters renovation in 2028, reopening in 2030 with laboratory space, meeting rooms, and offices for community partnership staff. Two coordinators are hired — one focused on water-district and Forest Service partnerships, one focused on tribal and schools partnerships. Stewardship of the long records continues without disruption. The marmot study, the meadow phenology series, and the snowmelt-driven plant work each get the protocol and archival support they need, and no more. The SAIL atmospheric instruments wind down on the federal side; RMBL does not pick them up. The first AI-assisted analyses of the digitized phenology archive appear in 2030, mostly as proof-of-concept work with guest scientists.

### Phase 2: The records become readable, the partnerships take shape (2031–2035)

By the early 2030s the data team has grown to roughly ten people and the digitized archive covers most of the marmot and phenology records back to the 1970s. Guest scientists begin running analyses that prior generations could not attempt — phenology trends segmented by individual plant lineage, marmot social network reconstructions across decades, snowmelt-driven plant decline traced through annotated field notebooks. The first major synthesis papers appear in 2032 and 2033. At RMBL365, the community partnership work matures. A standing collaboration with the Gunnison Valley water districts produces shared monitoring protocols; the partnership with the Ute Indian Tribe yields co-designed research questions about culturally important plants and water sources; county schools run year-round programming in the renovated classrooms. The marmot study passes its seventieth year in 2033, marked publicly but without expansion. A second federal contraction in 2033 stresses the system; the modest savings the campaign built absorb the shock for the data team and community staff.

### Phase 3: The connective tissue holds (2036–2040)

By the late 2030s, the work the campaign funded is recognizable as something new about RMBL. The data team has produced a working platform that guest scientists use routinely — the Knowledge Commons has grown into a research environment, not just an archive. AI-assisted re-readings of the long records have produced a body of synthesis work that situates the basin in global mountain-system science. The community partnerships have matured into standing relationships. Water districts use RMBL-produced records in their planning; tribal natural-resources offices co-author findings; county schools have a decade of students who grew up doing basin science at RMBL365. The long records continue intact. The next horizon planning conversation begins around 2038, and the question on the table is whether the basin's contribution can grow further outward — to peer mountain stations, to broader Mountain West water governance, to a generation of scientists trained on RMBL-built tools. The marmot study has been protected; the data tools and community partnerships are doing more of the work of making it matter.

## Lines of inquiry

The deepest line of work that matures in this period is the re-reading of the long records. Phenology trends that were known in aggregate become resolvable at the level of individual plants and lineages. Marmot social networks, which had been studied in five- and ten-year windows, are reconstructed across decades. Snowmelt-driven decline in subalpine plants is traced through annotated field notebooks back to the 1970s. The phenology mismatch question — whether observed shifts reflect plasticity or evolutionary change — gets new traction as long-lineage data become tractable. A second line of work emerges from the community partnerships. Co-designed research with the Gunnison Valley water districts produces paired flow and ecology records tied to specific reservoir operations. Work with the Ute Indian Tribe yields findings about culturally important plants and water sources that would not have been done otherwise. School partnerships produce a longitudinal dataset of student-collected observations that becomes a real research resource by the late 2030s. What does not mature: the atmospheric and watershed integration work that SAIL began does not continue at RMBL after federal support ends. Mechanistic experimental work — common gardens, factorial warming, paired-treatment networks — is not part of this scenario. The basin remains a place where observation and synthesis are the strengths, not manipulation.

## Moments of choice within the primary horizon

1. **2028 — How to split the campaign close between capital projects, in-house staff growth, and savings.** *Shared inflection: `endowment-capital-split-2028`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and board, in consultation with the Development and Advancement team
   - Alternatives: Weight more heavily toward savings and independence, building deeper buffers at the expense of the data team and community work. Weight more heavily toward stewardship of the long records, with smaller new programs. Weight toward the data team and RMBL365 renovation, accepting thinner reserves.
   - Scenario assumption: RMBL weights toward data team growth and RMBL365 renovation, with savings sized to protect the new staff rather than the institution at large.

2. **2029 — How to structure the year-round community partnerships at RMBL365 — as service delivery, as co-designed research, or as governance partnership.** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the new community partnership coordinators
   - Alternatives: A service model where RMBL provides expertise and space to community partners on request. A co-design model where research questions are shaped jointly with water districts, tribal offices, and schools. A governance model where partners participate in setting RMBL priorities.
   - Scenario assumption: RMBL chooses co-design — partners shape research questions but RMBL retains institutional direction. This is more demanding than service delivery but less destabilizing than shared governance.

3. **2030 — Whether the digitized archive and AI-assisted tools are built primarily for guest scientists or also made publicly available.** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the data team lead
   - Alternatives: Restrict access to credentialed guest scientists, protecting partner researchers' priority. Build a fully public platform from the start. Build for guest scientists first with a planned public release after a few years.
   - Scenario assumption: RMBL builds for guest scientists first and releases publicly in stages, with the most basic phenology and marmot summaries reaching the public Commons by the mid-2030s.

4. **2032 — How to mark the marmot study's seventy-fifth year — as a public moment, an internal milestone, or a fundraising platform.** *Shared inflection: `stewardship-priority-2032`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the guest scientists carrying the marmot study
   - Alternatives: A major public celebration tied to a follow-on campaign. A working scientific symposium with limited public component. A quiet milestone marked mainly within the research community.
   - Scenario assumption: RMBL holds a working symposium with modest public framing, declining to use the centenary as a follow-on fundraising platform.

5. **2033 — How to respond to a second federal funding contraction that stresses both guest-scientist work and RMBL operations.** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and board
   - Alternatives: Draw down the modest savings to maintain all new work. Pause hiring in the data team to protect the long records and community staff. Reduce community programming to protect research staff.
   - Scenario assumption: RMBL draws down savings partially and pauses one of the planned later data hires, protecting both the records and the community work.

6. **2037 — Whether to expand the community partnership model beyond the Gunnison Basin to broader Mountain West water governance work.** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the community partnership coordinators
   - Alternatives: Stay focused on Gunnison Basin partners, deepening rather than broadening. Take on regional partnerships with downstream water managers and other tribal nations. Build a hub-and-spoke model with RMBL as a connector across mountain communities.
   - Scenario assumption: RMBL stays focused on the Gunnison Basin through 2040, treating regional expansion as a question for the next horizon rather than this one.

7. **2039 — Whether the next campaign extends the data and community work outward or returns to a stewardship anchor.** *Shared inflection: `next-horizon-2040`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and board, beginning the next-horizon planning conversation
   - Alternatives: Extend the data platform and community model to peer mountain stations. Return to a stewardship-anchored campaign that protects what has been built. Pivot toward mechanistic experimental work that this scenario forgoes.
   - Scenario assumption: RMBL begins planning a next horizon that extends the data and community work outward, with stewardship of the records as a continuing baseline.

## Audience lens — research

If you are a scientist considering bringing your work to RMBL during this period, what you find is something newer than what the basin offered a decade earlier. The long records are intact and accessible, but the bigger change is that they are now digitized, machine-readable, and accompanied by working tools that let you ask questions you could not have asked before. The in-house data team partners with you on analysis rather than just hosting your files. If your work touches water management, tribal natural resources, or community-relevant questions, RMBL365 gives you year-round access to partners and co-designed research opportunities that did not exist in the seasonal Gothic model. What you do not find: new atmospheric instrumentation, factorial experimental platforms, or expansion of the long records into new sites. The basin offers depth of record and depth of partnership, not breadth of new infrastructure.

## Audience lens — institution

If you work at RMBL during this period, the institution feels meaningfully different from the seasonal field station of 2026. The Gothic site is still the summer heart of the work, but RMBL365 in Crested Butte is staffed year-round and hosts a steady rhythm of water-district meetings, tribal partnership work, school programming, and visiting researchers. The data team has grown from a handful of people to something more like a small department, and the work it does — digitizing archives, building tools, partnering with guest scientists on analysis — is recognized as central to the mission rather than support for it. The long records are protected but quieter; the field side of the institution is steadier rather than growing. You feel a shift in what RMBL is for: less a place where scientists come to do their fieldwork, more a place that holds records, builds tools, and convenes a community around questions that matter to the basin.

## Audience lens — donor

If you are considering a gift to this campaign, what your contribution is part of building is a particular bet about where RMBL's next century of contribution comes from. The basin's longest records are protected — the marmot study passes its seventy-fifth year, the phenology and snowmelt work continue without lapse — but the larger investment goes into two things that compound differently. One is the in-house data team that can finally read a century of accumulated records at scales prior generations could not attempt. The other is the year-round partnerships at RMBL365 with the Ute Indian Tribe, Gunnison Valley water districts, and county schools. Your contribution joins the work of making RMBL not only a place where science happens but a place where science connects to a community and to its own deep archive. The bet this campaign asks you to consider is that durable connective tissue matters as much as protected records.

## Overlay robustness

**Central case.** Under continued gradual federal contraction, steady AI evolution, and a roughly mid-range warming trajectory, the scenario plays out close to as described. The data team grows on schedule; the community partnerships mature; the long records are protected; the second federal contraction in 2033 is absorbed by the modest savings the campaign built. The basin's contribution by 2040 is recognizably larger than the basin itself, through the data tools and community partnerships rather than through new field infrastructure.

**Stress case — Deep federal collapse.** A sharper and more sustained federal contraction in the late 2020s reduces the guest-scientist pipeline that the data team partners with, leaving the tools well-built but less used. The community partnerships become more central than expected — water districts and tribal offices, less affected by federal cuts, sustain the work. RMBL becomes more of a community institution and less of a research institution than the scenario assumed. The long records survive because they were protected at critical-path level.

**Stress case — AI capability surge.** Faster-than-expected AI capability makes the data team's tools more powerful but also commodifies some of what they do. The team shifts toward archival quality and domain integration rather than tool-building. Synthesis papers from re-read records come faster than expected, and the basin becomes an early example of what a century of careful records can yield when modern tools are brought to bear.

**Stress case — Sharp warming acceleration.** A warming trajectory at the higher end of central projections stresses the long records — the snowpack-thin years intensify, phenology mismatches sharpen, marmot demography destabilizes. The records become more valuable because they document the transition, but the field side is harder to sustain at the protected level. RMBL faces pressure to redirect data-team capacity toward real-time monitoring rather than retrospective synthesis.

## Plausibility caveats

This scenario rests on several external assumptions worth naming. It assumes the campaign reaches the upper end of what RMBL might raise — a fundraising outcome that depends on donor base depth the planning process cannot verify. It assumes that the data team can be staffed at the level described, in a hiring environment where data scientists are in broad demand. It assumes that the community partnerships described will be welcomed by the partners — the Ute Indian Tribe, water districts, and schools — on terms RMBL can sustain. None of these are guaranteed. The scenario's most distinctive risk is structural and specific to its bet. The campaign protects the long records at critical-path level rather than expanding them, and routes the larger investment into data tools and community work. If the long records turn out to need more than critical-path support — a key observer succession fails, a plot is lost, a protocol breaks in a way the protected level cannot absorb — the data team has less to work with and the bet loses its anchor. The scenario's blind spot is its assumption that connective tissue matters more than protected records at the current moment; a careful reader can reasonably disagree. A second blind spot is institutional: the scenario assumes RMBL can hold a data team and a community partnership operation together with a small leadership group, and that the cultural shift from seasonal field station to year-round connective institution can be managed without losing what made RMBL distinctive.

## Looking further out: 2040–2050

*Speculative. Lower resolution than the primary horizon.*

By the late 2040s, if this scenario's bet plays out, RMBL has become something the 2026 institution could only partly imagine. A century of basin records have been re-read at scales prior generations could not attempt, and the synthesis work has placed the basin in global mountain-system science in a way the original observations alone could not. The partnerships at RMBL365 have matured into standing relationships — water districts, tribal natural-resources offices, and schools treat RMBL as part of the community's working infrastructure, not as a visiting research presence. The marmot study, now in its eighth decade, continues. The next-horizon question, opened around 2038, is whether the data and community model can extend outward to peer mountain stations and to broader Mountain West water governance. This is speculative; the further future is hazier than the primary horizon. What seems durable is that the basin's contribution has become bigger than the basin itself, through the connective tissue this campaign chose to build.
