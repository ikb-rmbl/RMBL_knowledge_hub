# Centennial Adaptive Co-Management

*A Future Scenario in the `centennial-2027-upside` set. Target magnitude, innovation-leaning. v1.0 under [Future Scenarios Framework v0.15](../../Future_scenarios_framework.md).*

---

## Synopsis

This scenario describes an upside-tail future in which a sustained shift in public attitudes — not one law, but a decade of climate-driven recognition — makes ecological science a routine input to how Western land and water get managed. States, tribes, water districts, and federal field offices adopt adaptive co-management as the working paradigm, and they need continuous scientific input to run it. RMBL responds by building a translation function at a scale no realistic central-case campaign would support: dozens of staff embedded across agency, tribal, and water-district partnerships, working out of a renovated RMBL365 and from partner offices across the Mountain West. The basin's long records continue; the marmot study passes its 75th year, the meadow phenology series approaches 65. What's forgone is institutional simplicity. Translation becomes the largest single thing RMBL does, and the loudest internal tension is whether the basin remains, in identity and practice, a place where basic science is done. Donors are invited into that experiment.

### Campaign deliverables

- **primary.** Build a sustained translation function — staff who work directly with tribal natural-resources offices, water districts, BLM and Forest Service field offices, and state agencies to convert basin science into usable input for planning and adaptive management. This is the largest single commitment in the scenario and the one most distinctive to it: a translation team numbering in the dozens by the late 2030s, embedded across partner operations, not housed only in Gothic.
- **primary.** Renovate and expand RMBL365 in Crested Butte as the year-round hub for partnership work — convening space for water districts and tribal staff, offices for embedded translation staff, and community-facing rooms for stakeholder meetings. The building becomes the institution's most-used facility outside the summer season.
- **secondary.** Protect the basin's long records as continuing scientific assets. Steward the marmot demographic study through its 75-year mark, sustain the meadow phenology series, and keep the snowmelt-plant work going. Fund the archival systems and observer succession that make these records usable both for basic-research guest scientists and as inputs to the translation function.
- **secondary.** Expand RMBL's data systems and geospatial team to support continuous, decision-ready data products — the kind that water managers and tribal staff can actually use month-to-month. This grows the existing technical core rather than creating a parallel structure.
- **supporting.** Sustain hosting infrastructure for peer mountain stations whose scientists want to compare basin records with their own. Cross-mountain comparison becomes a routine part of how translation work is validated.
- **supporting.** Build savings that buffer RMBL against funding shocks and protect institutional independence as the translation function scales — so that partnership commitments to tribes and water districts can be honored across funding cycles.

### Frontier support strategies

- **F.cont.1.** The campaign protects the basin's long records — marmots, meadows, snowmelt-driven plants — through archival systems, observer-succession support, and data infrastructure. In this scenario the records serve two audiences at once: the guest scientists who continue to use them for basic-research questions, and the translation staff who feed them into agency and tribal planning cycles.
- **F.innov.2.** This frontier is the scenario's center of gravity. The campaign funds the people, the partnership infrastructure, and the year-round space at RMBL365 that let basin science feed continuously into water management, agricultural adaptation, Forest Service planning, and tribal natural-resources work. The scale is well beyond what a central-case campaign would support.
- **F.innov.4.** Community-science partnerships and stakeholder co-production become routine operating practice rather than projects. The campaign funds the staff time, governance work, and convening space that let tribal nations, water districts, and local communities help shape what gets studied and how findings are used.
- **F.both.3.** Cross-mountain comparison is supported as connective tissue. Hosting peer-station scientists in the basin, and sending RMBL staff to peer stations, helps validate the translation model and lets the basin's distinctive long records contribute to questions other ranges are asking.

### Forgone

This scenario does not invest at scale in atmosphere-to-bedrock integration or in new sensing infrastructure beyond what the translation work requires. The instrument-heavy frontier that grew out of SAIL is left to external partners and federal programs. Retrospective AI synthesis of the basin's historical records is supported only where it directly feeds translation, not as a standalone research direction. The scenario also forgoes the institutional simplicity of staying small. By the late 2030s, RMBL is recognizably a different kind of organization than it was in 2026 — translation is the largest single thing it does, and basic research, while continuing, is no longer the institution's most visible function. Some guest scientists who valued RMBL precisely for its quiet, focused character will find the new institution less to their taste. That loss is real and is part of what is forgone.

### Seeds in the present

The seeds are already visible. The 2021–25 era saw basin findings move from journal pages into regional and national press coverage on water, pollinator decline, and snowpack — a deliberate communications shift by RMBL and its partners. The cross-lens planning themes show the same signal: fifteen of nineteen clusters in the land-management theme are stakeholder-pull, meaning agencies and tribes are already waiting on basin evidence. The Tier 1 Colorado River shortage declared in 2021 made water managers newly receptive to ecological inputs. RMBL365, purchased in 2025, gives the institution a year-round community-facing platform. The existing technical core — geospatial data scientist, GIS manager, instrumentation staff — is the kernel around which a translation function can grow. The Knowledge Commons itself, a product of the recent pattern where outside grants build RMBL's own tools, is the data platform that translation work will depend on.

## Setting

This scenario takes as its central assumption a durable shift in public attitudes toward environmental science. Not a single political moment, but a decade of compounding climate stress — bad fire years, low-runoff years, agricultural losses, tribal water-rights settlements — that makes continuous ecological input feel necessary to people who run land and water systems. By the early 2030s, adaptive co-management is the working paradigm across much of the Mountain West. The Western Governors' Association has an adaptation framework that calls for multi-year ecological monitoring as planning input. Several tribal nations have negotiated expanded co-management roles on public lands. Water and biodiversity markets are integrating real-time ecological data. BLM and Forest Service planning is being revised on shorter cycles tied to monitoring rather than decade-long environmental-impact processes.

Federal regulatory reform of the kind that produced the Clean Air Act has not happened and is not assumed. The change is subnational and operational — states, tribes, regional compacts, and water districts adopting adaptive co-management because climate makes the old planning cycles untenable. Foundation funding for translation work has expanded substantially. Federal funding has partially recovered from its mid-2020s contraction, though not to pre-disruption levels.

In this environment, RMBL raises well above the realistic campaign ceiling on the translation and community side. The institution uses that capacity to build a translation function at a scale no central-case campaign would support — dozens of staff embedded across agency, tribal, and water-district partnerships by the late 2030s. The basin's long records continue. The marmot study passes its 75th year in 2038. But the institution's center of gravity has moved.

## The arc — three phases across the primary horizon

### Phase 1: Building the partnership infrastructure (2026–2030)

The campaign launches in 2027 around RMBL's centennial. The early commitments are recognizable: protect the long records, renovate RMBL365 to support year-round work, grow the data team. What distinguishes this scenario is what gets built alongside. RMBL hires its first dedicated translation staff — initially three or four people working with the Upper Gunnison River Water Conservancy District, the Ute Mountain Ute and Southern Ute natural-resources offices, and the local Forest Service ranger districts. The work is modest at first: standing meetings, joint data products, helping translate monitoring data into planning inputs. RMBL365 renovations finish in 2029, giving the partnership work a year-round home in Crested Butte. Foundation funders, watching adaptive co-management gain traction at the state level, begin underwriting translation work at scales that surprise even RMBL leadership. By 2030, the translation team is eight people, and similar partnership requests are arriving from outside the basin.

### Phase 2: Scaling translation across the Mountain West (2031–2035)

The Western Governors' Association adopts an adaptation framework in 2032 that calls for continuous ecological monitoring as planning input. Several tribal nations negotiate expanded co-management agreements on public lands. Water and biodiversity markets begin integrating ecological data in ways that require sustained scientific partners. RMBL's translation work scales accordingly. By 2034 the team is more than twenty people, with staff embedded in partner offices across Colorado, Wyoming, New Mexico, and Utah. The model that worked in the Gunnison Basin — long-term records translated into planning input through standing relationships — gets adapted to other ranges. Cross-mountain comparison becomes a routine part of how findings get validated. The basin's long records continue to be collected by a small succession of observers; the marmot study reaches its 70-year mark in 2033 with no break in continuity. Inside RMBL, the loudest tension begins to surface: some longtime guest scientists worry that the institution is becoming something other than the place they knew.

### Phase 3: Translation as institutional center of gravity (2036–2040)

By the late 2030s, translation is the largest single thing RMBL does. The team numbers in the dozens; partnerships with tribal nations, water districts, and federal field offices are the most visible part of how the institution operates externally. RMBL365 has been expanded a second time to accommodate the partnership work. The marmot study passes its 75-year mark in 2038, an occasion marked with a basin gathering that draws both basic-research scientists and translation partners. The meadow phenology series approaches 65 years. Guest scientists continue to come to Gothic in summers, and the basic-research community remains real and active — but it is no longer the institution's primary public face. Peer institutions in other ranges and on other continents begin adopting the RMBL translation model. RMBL leadership spends substantial time visiting peer stations as the model gets exported. The internal tension about institutional identity remains unresolved at the close of the campaign horizon, and leadership treats that openness as part of the experiment rather than a problem to be solved.

## Lines of inquiry

The basin's long-running scientific questions continue, but they are joined by a set of translation-driven questions that did not exist before. The marmot work extends into questions about how social structure and individual life-history variation propagate through climate stress; the molecular-age tools developed in the early 2020s mature into routine inputs. The meadow phenology series continues its long trajectory, now feeding both basic-research questions about plasticity and evolution and applied questions about pollinator-pollination service for ranchers and orchardists across the region. Snowmelt-driven plant work continues and increasingly informs water-yield forecasting at scales water managers can use. The atmosphere-to-bedrock integration work continues at the basin scale through federal partners, though RMBL does not lead it. Cross-mountain comparative work — pollinator decline across ranges, snowpack thresholds in different geologies, demographic responses across tribal and federally managed lands — emerges as a substantial line of inquiry because translation partners across the Mountain West increasingly want comparable evidence. What does not mature: instrument-heavy new sensing campaigns, retrospective AI synthesis as a standalone direction, basic-research-only frontiers that lack a translation pathway.

## Moments of choice within the primary horizon

1. **2028 — How aggressively to scale the translation function during the campaign — staying small and focused, growing to a moderate dedicated team, or committing to translation as a defining institutional function.** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and board
   - Alternatives: A central-case campaign would build a modest translation capacity of two or three staff. This scenario assumes leadership and board commit to a much larger translation function as the campaign's defining priority.
   - Scenario assumption: Leadership and board read the public-attitude shift early and commit to translation at a scale that defines the campaign and reshapes the institution.

2. **2029 — How extensively to renovate RMBL365 — modest upgrades for year-round housing and workshop space, or substantial expansion to support a translation team and partner convenings.** *Shared inflection: `rmbl365-renovation-scope`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership, board, and Development team
   - Alternatives: Modest renovation preserves capital for other priorities. Substantial renovation commits to RMBL365 as the institutional hub for partnership work.
   - Scenario assumption: Substantial renovation is chosen, and a second expansion follows in the late 2030s.

3. **2031 — Whether to expand translation work beyond the Gunnison Basin to other ranges across the Mountain West, or to keep the model basin-focused.** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and translation partners
   - Alternatives: Staying basin-focused preserves institutional simplicity. Expanding turns RMBL into a regional translation institution and stretches a small organization.
   - Scenario assumption: Expansion is chosen, and within four years staff are embedded across four states.

4. **2033 — How to handle the growing tension between the translation function's institutional dominance and the basic-research community's sense of what RMBL is.** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership, board, and the basic-research guest-scientist community
   - Alternatives: Reassure the basic-research community by capping translation growth, formalize a separation between basic and translation work, or treat the tension as an ongoing institutional experiment.
   - Scenario assumption: Leadership treats the tension as an experiment to be carried, not resolved — investing in conversations rather than structural separations.

5. **2035 — Whether to formalize a network with peer stations adopting the translation model, or to share the model informally without taking on convening responsibilities.** *Shared inflection: `cross-mountain-network-form`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and peer mountain stations
   - Alternatives: A formal network gives RMBL a hemispheric role but stretches staff. Informal sharing keeps the institution focused on its own translation work.
   - Scenario assumption: RMBL takes on a formal convening role, lighter than a central network but more than informal sharing.

6. **2037 — Whether to build substantial savings that buffer the institution against funding shocks, given long-term commitments to tribal and water-district partners, or to keep operating closer to break-even and trust continued foundation support.** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the Development team
   - Alternatives: Heavier savings protect partnership commitments but consume campaign capacity. Lighter savings free capacity for current work but expose the partnership commitments to funding shocks.
   - Scenario assumption: Substantial savings are built, because honoring multi-year partnership commitments to tribes and water districts is treated as non-negotiable.

## Audience lens — research

For scientists coming to the basin during the period, the experience is mixed. Guest scientists working on the long records find them protected and well-supported; the marmot study, the meadow phenology series, and the snowmelt-plant work all continue without interruption. Data systems are better than they have ever been. But the institution feels different. The summers are busier with translation partners, tribal staff, and water managers in addition to scientists. RMBL365 in winter is a hub of partnership meetings rather than a quiet field-station office. Scientists whose work connects to translation — pollinator service to ranchers, water-yield forecasting, demographic monitoring on co-managed lands — find new audiences and new collaborators. Scientists whose work is purely basic-research find themselves part of a smaller share of what RMBL does. Some welcome the change; some find it disorienting. The basin remains an exceptional place to do mountain science. It is no longer only that.

## Audience lens — institution

RMBL by 2040 is a recognizably different institution than it was in 2026. The staff has grown from a small technical core to a translation team numbering in the dozens, embedded across partner offices throughout the Mountain West. The Crested Butte facility, RMBL365, has been expanded twice and serves as the year-round hub. The summer field season in Gothic continues, with guest scientists doing the basic-research work that defines the basin's long records. But the institution's external face — the way agencies, tribes, and water districts know RMBL — is the translation work. Leadership spends substantial time on partnership management and on visits to peer stations adopting the model. The board carries an ongoing conversation about what basic-research identity means when translation is the largest single function. The institution's nonprofit independence is more visible than it has ever been: partners chose RMBL because its work is not constrained in the ways some federal-adjacent institutions have become.

## Audience lens — donor

Your contribution joins a substantial bet that the way Western land and water get managed is changing — and that basin science can be part of that change in a way no other institution is positioned to be. The bet depends on conditions outside RMBL's control: a public-attitude shift that the institution does not cause, foundation and federal funding that continue to support translation work at scale, and partner agencies and tribes that want continuous scientific input. If those conditions hold, what your gift is part of building is genuinely new — a model of place-based translation that other institutions across the world begin to adopt. The basin's long records remain protected. The marmot study passes its 75th year. The meadow phenology series approaches 65 years. And the question RMBL is carrying — what does it mean for a basic-research institution to do translation at this scale — becomes part of how Western science is done.

## Overlay robustness

**Central case.** Under gradual federal funding partial recovery, gradual AI evolution, and roughly RCP6.0 climate, the scenario plays out as described. Federal recovery supports translation partners' capacity to use basin evidence. AI tools mature enough to help with data products but do not replace the human relationships translation depends on. RCP6.0 climate produces enough disruption — fire years, low-runoff years, agricultural stress — to sustain the public-attitude shift this scenario depends on.

**Stress case — Public-attitude shift stalls.** If climate disruption produces backlash rather than recognition — politically polarized responses, withdrawal from co-management — the scenario's foundation collapses. RMBL would be left with a large translation team and partnership commitments that funders no longer want to underwrite. Leadership would face a hard contraction. The basin's long records would remain protected, but the institution would have to absorb a painful reduction in the translation function.

**Stress case — Federal contraction deepens.** If federal funding contracts further rather than partially recovers, foundation funders cannot fully substitute. The scenario plays out at a smaller scale — translation team in the mid-teens rather than the dozens, partnerships concentrated closer to the basin. The institutional identity tension is smaller because the institutional change is smaller. The basin's long records are still protected; RMBL365 is still expanded; the model is real but more modest.

**Stress case — Climate stress accelerates beyond RCP6.0.** Faster climate change makes adaptive co-management more urgent and the translation function more central. Foundation and federal funding for the work expand further. RMBL is asked to do more than its staff can sustain. Leadership faces choices about how quickly to grow without breaking the institution. Some long-record work is squeezed as translation demands intensify, and the basic-research community's concerns get louder.

## Plausibility caveats

This scenario sits in the upside tail. It depends on several favorable conditions stacking. First, a sustained public-attitude shift toward valuing environmental research as routine input to land and water decisions — not driven by a single political moment, but by a decade of compounding climate stress. Second, subnational policy reform: states, tribes, and regional compacts adopting adaptive co-management frameworks. The scenario does not assume federal regulatory reform of the Clean Air Act variety, which remains politically implausible. Third, sustained foundation and federal funding for translation work at scales not seen in recent decades. Fourth, RMBL successfully scaling partnership infrastructure without losing the basic-research community that brings guest scientists. Fifth, the basin's identity as a basic-science place remaining intact even as translation becomes institutionally dominant.

The characteristic failure mode for this scenario is institutional drift. RMBL becomes so good at translation that it stops being recognizable as a basic-research institution to its guest-scientist community. Guest scientists go elsewhere; the long records lose their connection to active basic-research questions; the translation function eventually has nothing distinctive to translate. Leadership in this scenario must hold both functions simultaneously, and the scenario assumes they succeed at a task that is genuinely hard.

Structural blind spots: the scenario assumes translation work scales linearly when in practice partnership work has step changes that are hard to predict. It also assumes tribal and water-district partners want sustained relationships with RMBL specifically, when in practice they may prefer to build their own scientific capacity rather than depend on an outside institution. And it underspecifies how the basic-research community's concerns get addressed beyond ongoing conversation.

## Looking further out: undefined–undefined

*Speculative. Lower resolution than the primary horizon.*

Looking past 2040, the scenario's further trajectory depends on whether the translation model survives the next political and climate cycle. If adaptive co-management holds and deepens, RMBL by the late 2040s is a continental institution with translation staff across the Mountain West and partnerships extending to peer stations in the Andes, the Cascades, and beyond. The marmot study moves through its 85-year mark and continues toward its 2063 centennial. The meadow phenology series passes 70 years. The basic-research community has either reconciled itself to the translation function or partially relocated to other stations; this resolution is genuinely uncertain. If adaptive co-management retreats — through political backlash, funding contraction, or institutional fatigue — RMBL faces a contraction painful in proportion to the scale it grew to. The basin remains. The records remain. What RMBL is in 2050 is a question this scenario opens rather than answers.
