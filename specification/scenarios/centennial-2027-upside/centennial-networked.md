# Centennial Networked Mountain Science

*A Future Scenario in the `centennial-2027-upside` set. Target magnitude, innovation-leaning. v1.0 under [Future Scenarios Framework v0.15](../../Future_scenarios_framework.md).*

---

## Synopsis

This scenario describes an upside-tail future in which RMBL becomes the institutional anchor of a continental network of mountain field stations. It depends on a public shift RMBL does not cause: federal and foundation funders decide that place-based, long-term mountain science is essential climate-adaptation infrastructure, and they fund it at a scale not seen in decades. RMBL raises substantially more than its realistic campaign ceiling and uses that capital to anchor shared instruments, shared graduate cohorts, and shared data platforms across stations from the Andes to the Cascades. The basin's own long records — marmots, meadows, snowmelt — are protected and deepened. What's forgone is institutional simplicity: RMBL takes on convening responsibilities that stretch a small staff and that could, if mismanaged, pull attention away from the basin itself. The invitation to donors is to be part of building a hemispheric scientific commons whose center of gravity is still Gothic.

### Campaign deliverables

- **primary.** Build the coordination capacity at RMBL to anchor a network of peer mountain field stations across the Americas. This includes hiring a small program team based at RMBL365, building the shared data platforms and protocol library that let stations work to common standards, and creating the legal and financial scaffolding for cross-border partnerships. This is a distinctive commitment of this scenario.
- **primary.** Grow RMBL's in-house data science and geospatial team substantially beyond its 2026 size. Network-scale comparative analysis depends on RMBL building and operating the data systems that let basin records talk to records from other mountains. This is catalytic capacity that guest scientists across the network would draw on.
- **primary.** Renovate RMBL365 to serve as the year-round convening hub for the network — workshop space, visiting-researcher offices, a synthesis room where cross-station working groups meet for weeks at a time. The building becomes the network's working address, not only RMBL's town presence.
- **secondary.** Protect and extend the basin's longest records — the marmot demographic study, the meadow phenology series, the snowmelt plant work — through investments in archives, observer succession arrangements, and the data infrastructure that makes a long record useful to comparative analysis.
- **secondary.** Extend the East River and SAIL sensing footprint into a permanent integrated observatory — atmosphere, snowpack, hydrology, soil, biology measured together on the same ground. This is the basin's distinctive contribution to the network: depth other stations can compare against.
- **supporting.** Build the institutional reserves and diversified-funding capacity that let RMBL hold its independence and its anchor role through funding-cycle shocks. The network is only as durable as its anchor.

### Frontier support strategies

- **F.cont.1.** The campaign protects the basin's long records as the depth contribution to the network. Archival systems, observer-succession arrangements with guest scientists, and data infrastructure ensure the marmot, phenology, and snowmelt records remain usable as comparative baselines for shorter records at peer stations.
- **F.innov.1.** The integrated basin observatory — atmosphere through bedrock — becomes the reference site against which other mountain stations calibrate their own integrated measurements. RMBL's geospatial and data staff maintain the systems that let guest scientists work across all the basin's measurement layers at once.
- **F.innov.3.** RMBL's growing data team builds the AI-assisted tools that comb a century of basin records, then extends those tools so they work across the network's records as well. This is the frontier where in-house capacity is most clearly load-bearing: the synthesis depends on systems RMBL builds and runs.
- **F.both.3.** This is the frontier the scenario is organized around. The campaign builds the coordination team, the shared protocols, the rotating graduate cohort program, and the convening space at RMBL365 that turn a loose set of friendly stations into a working network with shared instruments, shared data, and shared questions.

### Forgone

Even with a campaign well above the realistic ceiling, this scenario does not fund everything. Several frontiers get little direct support: pollinator network science continues mainly through guest scientists working on their own grants, without dedicated new infrastructure. Community science partnerships at the scale envisioned in other scenarios — deep co-production with tribal nations, water districts, and ranching communities — are scaled back to what RMBL365's convening function can carry, because the network role absorbs the coordination capacity that would otherwise go there. Mountain water security translation to local decision-makers is real but secondary; the network's audience is primarily the scientific community and federal agencies, not Gunnison County water managers. The scenario also forgoes the simpler institutional identity of a place-based station that just is itself. RMBL becomes a node in something larger, and that changes what working there feels like.

### Seeds in the present

The seeds are already visible. The East River watershed testbed and the SAIL atmospheric campaign that ran from 2021 onward demonstrated that the basin can host integrated measurement at a scale beyond any single research group. The Knowledge Commons itself — a product of the recent pattern where outside grants build RMBL's own tools — shows that RMBL can operate the kind of data infrastructure a network would need. The 2025 acquisition of RMBL365 gave the institution a year-round convening venue in Crested Butte. The basin's long records (marmots since 1963, meadow phenology since the mid-1970s) are the comparative depth other mountain stations lack. Cross-mountain comparative work already appears in publications. Planning conversations across the Americas — Niwot, Andrews, Sierra Nevada sites, partners in the Andes and Mexican highlands — have been circling network coordination for years without an institution willing to anchor it.

## Setting

This scenario assumes a shift RMBL does not cause but is well-placed to operate inside. Beginning around 2027, public and foundation funders decide that place-based, long-term mountain science is essential climate-adaptation infrastructure. The shift could come several ways — a federal mountain-systems initiative, an expansion of long-term ecological research programs, state climate funds, or a major foundation moving into inter-station coordination. In this scenario, several of those things happen at once.

RMBL's Centennial Campaign comes in well above what the institution could realistically have expected. The board uses that capacity to make a bet other stations are not positioned to make: RMBL takes on the role of anchor for a continental network of mountain field stations across the Americas. The basin remains the basin. Gothic stays small. The marmot study, the meadow phenology work, the snowmelt records all continue under the guest scientists who carry them. What changes is RMBL's role in the broader scientific landscape.

The coordination work happens mostly at RMBL365, renovated for the purpose, with a small program team and a substantially expanded data and geospatial staff. Stations from Niwot to the Andes adopt shared protocols, send graduate cohorts that rotate across sites, and contribute records to synthesis platforms RMBL builds and runs. The basin's depth — a half-century of phenology, six and a half decades of marmot demography by 2028 — becomes the comparative reference the network uses to read shorter records at younger stations.

The tension that runs through the scenario is whether a small place-based institution can anchor something hemispheric without losing what made it worth anchoring around.

## The arc — three phases across the primary horizon

### Phase 1: Anchor commitment (2027–2030)

The campaign closes well above the realistic ceiling in 2028, RMBL's centennial year. By then it is clear that federal interest in place-based mountain science has shifted. A coordinating planning grant from a major foundation, combined with two federal program awards, lets RMBL formally take on the anchor role for a network of mountain field stations across the Americas. Twelve stations sign initial coordination agreements by 2029. The first task is hiring: a small program team (three people) based at RMBL365, two additional data scientists and a cyberinfrastructure engineer added to the existing technical staff, and a network coordinator who splits time between Gothic and partner sites. Renovation of RMBL365 begins in 2028 and finishes in 2030, adding a synthesis workspace, visiting-researcher offices, and a small wet lab. The basin's long records get their first round of investment: archive digitization for the marmot study (then in its 66th year), expanded protocols for phenology observer succession, sensor extensions in the East River corridor. Some inside the institution worry the network commitments are moving too fast. The board's view, articulated in 2029, is that the funding window is real but narrow.

### Phase 2: Network operations (2031–2035)

The network becomes operational. Shared protocols for snow, phenology, and demographic monitoring are adopted across all member stations by 2032. The first rotating graduate cohort — twelve students moving across four stations over their PhDs — begins in 2031, with RMBL hosting one rotation each summer. Cross-border partnerships with stations in Mexico and the Andes are working by 2033, supported by a federal-international funding mechanism that takes three years of advocacy to establish. The data and geospatial team at RMBL, now eight people, builds the synthesis platforms the network uses; AI-assisted analysis of a century of basin records, extended to comparable records at peer stations, produces a first cross-station synthesis paper in 2034. The basin's marmot study passes its 75-year mark in 2038, and the planning around that milestone — observer succession, archive completion, methodological continuity — becomes a model for how other stations handle their own long records. Tension within RMBL is real. Staff working on the basin sometimes feel the network has crowded their attention. Leadership rebalances twice during this phase, formalizing protections for basin-only work.

### Phase 3: Comparative depth at scale (2036–2040)

By the late 2030s the network's distinctive product is mature: comparative climate-adaptation science across mountain systems of the Americas, grounded in long-term records, integrated measurements, and standardized synthesis. The basin's contribution is depth — the marmot study at 75 in 2038, the phenology series past 60 years, the integrated observatory in its second decade. Guest scientists at the basin work routinely with comparative data from other stations; basin findings are read in real comparative context. RMBL365 in Crested Butte hosts cross-station working groups throughout the year, becoming the network's working address. Federal funding has cycled — a contraction in 2036–37 forces hard choices about which network investments to defer — and the diversified funding base built in the early phase carries RMBL through. The campaign's reserves prove their worth. By 2040, RMBL is recognizably the institution it was in 2026 — small, place-based, responsive to guest scientists — and also something new: the institutional center of a hemispheric scientific commons whose findings are reshaping how mountain ecology is done.

## Lines of inquiry

Several lines of work mature through this period. Comparative phenology across mountain systems becomes possible at a level it never has been: the basin's half-century-plus record, read against shorter but well-resolved records from a dozen other stations, lets researchers separate what is global from what is regional from what is basin-specific. The marmot work, passing its 75-year mark in 2038, becomes the comparative reference for other mountain mammal studies across the network — including ones that started decades later and gain interpretive depth by comparison. Integrated atmosphere-to-bedrock measurement, pioneered in the East River corridor, becomes a shared method across the network, with the basin remaining the reference site where the longest integrated records live. AI-assisted synthesis of long records, with RMBL's data team building the tools, opens up findings that previous methods could not extract — both in the basin's own archives and across comparable records at other stations. What does not mature as fully in this scenario: deep community co-production work, in-depth water management translation in the Gunnison Basin specifically, and pollinator network science beyond what guest scientists carry on their own. The network role absorbs coordination capacity that would otherwise go to these.

## Moments of choice within the primary horizon

1. **2027 — Whether to formally pursue the anchor role for a continental mountain field-station network during the campaign, or treat cross-mountain partnerships as one priority among several.** *Shared inflection: `cross-mountain-network-form`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and board
   - Alternatives: RMBL could have stayed at the partnership level — friendly with peer stations, occasionally collaborating, but not taking on coordination responsibility. Other institutions (a federal lab, a major university consortium) might have anchored instead. RMBL could also have used the upside fundraising to deepen the basin in place without taking on hemispheric commitments.
   - Scenario assumption: Leadership commits to the anchor role, betting that the funding window is real and that no peer institution combines RMBL's nonprofit independence, place-based depth, and operational lightness in a way that lets it serve as a credible center.

2. **2028 — What functions to build into the RMBL365 renovation.** *Shared inflection: `rmbl365-renovation-scope`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership, RMBL365 planning team
   - Alternatives: RMBL365 could have been renovated primarily as community engagement and housing space; or as laboratory and staging space for basin science; or as the network convening hub.
   - Scenario assumption: The renovation prioritizes network convening functions — synthesis workspace, visiting-researcher offices, working-group meeting space — with community functions preserved but not expanded. This is consistent with the scenario's anchor commitment.

3. **2029 — How much network coordination capacity to build in-house at RMBL versus distribute across partner stations.** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the program team
   - Alternatives: A more distributed model would have spread coordination roles across multiple stations, reducing the strain on RMBL's small staff. A more centralized model concentrates capacity at RMBL but creates institutional dependence and risks pulling attention from basin science.
   - Scenario assumption: RMBL builds a small but real in-house coordination team (three program staff plus expanded data staff) while protecting a clear boundary: basin science staff are not network staff. The boundary holds imperfectly through the horizon.

4. **2031 — Whether to launch the rotating graduate cohort program, which requires deep institutional buy-in from all member stations.** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and partner station directors
   - Alternatives: A lighter-touch model — shared protocols and data platforms, but no rotating cohort — would have been less institutionally demanding. A formal joint graduate program would have been more demanding but also more durable.
   - Scenario assumption: Partner stations commit to the rotating cohort, with RMBL hosting one rotation each summer. The program becomes the most visible expression of the network's culture and a key recruiting tool for all member stations.

5. **2034 — Whether and how to rebalance institutional attention as staff working on the basin report that the network is crowding their work.** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the board
   - Alternatives: Continue as is, accepting tension as a cost of the anchor role. Scale back network commitments. Formalize protections for basin-only work and expand staff capacity to absorb the tension.
   - Scenario assumption: Leadership formalizes protections for basin work — guaranteed staff time, separate reporting lines, an explicit charter for what the network does and does not ask of basin staff. The tension softens but does not disappear.

6. **2037 — How to respond to a federal funding contraction that threatens several network programs.** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and finance team
   - Alternatives: Cut network operations to protect basin science. Cut basin investments to protect network commitments. Use campaign reserves to bridge the contraction while restructuring.
   - Scenario assumption: Reserves built early in the campaign are used to bridge the contraction. Some network programs are deferred but none are cancelled. The basin's long records are protected. The episode reinforces why diversified funding and reserves were worth building.

## Audience lens — research

A scientist working in mountain ecology during this period has access to something that did not exist before. Long records at the basin — marmots, phenology, snowmelt — sit alongside shorter but well-resolved records at a dozen other stations across the Americas, all available through shared data platforms RMBL builds. Standardized protocols mean a finding at the basin can be tested at peer sites without renegotiating methods. A rotating graduate cohort lets early-career researchers learn multiple mountain systems before deciding where their work belongs. Integrated atmosphere-to-bedrock measurement is available at the basin as a deep reference, with comparable but less complete measurement at partner stations. AI-assisted synthesis tools, built and maintained by RMBL's data team, make a century of records analyzable at a scale prior generations could not approach. The basin itself remains what it was — responsive, small, focused on guest scientists' work — but its place in the comparative landscape is richer.

## Audience lens — institution

RMBL through this period is recognizably itself and also something new. Gothic stays small. The summer rhythm of guest scientists arriving, working, leaving continues. The marmot study, the phenology work, the East River instruments all run. What's different is the year-round center of gravity in Crested Butte, where the renovated RMBL365 hosts network working groups, visiting researchers from partner stations, and a small program team. The technical staff has roughly doubled by 2035, with new data scientists and a cyberinfrastructure engineer joining the existing geospatial team. The board's composition shifts to include directors of partner stations as advisory members. There is real institutional tension — staff working on the basin sometimes feel the network has crowded their work, and leadership rebalances twice to protect basin-only capacity. By 2040 RMBL is the anchor of something larger than itself, and the staff who have stayed through the period understand what that has cost and what it has built.

## Audience lens — donor

A contribution to this scenario joins the building of something at hemispheric scale, anchored in a place that has been doing this work for a century. The basin remains itself: the marmot study, the meadow phenology series, the snowmelt records all continue. What your contribution is part of is the institutional capacity that lets RMBL serve as the center of a network of mountain field stations across the Americas — the data systems, the convening space at RMBL365, the small program team, the reserves that protect the institution's independence through funding shocks. This is an upside scenario; it depends on a public shift toward funding long-term mountain science at a scale not seen in decades. Without that shift, the network role does not materialize. With it, RMBL's contribution to mountain ecology in 2040 looks different in kind from what one station alone could offer. The invitation is to be part of that bet.

## Overlay robustness

**Central case.** Under gradual federal contraction, gradual AI evolution, and moderate climate change, this scenario does not function — the central bet requires federal and foundation expansion, not contraction. The scenario is explicitly an upside-tail future where the funding environment shifts substantially. If the central case actually holds, the scenario collapses into something closer to the central set's middle range: RMBL protects its records, grows its data team modestly, but does not take on the network anchor role. The campaign closes at the realistic ceiling, not above it.

**Stress case — Partial public expansion.** If federal funding expands for mountain science but foundations do not move in coordination, RMBL takes on a smaller network role. Coordination happens but at three or four partner stations rather than twelve. The rotating graduate cohort does not launch. RMBL365's renovation still happens but functions more as basin convening space than as network hub. The campaign comes in above the realistic ceiling but not as far above. The basin's long records still get strong investment; the hemispheric ambition is deferred.

**Stress case — Network partner failure.** If two or three key partner stations lose their own funding or close during the horizon, the network's value proposition weakens. RMBL absorbs some of the coordination strain but cannot replace lost partners. The network continues at reduced scope, and RMBL redirects some capacity back to basin work. The diversified funding base built in early phases proves essential to weathering partner instability.

**Stress case — Identity strain past the rebalancing point.** If the internal tension between network role and basin focus is not resolved by the 2034 rebalancing, the scenario gets worse, not better. Basin scientists leave. The marmot study loses observer succession in a key year. The board faces a harder choice in 2037 — wind down the network role, or accept that RMBL has become primarily a coordinating institution. Neither outcome is what the scenario intended. The plausibility of avoiding this is the scenario's loudest internal question.

## Plausibility caveats

This scenario is explicitly an upside-tail future. It depends on a stack of favorable conditions, and the loss of any one collapses it. First: federal commitment to place-based long-term ecological research has to grow substantially, whether through a new mountain-systems initiative, expanded long-term ecological research programs, or sustained reauthorization at increased magnitude. Second: at least one major foundation has to move into inter-station coordination as a funding priority. Third: peer mountain field stations across the Americas have to remain functional and willing to participate — political and funding instability at partner sites is real. Fourth: cross-border funding mechanisms have to exist or be built. Fifth, and most internally salient: RMBL's anchor role cannot consume so much capacity that basin science suffers. This is the scenario's characteristic failure mode. The 2034 rebalancing in the phase arc is the moment that question gets answered, and the scenario assumes it gets answered well. Structural blind spots: the scenario assumes the network's scientific value is large enough to justify the institutional strain, which is plausible but not certain. It assumes peer stations want RMBL as anchor rather than wanting distributed coordination, which is a real institutional preference question that planning conversations have not fully resolved. It assumes the basin's identity survives the role change, which is the bet the whole scenario rests on.

## Looking further out: undefined–undefined

*Speculative. Lower resolution than the primary horizon.*

Looking past 2040, the network's durability becomes the central question. If it holds, comparative mountain ecology of the Americas matures into a recognized field with its own training pathways, methods, and synthesis traditions, and RMBL's role as anchor becomes part of how the institution is understood for the rest of the century. The marmot study would pass its centennial in 2063, with comparative work at partner stations giving the milestone richer meaning than a single-site celebration could. The phenology series would approach 75 years in the early 2050s. If the network does not hold — if funding cycles back, partners drift, or the anchor role proves unsustainable — RMBL returns to being a place-based station with unusually strong data infrastructure and an expanded sense of what it could have been. Both futures are recognizable as RMBL. The next campaign, whenever it comes, will face the question of which of them the institution has become.
