# Centennial Accelerating Basin Change

*A Future Scenario in the `centennial-2027-downside` set. Floor magnitude, continuity-leaning. v1.0 under [Future Scenarios Framework v0.16](../../Future_scenarios_framework.md).*

---

## Synopsis

This is a downside scenario. It assumes climate change advances faster than central-case modeling assumed. By the mid-2030s, low-elevation snowpack in the basin collapses in three of five years instead of one in five. Subalpine fir dies back across slopes that had been stable. Some marmot colonies shift up-drainage; others fail. Phenology stacks fragment in ways the long records had no analog for. Fire seasons compromise summer fieldwork two years out of three. The campaign closes at the campaign floor, with operating conditions degraded by faster-than-modeled climate change. It reorients accordingly: the marmot study and the meadow phenology series are extended through the discontinuity, instruments are rebuilt at higher elevations where analog systems have shifted, and fire-resilient field operations become a core institutional concern. The scenario asks donors and the institution to back the records of transformation — to document what was becoming what — rather than preserve a steady-state that no longer exists.

### Campaign deliverables

- **primary.** Extend the long demographic and phenology records through the discontinuity. This means stewarding the marmot study (in its 75th year in 2038) and the meadow phenology series (approaching its 65th year) so they keep observing as the systems they track shift. The campaign protects protocols, observer-succession arrangements with guest scientists, and the archival systems that hold the data, with primary share of the campaign directed here.
- **primary.** Build modest higher-elevation observing capacity. As marmot colonies shift up-drainage and meadow species track snow lines upward, RMBL needs sensors, plots, and access at elevations the existing infrastructure does not reach. This is a distinctive commitment: not new core facilities, but a focused expansion of where the basin's instruments and plots actually sit. Funded as a secondary priority within the constrained envelope.
- **secondary.** Fire-resilient field operations. With smoke and active fire compromising summer fieldwork in roughly two of every three years by the mid-2030s, the campaign funds smoke-aware scheduling tools, protective gear and air filtration at Gothic, contingency housing arrangements through RMBL365 in Crested Butte for evacuation seasons, and shoulder-season field capacity so work can proceed outside peak fire windows.
- **supporting.** Targeted growth in RMBL's data and geospatial team to handle the data products that come from documenting a system in transformation — repeat aerial imagery of dieback, sensor networks that span elevation gradients, archival pipelines that hold both the old records and the new shifted ones in legible relation. Modest in scale, consistent with a small-nonprofit technical core.
- **continuity_residual.** Basic stewardship of RMBL365 as a year-round operating base, including the housing and workshop capacity already in place. No expansion beyond what the constrained campaign supports; renovations deferred.

### Frontier support strategies

- **F.cont.1.** The campaign treats the marmot and phenology records as the most valuable assets RMBL holds — and as records whose value rises rather than falls when the systems they track stop being steady-state. Investment goes to protocol stewardship through the transition (how do you keep counting marmots when colonies move?), to archival systems that can hold the discontinuity legibly, and to observer-succession partnerships with the guest scientists who actually carry the records.
- **F.both.1.** This frontier is what the scenario is actually about. The campaign funds the higher-elevation infrastructure and sensor networks that let basin science follow systems as they shift, while protecting the lower-elevation baselines against which the shifts are measured. RMBL's data team supports guest scientists working on post-snowpack ecology with the geospatial products — elevation-banded phenology, dieback maps, snow-regime classifications — that make transformation studies tractable.

### Forgone

This scenario does not fund the broader atmosphere-to-bedrock integration work that a stable-climate, well-funded campaign would. It does not build a community-science partnership program at the scale the moment arguably calls for. It does not expand RMBL365 with new laboratory space or substantial renovation. It does not grow the in-house team enough to mount an ambitious AI-assisted synthesis effort across the full archive. It does not fund cross-mountain comparative networks. Several research lines active in the late 2020s — particular pollinator network studies tied to lower-elevation meadows, snowmelt experiments at sites that no longer reliably have snowpack — are paused, handed to peer institutions, or quietly retired. The campaign chooses depth in documenting transformation over breadth across the full frontier portfolio. Donors are asked to accept this narrowing as the honest consequence of the conditions, not a failure of ambition.

### Seeds in the present

The Surface Atmosphere Integrated Field Laboratory campaign (Feldman et al., 2023) established that the basin could host instrument-dense, multi-team science across the East River. Kerins et al. (2025) on snow, soil, and steepness driving diverging subsurface and streamflow trends already documents the kind of regime-shift signal this scenario assumes intensifies. Powers et al. (2022) on earlier snowmelt altering floral traits, and Prather et al. (2023) on current and lagged climate effects on phenology across taxa, are the leading edge of the fragmentation this scenario sees become dominant. The marmot record (Blumstein lineage, ongoing since 1963) and the meadow phenology series (Inouye and collaborators, ~1974) are the records the scenario protects. RMBL's existing data and geospatial team, the Knowledge Commons itself, the SAIL-era sensor and data infrastructure, and the RMBL365 building in Crested Butte are the current capacities the campaign builds from rather than replacing.

## Setting

This scenario assumes that climate change advances faster than the modeling of the late 2020s expected. By the early 2030s, basin scientists begin to recognize that several thresholds are being crossed at once. Low-elevation snowpack, modeled to collapse in one of every five winters, collapses in three of five. Subalpine fir dies back across slopes that field crews had considered stable. Marmot colonies on the lower edges of their range fail demographically; some move up-drainage to ground where they have not been recorded. The phenology stacks that the meadow series tracked through the 2010s — orderly cascades of bloom and emergence — fragment into asynchronies the long records had no analog for. Fire seasons compromise summer fieldwork in roughly two of every three years by the mid-2030s, with smoke shutting down access even in years when the basin itself does not burn.

The campaign opens in this context. Donors who care about basin science see, as the scientists do, that the systems being studied are no longer the systems the records were designed to track. The campaign closes at the campaign floor, with operating conditions degraded by faster-than-modeled climate change. The institution does not collapse. It reorients. It chooses to document the transformation at the resolution only a place with sixty- and seventy-year records can provide. It rebuilds some infrastructure at higher elevations, hardens summer operations against fire, and protects the long records as they pass through the discontinuity. What it does not do is pretend the basin of 2026 is still the basin being studied.

## The arc — three phases across the primary horizon

### Phase 1: Recognition (2026–2030)

The early campaign years run before the full pattern is obvious. The 2027 and 2029 winters produce snowpack near the low end of recent ranges, but within historical variability. The 2030 fire season closes access to several research sites for parts of July and August. Researchers note the disruption but most assume normal years will return. The campaign opens with priorities set against a central-case climate that already feels generous. RMBL leadership and the board commit early to protecting the marmot study and the meadow phenology series, regardless of how operating conditions evolve. The data team begins quietly building elevation-banded versions of standard geospatial products in anticipation that range shifts will need to be mapped at finer resolution. RMBL365 hosts an expanded shoulder-season program as guest scientists begin compressing field work into May and September windows around the fire season. By 2030, the campaign has raised enough to lock in the long-record protections; the rest of the deliverables are paced carefully against an uncertain operating environment.

### Phase 2: Reorientation (2031–2035)

Three of the five winters between 2031 and 2035 produce snowpack collapses at lower elevations. Subalpine fir dieback becomes visible from the road into Gothic. In 2033, the marmot study records its first complete failure of a colony that had been monitored continuously since the 1970s; another colony, three hundred meters higher, shows up in the records for the first time. The meadow phenology series begins logging events that arrive in orders the protocol's data structures do not cleanly handle. Researchers and the data team revise codebooks to capture the fragmentation rather than smooth it out. The campaign uses this phase to deploy higher-elevation sensor capacity and to fund the observer-succession arrangements that keep the long records intact as their subject systems move. The fire seasons of 2032 and 2034 are bad enough that RMBL convenes a working group on field-season operations; the resulting investments — air filtration, smoke-aware scheduling, evacuation contingency through RMBL365 — are funded out of the campaign's secondary priorities. Several research lines tied to lower-elevation snowmelt experiments are paused; their PIs and RMBL leadership decide together which can transition to new sites and which are retired.

### Phase 3: Documentation (2036–2040)

By the late 2030s, the basin's scientific identity has shifted. RMBL is no longer primarily a place where long-term records describe a stable mountain system; it is a place where long-term records describe what a mountain system looks like as it transforms. The marmot study passes its 75-year mark in 2038, now spanning colonies at elevations the original protocol did not anticipate; published work from the period draws explicit contrasts between the demographic patterns of the 1970s–2000s and those emerging after 2030. The meadow phenology series, approaching its 65th year, becomes a reference dataset internationally for how phenological stacks fragment under accelerated warming. The data team's elevation-banded products are used by researchers across the western mountains. The campaign closes near its floor. Several deliverables that looked plausible in 2027 were narrowed or dropped along the way. What was protected, was protected. RMBL ends the period smaller in scope than the central-case scenarios imagined, but with the records intact and a new kind of scientific work — documentation of transformation at sustained resolution — at its center.

## Lines of inquiry

The lines of inquiry that mature in this period are about transformation rather than baseline. The marmot study extends into colony shifts, partial failures, and the demographic consequences of moving into ground the species has not previously occupied at this density. The meadow phenology series matures into a record of how orderly phenological stacks fragment, with the late-2030s publications becoming the empirical reference for asynchrony research globally. Snowmelt-driven plant work captures the years the snowpack regime broke and becomes a record of regime transition rather than gradient response. Higher-elevation observing matures as a working capacity, with elevation-banded data products becoming standard outputs from the RMBL data team. Fire ecology, not historically a basin specialty, becomes a necessary lens as smoke and burn footprints reshape what fieldwork is possible. What does not mature: the broader atmosphere-to-bedrock integration agenda that SAIL set up, which proceeds elsewhere with thinner basin involvement; the AI-assisted retrospective synthesis program that a better-resourced campaign would have launched; the community-science partnership work that the moment arguably calls for. Pollinator network studies tied to lower meadows shrink or migrate; the basin's pollination tradition continues but with a narrower geographic footprint than in the 2010s. The lines that mature are the ones aligned with the bet on documenting change.

## Moments of choice within the primary horizon

1. **2028 — Whether to lock in primary campaign commitment to extending the marmot and meadow phenology records through whatever operating conditions emerge, before the climate picture is clear.** *Shared inflection: `long-record-stewardship-transfer`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and board, in consultation with the lead investigators of the long records
   - Alternatives: Hold long-record commitments at current levels and preserve campaign flexibility for emerging frontiers; or commit early to the records as the campaign's anchor and accept reduced flexibility downstream.
   - Scenario assumption: Early commitment to the records as anchor, accepting reduced flexibility.

2. **2031 — Whether to invest in higher-elevation observing capacity in advance of confirmed range shifts, or wait for more evidence before reallocating limited campaign capital.** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the data and geospatial team
   - Alternatives: Wait until shifts are unambiguous, then build; or build in anticipation and risk the investment being premature.
   - Scenario assumption: Build in anticipation, on the judgment that observing the early years of range shift is more valuable than observing the late years.

3. **2033 — Which research lines tied to systems that no longer reliably exist (lower-elevation snowpack experiments, particular pollinator network sites) to pause, transition, or retire.** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership, guest scientist PIs of lower-elevation research lines, and the board
   - Alternatives: Try to sustain all active lines and dilute support across them; or make explicit retirement and transition decisions and concentrate support on what remains.
   - Scenario assumption: Explicit retirement and transition decisions, made jointly with the PIs involved.

4. **2034 — How much of the remaining campaign envelope to direct toward fire-resilient field operations as smoke compromises summer access in two of three years.** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL operations leadership and the board
   - Alternatives: Treat fire disruption as a periodic operating cost to absorb; or treat it as a structural change requiring dedicated infrastructure (filtration, scheduling tools, RMBL365 evacuation capacity).
   - Scenario assumption: Treat it as structural and fund the operational investments out of the campaign's secondary priorities.

5. **2036 — Whether to grow the data and geospatial team modestly to handle the products that transformation documentation requires, or hold the team at current size and reduce other commitments to fund essential operations.** *Shared inflection: `reserves-versus-staffing-tradeoff`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the board
   - Alternatives: Hold the team at current size and live with what can be produced; or grow it modestly at the cost of other deliverables.
   - Scenario assumption: Modest growth in the data team, at the cost of further reductions in renovation and partnership work.

6. **2038 — How to communicate publicly about a basin science program whose subject systems have transformed — whether to emphasize loss, transformation, or continuity-through-change.** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership, lead investigators, and external collaborators
   - Alternatives: A loss framing that may resonate with concerned audiences but risks fatigue; a transformation framing that centers the science of change; or a continuity framing that risks understating the discontinuity.
   - Scenario assumption: Lead with transformation: the science of documenting a basin becoming a different basin.

## Audience lens — research

For scientists working on the long records and on post-snowpack ecology, this period is demanding but unusually consequential. The marmot study extends through colony shifts and partial failures, generating data that no other long mammal record in North America will hold. The meadow phenology series logs the fragmentation of phenological stacks as it happens, with the late-2030s data becoming a reference internationally for asynchrony research. Researchers willing to work at higher elevations, in compressed field seasons, and with the smoke and access constraints fire imposes, find RMBL a place where transformation can be observed at resolution unavailable elsewhere. Researchers whose work depended on stable lower-elevation systems face harder choices — some transition to new sites, some hand off lines to peers, some retire projects. The basin is a smaller place for some kinds of work and a more important place for others.

## Audience lens — institution

RMBL through this period is constrained, focused, and clear-eyed about what it is doing. The staff is roughly its current size, with modest growth in the data team and no growth in administrative or partnership functions. Summer field operations are reorganized around fire risk; shoulder-season work expands through RMBL365 in Crested Butte. The institutional identity shifts: RMBL is no longer mainly a host to a stable program of long-term ecological observation in a stable mountain system, but a host to long records of a mountain system transforming. The mood is sober and purposeful rather than triumphant. The institution carries the records through the discontinuity. It does fewer things than the central-case scenarios imagined. The things it does, it does because they could not be done anywhere else.

## Audience lens — donor

Your contribution joins an effort to keep the basin's long records observing as the basin itself transforms. The marmot study and the meadow phenology series — among the longest continuous records of their kind in North American mountain systems — pass through a period when the systems they track stop being what they were. A contribution is part of the conditions that let those records continue: protocols that adapt, data systems that hold the discontinuity legibly, instruments that move with the species, field operations that work around fire and smoke. It is also part of accepting that a basin science responsive to faster-than-expected climate change must do fewer things, more carefully, than a basin science under steady conditions could. What donors are part of building is a record of transformation at a resolution only this place can produce.

## Overlay robustness

**Central case.** The scenario already departs downward from the central case on the climate axis. Federal contraction is assumed to continue at roughly its current pace. AI tools evolve at the pace the central case projects and are useful for the data team's elevation-banded products and for managing the larger imagery archives transformation documentation produces, but they do not unlock a synthesis program at the scale a better-resourced campaign would. The campaign closes at its floor. The institution remains an institution: smaller in scope than the central case imagined, but functioning, with its core records intact.

**Stress case — Compounded federal contraction.** If federal science funding contracts more sharply than the central case assumes — particularly affecting the guest scientists who carry the marmot and phenology records — the campaign's commitment to the long records becomes harder to honor. Observer succession arrangements that depend on graduate-student support at peer institutions become fragile. RMBL would have to consider direct stewardship of more of the data collection than the operating model historically supports, or accept gaps in the records. The scenario's central bet — that the records of transformation become highly valuable — still holds, but the records themselves are harder to keep continuous.

**Stress case — Catastrophic fire in the immediate basin.** If a major fire burns through long-term monitoring plots or close to the Gothic site, the scenario's structure does not change but its tone does. Some records lose physical reference points. Insurance and rebuild costs draw down what little campaign flexibility remained. RMBL365's role as evacuation and operating base becomes essential rather than supplementary. The transformation framing becomes more urgent: the basin scientists are documenting are now a basin that has burned, and the records become records of recovery as well as of climate-driven change.

**Stress case — Donor fatigue with climate science.** If philanthropic appetite for climate-related environmental science declines through the period — as it has at times historically in response to political and economic conditions — the campaign closes below the floor rather than at it. In this case the higher-elevation observing investments are scaled back, the fire-operations investments are partially funded, and the data team does not grow. The long records are still protected. Everything else is further narrowed.

## Plausibility caveats

This scenario assumes climate change advances meaningfully faster than the central-case modeling of the late 2020s expected — specifically, that low-elevation snowpack collapses become routine by the mid-2030s rather than late-2040s, and that fire seasons compromise summer fieldwork in two of three years rather than one in three. This is a downside-tail climate assumption. Central-case climate trajectories would produce a less acute version of this scenario; if climate actually evolves at central-case pace, the campaign's choices look premature and the higher-elevation investments may be made before the systems have fully shifted. The scenario also assumes that the long records can in fact be extended through the discontinuity — that observer-succession arrangements hold, that protocols can adapt without losing comparability to the historical data, and that the species the records track are still present in the basin in some form. If marmot populations collapse basin-wide rather than shift up-drainage, the marmot study ends rather than transforms. The scenario assumes RMBL's institutional independence and operating model survive the period intact; federal contraction is assumed at central-case pace, not at the sharper rate the institutional-stress dominant scenarios assume. The characteristic failure mode of this scenario's bet is timing: if the campaign reorients toward transformation documentation before the transformation is clearly underway, the institution looks alarmist; if it reorients too late, the early years of the shift are missed. A structural blind spot: the scenario does not seriously model what happens if RMBL's small technical staff cannot in fact handle the increased data and operational complexity that transformation documentation imposes, particularly under the fire-disrupted operating conditions assumed. Staff burnout, succession of key technical roles, and the cumulative wear of working under degraded conditions are real risks the scenario gestures at but does not resolve.

## Looking further out: undefined–undefined

*Speculative. Lower resolution than the primary horizon.*

Looking past 2040, this scenario points toward a basin science whose value depends on the records continuing through whatever comes next. If the climate trajectory of the 2030s persists into the 2040s, the marmot study and the meadow phenology series become some of the only multi-decadal records of how mountain systems traverse the threshold from one regime to another. Their scientific use shifts further from documentation of pattern to reference for what mountain transformation looks like at sustained resolution. RMBL itself, if the institution carries through the campaign period intact, is positioned to be a place where the science of the post-snowpack mountain matures. The institution at the 2040s is smaller in scope than the 2020s imagined it might be, but it holds something — the records of how the basin became what it was becoming — that almost no other place will hold. The further future is hazier; the bet of this scenario is that this kind of record matters more, not less, as the conditions that produced steady-state mountain ecology recede further into history.
