# Centennial Politicized Science

*A Future Scenario in the `centennial-2027-downside` set. Floor magnitude, continuity-leaning. v1.0 under [Future Scenarios Framework v0.16](../../Future_scenarios_framework.md).*

---

## Synopsis

This scenario imagines a decade in which public attitudes shift durably against environmental science. Across multiple administrations, climate denial dominates federal and state politics. Agencies that long partnered with basin researchers — USGS, the Forest Service climate programs, EPA, NOAA — face program cuts, scientist relocations, and suppression of findings. Foundation interest in environmental science cools. The campaign closes below the realistic floor, with operating conditions further strained by political pressure. RMBL leans into what remains durable. The marmot demographic study and the meadow phenology series continue. Partnerships with tribal natural-resources offices deepen, because tribal sovereignty does not depend on federal continuity. Quiet work with the Upper Gunnison River Water Conservancy District holds. The translation function narrows but survives. The scenario asks donors and the institution to defend RMBL's independence as the working condition that keeps unrestricted basin science possible. It is a smaller RMBL, more clearly itself.

### Campaign deliverables

- **primary.** Protect the longest basin records — the marmot demographic study above Gothic and the meadow phenology series — through the period. This means stable funding for plot infrastructure, archival systems, and the small technical staff who steward protocols and data as guest scientists carry the fieldwork forward. The records are the asset political conditions cannot reach.
- **primary.** Build savings and governance capacity that buffer RMBL against funding shocks and protect its nonprofit independence. This is the working condition that lets guest scientists pursue questions without restrictions on direction or framing. Reserves are sized to outlast a multi-year contraction in federal and foundation support, not just a single bad year.
- **secondary.** Deepen partnerships with tribal nation natural-resources offices and with the Upper Gunnison River Water Conservancy District. These are the science-to-policy channels that remain viable when federal partnerships are disrupted. A small partnership-coordination role — well under a full endowed program — sustains the relationships across the period.
- **supporting.** Maintain RMBL's data scientists, GIS staff, and instrumentation technicians at roughly current scale, with modest investment in the data systems that hold the long records together. Growth ambitions from earlier plans are deferred. Continuity of the technical core matters more than expansion.
- **continuity_residual.** Keep RMBL365 operating year-round at reduced scope, prioritizing community engagement and partner meetings over new laboratory build-out. The in-town presence matters more in this period, not less, because it is where the durable partnerships meet.

### Frontier support strategies

- **F.cont.1.** The campaign treats the long records as the basin's most durable scientific asset and the one most clearly protected from political weather. Investment goes to plot infrastructure, protocol documentation, the archives, and the small staff who keep the data systems coherent across observer transitions. Guest scientists carry the fieldwork. The marmot study passes its 75-year mark in 2038 inside this scenario, and the phenology series approaches its 65th year, both protected by deliberate institutional choice rather than abundance.
- **F.innov.5.** The campaign invests in RMBL's nonprofit independence as a working condition for inquiry. Operating reserves, governance capacity, and a less federally-dependent funding base let guest scientists work without restrictions on direction or framing that some federally-adjacent institutions face. RMBL365 holds the community-facing dimension. The frontier is as much governance as research, but it is what makes the rest possible during a politically constrained decade.

### Forgone

The campaign does not fund the integrated atmosphere-to-bedrock observatory many basin scientists hoped to build on what SAIL began. That work continues at smaller scale through whatever federal funding remains, but RMBL does not anchor it. The retrospective AI synthesis program — using new tools to mine a century of basin records — is not launched at scale; the data scientists who would have led it are not hired. Cross-mountain partnerships with peer field stations are not formalized into the program some had imagined. New laboratory build-out at RMBL365 is deferred. The translation function does not grow into a multi-person team serving a wide range of stakeholders; it consolidates into a narrow set of partnerships chosen because they remain viable, not because they are the broadest reach. Several research lines active in the 2020s migrate to peer institutions or pause.

### Seeds in the present

The seeds are visible in 2026. The marmot demographic record passed its 60th year in 2023 and is carried by guest scientists with deep continuity. The meadow phenology series, anchored in the 1970s, is at its half-century. The East River watershed work and the SAIL campaign matured a generation of integrated mountain science that this scenario cannot fully sustain but can partially shelter. RMBL's small technical core — geospatial data scientist, GIS manager, instrumentation staff — already maintains the data systems and instruments that guest scientists depend on. The Knowledge Commons itself is a product of the recent pattern where outside grants build RMBL's tools, which then attract more grants. RMBL365, purchased in 2025, is the in-town platform for the community and partner work. Quiet existing relationships with the Upper Gunnison River Water Conservancy District and with tribal natural-resources offices are the channels the scenario leans on.

## Setting

This scenario takes as its starting condition a durable shift in public attitudes against environmental science. The shift is not a single election but a pattern across administrations and across states. Federal agencies relevant to basin work — USGS, the Forest Service climate programs, EPA, NOAA — face sustained hostility. Programs are cut. Scientists are relocated or leave. Findings are suppressed or rewritten. Agency partnerships that basin researchers had relied on for decades become unreliable or disappear.

Foundation appetite for environmental science contracts in parallel. Donors who once gave generously to climate work redirect attention to causes that feel more politically defensible to their boards. The Centennial Campaign closes below the realistic floor. Operating conditions are further strained by the political pressure on the work itself.

RMBL does not collapse. It leans into what remains durable. The long records on the slopes above Gothic and in the meadows continue, because they are protocol-bound, modestly funded, and protected by their own scientific weight. The partnerships that survive are the ones whose authority does not depend on federal continuity. Tribal nation natural-resources offices, operating from sovereignty of their own, become one of the few channels through which basin science still reaches policy. The Upper Gunnison River Water Conservancy District continues quietly. RMBL's independence — its nonprofit status, its responsiveness to guest scientists rather than federal program officers — becomes a more important asset than it had ever been. The scenario tests what that independence is actually for.

## The arc — three phases across the primary horizon

### Phase 1: Narrowing conditions, deliberate choices (2026–2030)

The early phase is defined by recognition and triage. By 2027 it becomes clear that federal partnerships once treated as durable cannot be relied on. Several long-standing agency collaborations end or shrink. Some basin scientists lose grants mid-cycle. RMBL leadership and the board make a hard decision in 2028: the campaign will protect what is hardest to replace rather than spread thin. The marmot demographic study and the meadow phenology series are placed at the center. Operating reserves are built. The data team is held at current scale rather than grown. Plans for a larger integrated observatory are shelved. RMBL365 holds reduced winter hours and becomes the meeting place for the partnerships that remain. Conversations with tribal natural-resources offices deepen during this period, often initiated by tribal partners themselves seeking science venues outside federal channels. The Upper Gunnison River Water Conservancy District relationship continues. By 2030 a leaner shape is visible, chosen rather than fallen into.

### Phase 2: Independence as working condition (2031–2035)

The middle phase tests whether the choices hold. Federal contraction does not reverse. State-level pressure on environmental research intensifies in some Western states. Some guest scientists from public universities facing direct restrictions begin shifting parts of their programs to the basin specifically because RMBL's nonprofit posture is unrestricted. By 2033 this is a noticeable pattern. The marmot record continues; a generational observer transition is managed carefully through guest-scientist partnerships, with RMBL's archives and protocols holding the continuity. The phenology series continues. Tribal natural-resources partnerships produce a small number of co-authored studies that route into tribal water and land decisions — quieter work than the press-cycle science of the 2020s, but durable. The Conservancy District relationship continues to deliver basin-scale water analysis into local decisions. The translation function, narrowed to two or three durable channels, is doing real work. RMBL365 becomes recognized locally as the place where this kind of partnership actually happens.

### Phase 3: Quieter, more clearly itself (2036–2040)

The final phase is one of consolidation. The marmot study passes its 75-year mark in 2038 — a milestone met with a small gathering at Gothic rather than a press release. The phenology series approaches 65 years. By 2040 the basin still has its longest records intact, an outcome that looked uncertain in 2028. The political climate has not warmed, but RMBL has adapted to it. The institution is smaller than the 2025 plan imagined. The data team is the size it was in 2026 rather than the expanded team some had hoped for. But it functions. Guest scientists continue to come, drawn partly by the unrestricted posture and partly by the records. Tribal and water-district partnerships are now part of how RMBL describes itself, not adjuncts to it. The board, looking back, sees that protecting independence and the long records was the right wager. The scenario ends with RMBL quieter than it was in 2026, and more clearly itself.

## Lines of inquiry

The lines that mature are the ones the long records can carry without large new investment. The marmot work continues to deepen, with social-ecology and climate-vulnerability questions that the demographic record uniquely supports. The phenology series continues to ground basin work on shifting flowering and pollinator timing, and to feed into the small number of synthesis papers that can still find venues. Snowmelt-driven plant ecology continues at the meadow scale.

Lines that mature more partially: the integrated East River watershed science continues at smaller scale through whatever federal hydrology funding survives, but RMBL is not its primary institutional anchor in this period. Cross-mountain comparative work continues informally rather than through funded partnerships. AI-assisted synthesis of basin records happens at a small scale through individual guest scientists rather than a built program. Plant-pollinator network work continues through dedicated researchers but loses some of the broader collaborative scope it had in the 2020s.

Lines that pause or migrate elsewhere: large-scale instrumentation development, new sensing platforms, ambitious data-platform expansion. These wait for conditions that allow them. What the period does mature is a clearer institutional understanding of what basic-research continuity costs and what it protects.

## Moments of choice within the primary horizon

1. **2028 — Whether to protect the long records and institutional independence as the campaign's primary commitments, or to maintain a broader portfolio that spreads the constrained resources across more frontiers** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and board
   - Alternatives: Spread campaign resources across four or five frontiers at reduced scale; or concentrate on the records and independence and accept that other lines pause
   - Scenario assumption: The board chooses concentration. The records and independence become the campaign's primary commitments; other lines are deferred or migrate elsewhere.

2. **2029 — Whether to formalize partnerships with tribal nations and the water district as a primary translation channel, or to wait for federal partnerships to recover** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership, tribal nation natural-resources directors, and Conservancy District staff
   - Alternatives: Wait out the federal contraction in hopes of recovery; or invest deliberately in the partnerships whose authority does not depend on federal continuity
   - Scenario assumption: RMBL invests in the durable partnerships. Federal recovery does not arrive in time to validate the alternative.

3. **2031 — Whether to grow the data and technical staff during the period or hold them at current scale** *Shared inflection: `reserves-versus-staffing-tradeoff`.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the development team
   - Alternatives: Grow the team modestly using campaign reserves; or hold current scale and use the reserves to buffer the institution against further contraction
   - Scenario assumption: Reserves are prioritized over staff growth. The technical core remains at roughly its 2026 size through the period.

4. **2033 — How to manage a generational transition in the observers who carry the marmot demographic record forward** *Shared inflection: `long-record-stewardship-transfer`.*
   - Actors: Guest scientists carrying the marmot study and RMBL's archival staff
   - Alternatives: Concentrate the record in a single successor program at a partner university; or distribute observer roles across several guest-scientist partnerships supported by RMBL's archives and protocols
   - Scenario assumption: The distributed model is chosen. RMBL's archives and protocols hold continuity across the transition.

5. **2035 — How publicly to describe RMBL's nonprofit independence as a feature for guest scientists from restricted institutions** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the board
   - Alternatives: Speak openly about RMBL as a venue for unrestricted inquiry, which could attract scientists but also political attention; or describe the role quietly through individual relationships
   - Scenario assumption: Quiet positioning is chosen. The role grows through word-of-mouth among guest scientists rather than public statement.

6. **2038 — How to mark the marmot study's 75-year milestone given the political climate** *Distinctive to this scenario.*
   - Actors: RMBL leadership and the long-record science community
   - Alternatives: A public anniversary tied to broader climate-science visibility; or a small in-person gathering focused on the science community itself
   - Scenario assumption: The smaller gathering is chosen, in keeping with the period's quieter posture.

## Audience lens — research

If you are a basin scientist during this period, the texture is mixed. The long records you depend on are protected. The plots are maintained. The archives function. RMBL's data scientists and technicians are still there, smaller in number than you might have hoped but stable. What is harder: writing grants in a federal landscape that has narrowed, finding venues for translation work that does not get politicized, watching collaborators at federally-adjacent institutions face restrictions. What is unexpectedly easier: RMBL's nonprofit posture means no one at the institution tells you what you can and cannot ask. If your home institution is under pressure, the basin becomes a venue where you can still pursue the questions you came to science for. The tribal and water-district partnerships are real channels for translation that survive the period intact.

## Audience lens — institution

RMBL in 2040 is smaller than the 2025 plan imagined. The technical core is the size it was in 2026. RMBL365 operates year-round but at reduced scope. The campaign closed below the realistic floor. The institution carries scars from a decade in which environmental science was politically embattled. But it functions. The long records continue. The archives are intact. Guest scientists come, some of them drawn specifically because RMBL did not let restrictions follow them. Two or three deep partnerships — with tribal natural-resources offices and the Upper Gunnison River Water Conservancy District — anchor the translation work. Staff feel the constraints, but they also feel that the institution stayed itself. The board, looking back, sees that the wager on independence and continuity was the right one.

## Audience lens — donor

A contribution to this campaign joins an effort to keep basin science possible during a politically narrowed decade. Your gift is part of protecting the longest records in the basin — the marmot study above Gothic, the meadow phenology series — and the nonprofit independence that lets guest scientists pursue questions without restriction. It is part of sustaining the partnerships with tribal nations and water districts that translate basin science into decisions when federal channels do not. This scenario does not promise a flourishing institution; it asks you to be part of preserving what most needs preserving. A general reader will recognize this as the work of keeping a scientific community intact through hard conditions. A donor will recognize it as the choice to defend the conditions for inquiry rather than expand the reach of a moment.

## Overlay robustness

**Central case.** Under gradual federal contraction, gradual AI evolution, and a moderate climate trajectory, this scenario plays out close to its sketched form. Political pressure on environmental science is the dominant driver. Federal partnerships disrupt but do not vanish entirely. Climate signals continue to be visible in the long records without overwhelming the basin's basic systems. AI tools help guest scientists do retrospective analysis at modest scale, even without a built RMBL program. The scenario's shape — protect records, protect independence, deepen the durable partnerships — fits the central case.

**Stress case — Faster climate change.** If climate change advances faster than central modeling, the long records become both more valuable and more compromised. Some marmot colonies fail. Some phenology plots stop functioning as the systems they tracked. RMBL faces a harder version of its choice: protect the records as records of transformation, or accept that some lines end. The institution narrows further. The political climate makes the costs of this transformation harder to communicate publicly. Independence still matters; community partnerships still hold. But the loss is more visible.

**Stress case — Sharper foundation contraction.** If foundation support for environmental science contracts further than the scenario assumes, the campaign closes well below its already-low position. Reserves cannot be built to the depth planned. The technical core shrinks rather than holds. The tribal and water-district partnerships continue but with thinner support. The long records still continue, because their costs are modest relative to other operations. The institution becomes smaller still, but the priority order remains the same.

**Stress case — Partial political recovery.** If political conditions partially recover in the late 2030s — a shift in the federal landscape that reopens some agency partnerships — RMBL is positioned to receive that recovery without having dismantled its core. The records are intact. The technical staff is small but functional. The tribal and water-district partnerships continue and are not abandoned. The institution does not snap back to its 2025 trajectory, but it is recognizable, and the foundation for renewed external funding is there.

## Plausibility caveats

This scenario assumes a durable shift in public attitudes against environmental science across multiple administrations and across both federal and state levels. That assumption is consequential and contestable. Political conditions could moderate sooner than the scenario imagines, in which case RMBL would emerge from the period under-built relative to what it could have done with bolder investment during a more favorable window. The scenario assumes tribal natural-resources offices and the Upper Gunnison River Water Conservancy District remain durable partners through the period; this depends on those institutions' own political and operational conditions, which the scenario treats as more stable than they may prove to be.

A structural blind spot: the scenario imagines RMBL's nonprofit independence as a clean asset, but in a sufficiently hostile political climate, nonprofit status itself could come under pressure — through changes in tax treatment, through grant-making restrictions, through pressure on universities whose scientists are RMBL's primary community. The scenario does not model that pressure deeply.

A scenario-specific failure mode: the bet on quiet positioning could backfire if it leaves RMBL's role unrecognized when conditions change and broader allies are needed. Choosing word-of-mouth over public statement protects the institution in the constrained period but may leave it without the public constituency it would benefit from in a recovery. The scenario takes the protective choice; it does not claim that was clearly the only right one.
