# The Fourth of July Booth

The wind off Mt. Crested Butte had been shifting all afternoon, dragging woodsmoke from somewhere up Slate River across the festival tents. Maren noticed it when she stood to refill the carafe of cucumber water. Not a near fire. Probably the one above Marble that had been on the InciWeb map for three weeks now, a slow burn in lodgepole that the Forest Service had decided to let run. The smell came and went depending on which way the canyon breathed.

Her booth was the standard kit: the trifold about indoor water use, the augered soil column showing root depth in irrigated hay versus native sage, a clipboard for the email list. Nobody had signed the clipboard. Two kids had stuck their fingers into the soil column and asked if there were worms. She'd said probably, but they were sleeping. The kids' mother had laughed.

Across the gravel path, the RMBL outreach table had the better draw. They always did. The botanical illustrations, the marmot pelt under glass that children were allowed to touch with one finger, the laminated phenology charts going back to the eighties. A graduate student Maren didn't recognize was working the table, talking to a couple in matching sun hoodies about the glacier lily bloom — how this year it had come and gone in under nine days above Gothic, the shortest window on record. The couple looked appropriately stricken. The grad student looked tired in a way Maren recognized from her own first summer at the district: the tiredness of explaining the same true thing too many times in a week.

She had been bracing for Diane all afternoon, and when Diane finally appeared it was from the wrong direction, from the beer garden side, carrying a paper boat of fries.

"You're staffing," Diane said. "I forgot you draw the short straw."

"It's not the short straw. It's good for us to be here."

"Is it."

"It's good for me to be here." Maren took a fry without asking. They were oversalted. "How was the parade?"

"I didn't watch the parade. I came down from Gothic this morning to get my truck looked at and the festival ate the parking, so now I'm here until it un-eats it." Diane sat down on the spare camp chair behind the district table without being invited, which after fifteen years was not a thing that required invitation. She was in field clothes — the same canvas pants she'd worn every summer Maren had known her, the cuffs darker where she knelt. "How's Eli."

"Starting fourth grade. He wants to do soccer and piano and he's going to do one of them and hate me about it."

"Piano."

"That's what I think. He thinks soccer."

"Piano travels better."

"That's what I keep saying."

They sat for a minute. A kid ran by trailing a streamer. The smoke smell came back, fainter. Diane ate her fries one at a time, methodically, the way she did most things.

"So," Diane said.

"So."

"I talked to the department chair yesterday. The Logan thing isn't happening. They can't take it on. They were honest about it — it's not the protocol, it's that they don't have anyone for the field season, full stop, not next year and not in three years either. The postdoc they were going to put on it took the USGS job in Boise."

Maren had known this was coming since June. She had still been hoping. "I'm sorry."

"Yeah." Diane finished a fry. "I'm sixty-three. I was going to do two more seasons and hand it off clean. I have the binders in order. The transect markers are all GPS'd, the protocols are written, the data is in the Knowledge Commons through 2030. It's a working study. It just needs a person to walk it three times a week from snowmelt through August and know what they're looking at."

"That's the part."

"That's the part."

The grad student across the path was now demonstrating something with the pollinator visit chart. Diane glanced over and her face did something complicated that resolved into nothing.

"I talked to Sarah," Maren said. Sarah ran science programs at the district. "Informally."

"And?"

"And she didn't say no."

"She didn't say yes."

"She didn't say yes." Maren turned the carafe a quarter-turn on the table, an unconscious tic. "She said she needed to understand what we'd be buying. She said the board would ask whether pollinator data serves the district's mission, and she'd need an answer that wasn't just *because it's good science.* Which it is, but."

"But that's not a line item."

"That's not a line item." Maren watched a woman lift a toddler to see the marmot pelt. The toddler patted it with a whole palm, breaking the one-finger rule, and the grad student smiled and let it happen. "The honest answer is that I think I can get her there. The pollinator community in the basin tracks something the irrigators care about — they don't know they care about it yet, but the alfalfa seed people will, and the hay yields are downstream of all of it. I can write a memo. I've been writing it in my head for a month."

"But."

"But it would be a forty percent position. Maybe forty-five. With benefits if I fight for them, without if I don't. And it would be funded year to year out of our outreach and science budget, which is not a budget that was designed to do this. Every year I'd be defending it."

"For how long."

"Until I stopped working there. Or until something changed in a way I can't predict."

Diane nodded slowly. She had a way of nodding that Maren had learned, over fifteen years, meant she was already three moves ahead and didn't like any of the boards. The piezometer project they had worked on together in 2018 — the one where Maren had spent a whole August driving up to Gothic to download data because the cellular relay kept dropping out — had ended in a meeting that started with this exact nod.

"It wouldn't be the study," Diane said. "If a district tech is running it, it's a district study. It looks like a district study. Other people see it that way. The publication trail changes. The graduate students don't come."

"I know."

"I'm not saying that to argue against it. I'm saying it because it's true and we'd both have to live inside it."

"I know."

A small wind came through and lifted the corner of the trifold. Maren weighted it with the soil column. Up the path someone was tuning a guitar badly. The smoke smell was back, stronger now; she could see it greying the light on the mountain.

"I don't think it'll be a yes from Sarah," Maren said. "I think it'll be a maybe that becomes a no by November because the board will ask the question I can't fully answer. I'm telling you because I owe you the actual probability, not the polite one."

Diane was quiet a long time. She finished the fries. She folded the paper boat into a tight rectangle and put it in her pocket rather than the trash can six feet away, which was a thing field people did automatically, packing out their own garbage even at a festival where there were bins.

"What would happen," she said, "if I just kept doing it. Past two more years. Until I couldn't."

"You'd be doing it for free."

"I'd be doing it on what's left of my retirement and a couch in someone's mudroom. I've thought about this. The protocols are clean. The plots are good. I could probably get five more seasons out of myself if I didn't break a hip."

Maren wanted to say *don't,* but it wasn't her place and it wasn't the conversation. Instead she said, "Eli would be in high school."

"Eli would be in high school," Diane agreed, which was not an answer to anything but also was.

The grad student across the way was packing up the marmot pelt for the day, sliding it into its acid-free sleeve, fitting it carefully back into the case. The phenology charts came down one at a time, each rolled and rubber-banded. It was the same packing-up Maren had watched at this festival every July for years, careful and unhurried, the gestures of someone who had been taught well by someone who had been taught well.

"I'll write the memo this week," Maren said. "I'll send it to you first. You can tell me what I'm getting wrong."

"Okay."

"And we should get dinner. Bring whoever's at Gothic this week. Eli wants to know if you still have the dog."

"I still have the dog. The dog is older than your son."

"He'll be devastated when she goes."

"So will I."

They sat with that. The smoke thickened a little and then thinned. Above the mountain the light was starting to go the color it went in late July, the gold that meant the short summer was already tipping toward its other side. Maren noticed Diane noticing it too, and neither of them said anything about it, because what was there to say. They had both watched this light for a long time. They would watch it for as long as they were able.
