# The Weight of a Light Year

Tess drove up from RMBL365 at six, the road dusty in a way it shouldn't be in late August — should be muddy, should have the willows along the East River already going red. Instead, gold. Early gold. The aspens above Gothic Road were turning two weeks ahead of any year she had on file, and she had a lot of years on file now.

She parked at the Gothic townsite and got out into woodsmoke. Not close — somewhere west, maybe the Muddy Basin complex, maybe the new one near Marble. Hard to tell anymore. The smoke settled into the basin the way fog used to in October, and the light through it went that flat orange that her phone always tried to correct and failed.

The weighing crew was already on the slope. She could see them as small bright dots above the talus, four of them, moving between the trap stations. She hefted the equipment crate from her trunk — two new spring scales, replacement zip ties, a hard drive in a Pelican case that she'd brought up mostly as an excuse to be useful with her hands.

The hike up took longer than it used to. She blamed the smoke. She was thirty-eight, which she also blamed.

Mira met her at the lower station, sweat-stuck hair, the radio clipped to her vest crackling with someone up-slope calling a recapture. "You brought the scales. Bless you. The old one's reading three percent light, we think."

"Three percent consistently or drifting?"

"Drifting. Hot days especially."

Tess made a note on her phone. Calibration drift correlated with ambient temperature was the kind of thing that, twenty years from now, somebody would want to know about every weight recorded this summer. She'd flag the affected entries in the archive when she got back down. There was a field for it now. There hadn't been, until she'd built one in 2031.

"How's the cohort?"

Mira's face did a thing. "Light. Pups especially. The adults are okay, the matriarchs look fine, but the pups are running maybe seventy percent of the mass we'd expect at this point in the season. Forage went brown in July."

"July."

"July."

They stood for a second with that. Glacier lilies had finished blooming the second week of June this year — Tess had the photo plot images, she'd been the one to date them. *Ipomopsis* had bolted and senesced before the bumble bee queens were really established. The whole phenological stack had compressed. Marmots eating brown grass in July was the downstream of that, and the pups were the downstream of the downstream.

"They'll hibernate light," Mira said. "Some of them won't come up."

"Some of them won't come up," Tess agreed.

---

She spent the afternoon doing what she'd come to do, which was mostly small. Held a yearling female while Lin took the measurements — the marmot warm and surprisingly heavy in her hands, the heart going fast against her palm, the smell of sage and something muskier under it. Logged ear-tag numbers into the tablet. Watched Aarav, who was new this season and a postdoc from Toronto, learn the rhythm of trap-check, weigh, record, release. He was good. He'd be back next year if his funding held, which was a sentence Tess found herself thinking about more people lately.

Around three she sat down on a rock with her laptop and ran the query she'd actually come up here to run.

The 1934 baseline. There had been a drought year that summer — not as sharp as this one in terms of snowpack, the snowpack records didn't really start until later, but the Gunnison county precipitation records were good enough, and there were field notebooks. The notebooks were the thing. She had digitized them in 2032, the summer her second kid was born, working from home in Crested Butte with the baby asleep on her chest and the scanner humming. A botanist named Langenheim had been working the meadow above Gothic that summer of '34, and her notebook entries were detailed enough — phenology by eye, mostly, but consistent — that Tess had been able to pull a rough flowering timeline out of them. Not the marmot data; the marmot data didn't exist yet. But the plants. What the plants had done.

The query was simple. She wanted to compare Langenheim's 1934 timeline against this summer's plot data, species by species where the species overlapped.

It took maybe two minutes to run. The data team had spent six years making it that easy.

The result was not what she'd expected. She'd expected '34 to look like this summer — early, compressed. It did, partly. But the species ordering was different. *Delphinium* came before *Ipomopsis* in '34; this year it had been the reverse. *Boechera* timing matched almost exactly. The lilies were five days later in '34 than in 2034. Five days, ninety years apart, in a year that everyone was calling unprecedented.

She sat with that for a while. It didn't mean anything in particular. It meant the system had history. It meant whatever was happening now had partial echoes she could now actually see. It meant some questions had become askable that had not been askable when she'd started.

It also meant nothing about the pups.

She closed the laptop. The smoke had thickened. Down the valley she could see the river, low, the gravel bars showing where they shouldn't be showing until October.

---

Dinner was at the dining hall, which was operating on reduced staff this season because the kitchen manager had broken her wrist in July and the replacement had quit after two weeks. Lin had picked up shifts. The food was fine. The food had always been fine. Tess sat with the marmot crew and Aarav and two visiting plant ecologists from Davis, and they ate elk chili and cornbread and talked about, in this order: the smoke, the new dishwasher that didn't drain properly, Aarav's sister's wedding in October, whether anyone had actually seen a pika this summer up by Copper Lake (Lin had, two weeks ago, sounding fine), the price of housing in Crested Butte, and only at the very end, briefly, what next summer might look like.

Nobody said the thing about the pups out loud at dinner. That was a rule Tess had learned years ago and never seen written down. You did not eulogize animals you would weigh again next year, or might.

After dinner she stepped outside. The smoke had pushed the stars into a kind of soft yellow blur. She could hear someone in the bunkhouse playing guitar badly, and someone else laughing about it. Aarav came out and stood next to her for a minute without saying anything, which she appreciated.

"Do you ever think about what this place was like in the eighties," he said eventually.

"I wasn't here in the eighties."

"I know. I mean — do you ever read the old notebooks and try to picture it."

"All the time."

He nodded. "I read the '78 marmot logs last week. The handwriting." He laughed a little. "The handwriting is terrible. Beautiful, but terrible. I had to ask Mira to read three pages for me."

"There's a project to transcribe those," Tess said. "We got the grant in February. Starting next summer if the federal piece holds."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we do it slower. With the people we have."

He nodded again. He was, she realized, asking whether he should plan to be one of those people. She didn't answer that, because it wasn't hers to answer, and because the honest answer was *I hope so and I can't promise.*

They stood there a while longer. Down at the trap line the radios were quiet. The marmots, the ones still up, were settling in their burrows for the night. The pups, the light ones, were doing whatever they could.

Tess thought about Langenheim, in 1934, sitting somewhere on this same slope writing down what bloomed when, not knowing anyone would ever read it. She thought about her own data, the calibration drift she'd flag tomorrow, the weight entries she'd tag with a temperature correction so that someone — some Aarav of 2090, maybe, or some archivist she would never meet — could ask a question she hadn't thought to ask.

She wasn't sure the question would get asked. She wasn't sure there'd be an archivist.

She went inside to find her sleeping bag. The drive back to Crested Butte could wait until morning. The smoke would probably be worse by then, but the road would still be there, and she had a meeting at ten about server costs.
