# The Shape of a Record

Maya got to RMBL365 at seven-fifteen with a thermos of coffee and the kind of headache that comes from rehearsing an argument in your sleep. The Crested Butte sky had that hard September blue that meant the aspens up Kebler would be turning by the weekend. She'd told her wife she'd be home by six; she already knew that wasn't true.

The partnership room smelled like the building always smelled in early fall — propane heat just turned on, dust burning off the registers, somebody's apple from the kitchen. She set the coffee down at the long table and pulled up the exhibits Lena had assembled overnight.

Lena was the system they'd licensed in 2034 — back when it was still called something else — and renamed when it got embarrassing to keep the vendor name. By now nobody on staff remembered what it had originally been called. Lena had read every field notebook in the digitized archive and most of the gray literature in the basin folder, and she annotated overnight queries with the tone of a slightly tired postdoc. The exhibit set she'd prepared was clean: the meadow phenology series from 1974 forward, the snowmelt-date record overlaid, the *Ipomopsis aggregata* first-bloom curve with its ugly inflection at 2029.

*Note,* Lena had written in the margin, *the 1989–1993 gap in the meadow notes is methodological, not observational. Recommend Dr. Ahn address proactively under direct.*

Maya almost laughed. Dr. Ahn was going to hate addressing it proactively.

Jess from the Conservancy District came in at seven-forty with two breakfast burritos from Camp 4, handed one to Maya without saying anything, and started setting up the screen. They'd been doing this nine years now. The burrito ritual had started during the 2032 drought hearings and stuck.

"Karen's at eight-fifteen," Jess said. "She wants to walk through cross before we get Diana on."

"Diana's going to push back on the framing."

"Diana always pushes back on the framing."

"This time she's right to."

Jess made a face that wasn't disagreement.

Diana Ahn arrived at five to eight, in field clothes because she'd come down from Gothic, and she had the look of someone who'd already been awake for four hours and resented being indoors. She'd run the meadow plots since 2011. Her hair had gone gray during the 2027 wildfire summer and stayed that way.

"I read the exhibits," she said, not sitting down yet. "Page four is wrong."

Maya pulled it up. "Wrong how."

"The 2031 first-bloom date for *Ipomopsis* is the meadow mean. The exhibit caption reads like it's the East River corridor mean. Those aren't the same record."

"Lena flagged it as the meadow mean in the metadata —"

"The caption."

Maya looked. Diana was right. The caption had been auto-generated from a template and the template predated the 2034 split between the corridor stations and the upper meadow plots. She tagged it for revision and asked Lena to rerun the captions against the current schema. Lena confirmed and offered to flag every caption built from the pre-2034 template across the exhibit set. Maya said yes.

Diana sat down. "Thank you."

"You're going to hate the cross."

"I already hate the cross."

Karen came on the video call at eight-fifteen exactly, from her office in Gunnison. She was the district's outside counsel and had been since the 2034 augmentation case. She'd learned more basin ecology in the last decade than most ecologists learn in a career, and she still apologized for not knowing enough.

"Morning, everyone. Diana, thanks for coming down. I want to start with the framing question and then walk through the cross I'm worried about."

Diana nodded once, which was as much encouragement as Karen was going to get.

"The opposing expert is going to argue that what you're describing is variability, not change. He'll point to the 1977 snowmelt, the 1981 anomaly, and the gap in the 1989–93 meadow record. He'll ask you whether you can distinguish a trend from a sequence of unusual years. He'll be polite. He'll be very polite."

"He's wrong," Diana said.

"I know he's wrong. The judge needs to know he's wrong. Walk me through how you'd answer."

Diana started in the register she used at lab meetings — methods first, then the comparison of variance structures across decadal windows, the breakpoint analysis the 2036 paper had done, the way the *Boechera stricta* flowering had decoupled from snowmelt in a way nothing in the historical record had done. She was twenty seconds in before Maya interrupted.

"Diana. Stop."

Diana stopped.

"He's going to ask you that question, and what the judge is going to hear is you explaining variance structures. The judge is going to hear a scientist being careful. The opposing expert is going to win the moment because he was simple and you weren't."

"I'm not going to oversimplify the —"

"I'm not asking you to oversimplify. I'm asking you to start with the answer."

Diana looked at her for a long second. The thing about Diana was that she actually listened, eventually, if you waited her out. It was one of the reasons Maya had stayed past the seven-year point when she'd thought about leaving for the policy job in Boulder.

"The answer is yes," Diana said slowly. "I can distinguish the trend from a sequence of unusual years. Then I can say how."

"Right."

"That feels —" Diana paused. "That feels like I'm being asked to talk like an advocate."

"You're being asked to talk like a witness," Karen said gently. "Which is a different register from the one you teach in."

"I don't like it."

"I know."

There was a silence. Jess opened her laptop and pulled up the snowmelt record without saying anything, which was the thing Jess did when she wanted to give somebody a minute. Maya took a sip of her coffee. It had gone cold.

"Okay," Diana said. "Walk me through it again."

They worked for two hours. Karen had the cross mapped out in her head and walked Diana through five different versions of the variability question. Diana got steadily better at starting with the answer. Around ten-thirty she asked Lena to pull every meadow record between 1985 and 1995 to see what could be reconstructed for the gap years, and Lena returned a hybrid record from cross-referenced phenology notebooks of three guest scientists who'd been at Gothic in those summers, with confidence intervals. It wasn't complete. It was better than nothing. Diana looked at it for a while.

"I didn't know we had those."

"They were in the 2036 digitization batch. Dr. Halverson's notes from '91. Pradeep's from '92 and '93. We don't usually surface them because the methods aren't consistent with the standard protocol."

"For the gap years they're better than the gap."

"That's what Lena thought."

Diana nodded, slowly, and Maya watched her decide to use them. That was the kind of decision you couldn't make for somebody else.

At eleven Karen had to drop for another call. Jess went to grab more coffee. Diana stayed at the table, staring at the hybrid record on the screen.

"My daughter's flying in tomorrow," she said, not looking up. "She wants to go up to Copper Lake. I told her the talus slope's different now. She doesn't believe me."

"The pikas?"

"They've moved up. I want to show her where they used to be."

Maya didn't say anything. They sat with it. Outside on Elk Avenue somebody was running a leaf blower, which was absurd because the leaves weren't down yet.

"I'm not going to be good at this," Diana said. "On the stand."

"You're going to be better than you think."

"That's not the same as good."

"No. It isn't."

Jess came back with the coffee. They started in on the second half of the exhibits — the snowmelt-and-runoff paired record the district and RMBL had built together starting in 2030, the one that meant the neighboring district was watching this case the way they were. Without that paired record, the changed-circumstances claim would have been harder to make. Without the nine years of meetings in this room, the paired record would not have existed in the form the court would accept. None of that was going to come up at trial. The court would see the record. It would not see the room.

By two in the afternoon they had the cross down to something Diana could live with. She left to drive back up to Gothic before the afternoon light changed. Maya watched her truck pull out and thought about texting her wife to say she'd be home for dinner after all, and then Karen called back and they spent another hour on direct.

At five-forty Maya sat alone at the long table with the exhibits still up on the screen. Lena had a query waiting: *Should I prepare the cross-reference for the neighboring district's likely 2041 filing? Their records gap is wider than ours.*

Maya looked at it. The neighboring district hadn't asked. They might never ask. They might ask next week.

She typed back: *Yes. Start with the snowpack record. We'll talk Monday.*

Then she packed up the thermos and went home.
