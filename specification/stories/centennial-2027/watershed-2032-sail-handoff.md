# The Inventory

The first frost had come three nights earlier than last year, and the willows along the East River were already the color of weak tea. Maren noticed because she always noticed; she had been noticing willows in this drainage since 2021, the first SAIL season, when she'd been a postdoc with a half-stake in a flux tower and the whole valley had felt like an experiment that might not work. The willows always told her where the season was. Today they told her it was over.

She walked the riparian transect with Devon, who had a clipboard with a real clipboard's spring clip, and a tablet zipped into a fleece pocket because the tablet's battery hated mornings like this. Devon was twenty-eight, maybe twenty-nine. They'd been on the technical staff three years, hired in the first wave after the campaign closed, when RMBL had started growing the team that kept everything coupled — the team that, in theory, made days like this one easier.

"Piezometer cluster four is on the list," Devon said.

"Of course it is."

"I'm not saying it should be. I'm saying it's on the list."

"I know what you're saying."

The list was the quiet inventory. Devon hadn't called it that, and neither had Maren, but they both knew what they were walking. Three weeks ago, the federal program officer had sent the letter that everyone had been half-expecting and wholly dreading: the post-SAIL bridge funding for the atmospheric profilers and two of the groundwater well clusters was not going to extend. Decommission by spring, or transfer to RMBL operation under a memorandum that would take six months to draft and then have to be paid for somehow. The campaign reserves were real, but they had been sized for one wave of this, not two.

The riparian transect dropped off the road in a slow curve and met the river where the beaver had rebuilt their dam after the 2029 blowout. Beyond the dam, the floodplain opened into the meadow that held cluster four — six wells, each capped with a steel sleeve, each one running continuous water-table and chemistry since 2022. The willow had grown up around them so that you had to know where to look.

"What's the science cost on four?" Devon asked. They were going through the motions of the conversation Devon had been told to have.

"You already know."

"I want to hear you say it."

Maren stopped at the second well and put her hand on the sleeve. It was cold enough that her palm came away tacky for a second, the moisture from the air freezing where she'd touched. "Cluster four is the only place in the basin where we've got groundwater chemistry under a beaver complex with full overstory recovery. You decommission four and you lose the coupling between the surface chemistry record and what the riparian does in a hot year. Cluster three doesn't have the wood. Cluster five is too far up."

"That's what I had."

"Then you had it."

They kept walking. A dipper went past them downstream, low and fast, the small gray shape of it more like a thrown stone than a bird. Maren had not seen a dipper in two weeks. She wondered where they went in October and realized she had wondered this every October for eleven years and had never once looked it up.

"My partner wants to move," Devon said, after a while. "Back to Bozeman. Her mom's not great."

"Are you going to?"

"I don't know. I told her I'd know by Thanksgiving."

"That's not very long."

"No."

The trail bent up onto the bench and the wind found them. Maren pulled her hood up and Devon didn't, because Devon was twenty-eight and from somewhere north of here and wore a wool shirt as if wool shirts were sufficient. They came out at the lower flux tower, the one that had been the first SAIL installation and was now, with new electronics and the same frame, RMBL property. The tower hummed faintly. A pair of ravens worked the air above the ridge.

"This one's fine," Devon said. "This one isn't on the list."

"I know."

"I just wanted to say it."

Maren laughed, once, short. "Thanks."

She had been there the morning the tower went up. The crew had come in at four a.m. to beat the convection, and someone had brought a thermos of coffee that turned out to be mostly chicory, and she had stood at the base of the frame and watched the cables go taut and thought *this changes what I can ask*. It had. The papers she had written in the decade since had all leaned, in one way or another, on the column of air this tower measured. Her grant renewal, the one currently sitting in a panel queue somewhere, leaned on it too. If the tower were on the list, she'd be doing different math right now.

"How's funding at the university?" Devon asked, in the careful tone of a person who knew the answer was bad.

"Tight. The dean is doing that thing where she talks about *strategic alignment*. You know how that goes."

"My advisor used to say *strategic alignment* meant the strategy was to align ourselves with whoever still had money."

"Your advisor was correct."

They turned off the tower path and took the cut through the sedge to the upper well cluster, the one above the oxbow. The light was getting better as the sun cleared the ridge — that low October light that made the frost on the sedge tips look like sugar and the river surface like hammered tin. A small wedge of geese went over, high, calling. Maren stopped to watch.

"I came here in '21 thinking I was going to spend three summers and write a chapter," she said. "And then it was four summers and a paper, and then it was the coupled study, and then the marmot people pulled me into the social-thermal thing, and then the floral chemistry came on top, and now —" She made a gesture that took in the tower and the river and the wells and the sky. "I don't even know how to describe my research program anymore. I just say I work at the East River."

"That's a fine thing to say."

"Is it?"

Devon shrugged. "You said it. That makes it fine."

They reached the upper cluster. Three wells, all clean, all running. Devon pulled the tablet out, blew on the screen, and tapped through to the chemistry feed. Maren leaned against the steel sleeve and looked back down the valley. From here you could see the lower tower, the new met station on the bench, the line of the flume where the gauge was, and beyond all of it the road up to Gothic. A pickup was coming down it slowly, taking the washboard. Someone heading to town. Probably to RMBL365 for the lunch shift; Maren had been going there on Thursdays for two years and she still couldn't reliably get the soup before they ran out. There was a joke in the staff about it.

"What are you going to recommend?" she asked.

Devon was quiet for a long minute. The tablet went to sleep. They didn't wake it. "I'm not the one making the call."

"I know. But what are you going to recommend."

"I'm going to recommend we keep four. And give up the second profiler. The one above the lodge."

"That's a real loss."

"I know."

"You'll get pushback from the atmospheric people."

"I know."

"Okay."

She didn't know if it was the right call. She suspected no one knew if it was the right call; the call was the kind that you found out about in 2041, when somebody tried to write the synthesis paper and discovered which gap in the record they couldn't close. She thought about saying this to Devon and decided not to. Devon already knew.

They started back down. The geese were gone. The frost on the sedge had begun to melt in patches where the sun reached, and the smell coming off the willow was the wet, slightly sweet smell of leaf that was finished. Maren had loved that smell for a long time. She loved it today too, in the slightly thinner way that things get loved when you have been doing them long enough to know they are not promised.

"Soup at the 365?" Devon said. "If we hustle?"

"You're an optimist."

"I'm hungry."

They hustled. The pickup was already pulling into the lot when they got there, and the soup, when they reached the counter, was almost gone but not quite. Devon took the last of it. Maren got the sandwich. They sat by the window. Outside, the willows were the color of weak tea, and the river ran past, and the inventory sat on the table between them, unmentioned, still being made.
