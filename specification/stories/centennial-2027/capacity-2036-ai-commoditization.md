# The Weekend Service

Maya had practiced not flinching, but the slide still caught her in the sternum.

The startup's CTO — a man in a good gray sweater, no older than she was — clicked through to a familiar-looking network diagram. *Pollinator co-visitation network, East River drainage, 1974–2034.* He had pulled it from the Knowledge Commons public release that spring. The colors were RMBL's. The layout was the basin's. The caption read: *Generated in 41 minutes from raw archive, no domain configuration.*

"Forty-one minutes," he said. "We didn't train on this. We didn't know what *Ipomopsis aggregata* was until last Tuesday."

Polite laughter. Maya kept her face composed. She knew the diagram intimately — she had spent eleven months in 2032 reconciling the visit-record schemas across four observer generations to make that figure possible, and another four months arguing with a guest scientist about whether the 1981 plot edges could be trusted. The startup's version had quietly merged the suspect edges. A small wrongness, invisible in the slide.

When it was her turn on the panel she said what she had decided to say. That the tools her team built were not faster, no, but they carried context. That a basin was not an archive. That the digitized field notebooks from the late seventies — the ones she and Priya had spent two summers transcribing with a retired marmot observer at her kitchen table in Crested Butte — contained marginalia the OCR could not yet parse, and the marginalia mattered. *Snow late. Yearlings thin. Camp robber took the eggs.* You could not weekend-service your way to the camp robber.

The moderator, who was kind, asked her to elaborate. She tried. She watched the audience listen with the careful attention people give to arguments they have already half-discounted.

Afterward in the hallway a woman from a Sierra Nevada station told her the talk had been useful. Two grad students asked if RMBL was hiring. A program officer from one of the foundations that had funded the Commons buildout shook her hand and said, vaguely, *keep going.* Maya smiled at all of them and felt her face hold the smile a beat too long.

---

Ahmed was waiting at the restaurant, a Vietnamese place off Larimer he had been recommending for years. He stood when she came in, and the hug was the long kind, the kind that acknowledged a hard day without making her describe it.

"You saw it," she said.

"I saw the demo this morning. They're using it at Stanford for a meta-analysis of phenological shifts. I sat in on the run."

"And?"

"And the meta-analysis is fine. It's fine, Maya." He sat down. He had aged since she had last seen him — he was sixty-one now, she thought, and his hair had gone properly white above the ears, and he carried himself in the careful way of someone whose knees had opinions. "It's a good tool. For meta-analysis it's a good tool."

She ordered the pho out of inertia. She did not want it.

"Tell me about the field season," she said. "Real things. Not this."

He smiled. "The glacier lilies were a disaster. Two weeks early — earlier than any year on the record. We lost the *Boechera* census for the eleven hundred meter plots because the snow patches that mark the transect were gone before I could get there. Sasha is writing it up as a gap year. She's furious."

"Was it warm in August?"

"Smoky. The fire near Paonia. We had three days in the meadow where we couldn't see Gothic Mountain from the cabin. Anil took the kids down to Crested Butte and they spent the week at the 365 building doing the school program. He said the new wet lab is nice. He said your coordinator — the tribal partnerships one, what's her name —"

"Daniela."

"Daniela. He said she ran a session with some kids from Paonia and one of them caught a damselfly in the parking lot and they spent an hour on it. Anil came home happy."

Maya took the lime wedge from the rim of her water glass and squeezed it into the water she did not particularly want to drink. She was waiting for him to say the thing.

He said it. "I used their service last month."

"I know."

"For the network analysis on the long-tongued bumblebee paper. The basin tools were going to take me through Christmas. The weekend service got me to a draft by Tuesday."

"I know, Ahmed. I saw the API calls."

He winced — gently, the way he did. "I should have told you."

"You don't owe me a notification. The tools are public."

"I am telling you, though, because I want to tell you something else." He set down his chopsticks. "I read your figure three times before I trusted the draft. I went back to the archive and pulled the 1991 field notes because something in the visit frequencies for *Bombus balteatus* looked off. The startup's tool had cleaned a transcription error that I needed to see. Your tool would have flagged it. I lost a day finding it and I was annoyed at myself the whole day, and at one point I thought, *I should have just used Priya's pipeline.*"

"That's nice of you to say."

"I'm not being nice. I'm telling you I went back. The tools you built make me a better reader of my own data. Theirs make me a faster writer of papers. Those are different things and I think we are about to find out which one the world is willing to pay for."

She looked at him. The restaurant was loud and the light was warm and her chest had loosened by a small increment without her noticing.

"That's the question, isn't it," she said.

"It is." He picked up his chopsticks again. "It's not the question you can answer alone. It's not even the question RMBL can answer alone. It's a question the next ten years answer."

"Sebastian wants me to write a position paper. For the board. About what we do now."

"What do you want to write?"

She thought about this for a while. The pho came. She lifted the bowl and let the steam rise into her face and remembered, with the suddenness these memories had, the first July she had spent at Gothic — the smell of woodsmoke from the cabin stoves, the way the light came down off Snodgrass in the late afternoon onto the meadow, a marmot whistling from the talus above the road and another answering, much farther up, almost beyond hearing. The thing she had thought, in her first week, that the work she would do here would matter because the place mattered. She had been right and she had also been naive, and she did not yet know how to feel about being both.

"I want to write that we keep building for the basin," she said. "And that the basin is not a dataset. And that the partnerships at 365 — the water districts, the tribe, the schools — those are things the weekend service cannot do, and we should not pretend they are a consolation prize. They are the work."

"Then write that."

"It sounds defensive when I say it out loud."

"It sounds true. Defensive and true are not mutually exclusive."

She laughed — a short laugh, more breath than sound, but real. "Ahmed."

"What."

"How are your knees."

"Terrible. I am thinking about not doing the Copper Lake transect next year. Sasha can do it. She's faster than me now anyway. She found a pika colony above the lake last August I have walked past for fifteen years without seeing."

"You walked past it."

"Fifteen years. She found it in twenty minutes. The pika was sitting on a rock the size of a dinner plate making the noise they make. Sasha said, *Ahmed, that is a pika,* with the tone of voice my daughter uses when I cannot find my reading glasses."

Maya did laugh then, properly, and Ahmed laughed with her, and for a few minutes they ate and talked about Sasha and about Ahmed's daughter and about a wedding in November that Maya had been invited to and had not yet decided whether to attend.

When the check came Ahmed paid it before she could reach for her card, which was the kind of small kindness she had stopped fighting him about years ago. They stood on the sidewalk on Larimer and the air had the particular dry cold of Denver in late September, the cold that meant Gothic was already getting its first real snow.

"Drive carefully tomorrow," he said.

"I'm flying."

"Fly carefully."

She walked to her hotel with her hands in her coat pockets and her panel badge still clipped to her lapel, and she did not take it off until she was in the elevator. The position paper would wait until Monday. Tonight she would call Priya, who was in Gothic closing the cabin for the season, and ask about the snow. The startup's tool would still be there in the morning. So would the basin.
