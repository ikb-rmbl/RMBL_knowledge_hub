# The Drift at Pumphouse

The piezometer had pinged at 3:47 in the morning, polite about it, with the diagnostic appended and a probability estimate on the drift — 0.71, which Mira read as *probably real, please come look*. She read the message at six over coffee in the trailer at Gothic, agreed with it, told the instrument she was on her way, and packed.

The skin track up from the winter gate was firm. Someone — probably Devon — had set it two days ago and the cold had locked it in. She climbed in the blue hour with the headlamp off because by the time she reached the bench above the river the eastern sky was already pale, and her eyes worked better without the light. The willows along the river were bare and red. Above her the talus was clean of snow on the south aspects, which it had not been in January when she'd first come up here in 2032, but which it routinely was now. She noted the absence the way she noted her own breathing. Take the world as it is.

She reached Pumphouse at 9:20. The instrument hut was a small grey box on a platform above the riparian zone, ringed by a fence that kept the moose from leaning on the conduit. The pressure transducer itself sat ten meters downslope in the casing, but the logger and the satellite uplink lived in the hut. Mira pulled her skis, stood them in the snow, and let herself in.

Inside it was twelve below. The logger had been running since 2021, originally on SAIL power, now on RMBL power, the same instrument with a different sticker on the case. She had replaced the desiccant in October and the cards in November and the battery bank in 2036. The work accreted. There was a smell in here she associated with this hut specifically — cold electronics, pine needles tracked in by her own boots over years, the faint plastic of the cable insulation. Other huts smelled different. She had opinions about all of them.

The tablet woke up and pulled the overnight diagnostic and overlaid it on the seven-year baseline. The drift was visible: a slow downward creep in the reference pressure starting in mid-October, about 4 millibars cumulative, which on a shallow piezometer was not nothing. It could be the transducer. It could be the reference port icing in a way it hadn't iced before. It could be the casing settling. The instrument had its own guess — *transducer reference, 64%; ice in port, 22%; casing, 9%; other, 5%* — and had attached the three previous winters' October-November pressure traces for comparison. She scrolled through them. 2036 was clean. 2037 was clean. 2038 had a small bump in early November she remembered chasing and clearing. This was not that.

She pulled the multimeter and ran the manual checks anyway. The instrument was probably right but she wanted her hands on it. The voltage on the reference was within spec. The current draw on the transducer was within spec. The case temperature logger showed the hut had stayed above its threshold all autumn, which ruled out one class of failure. She wrote her readings into the tablet by hand because Theo, whose project this was, liked seeing her handwriting in the record even when the tablet was perfectly capable of capturing the numbers itself. He had told her once that her field notes read like someone who had decided to care, and she had told him that was a low bar, and they had agreed to disagree on the bar.

Theo was in Reykjavík until April. He had a baby now. His messages had gotten shorter and more loving in a way she found funny — he used to write paragraphs and now he wrote *yes* and *do it* and *thank you, M*. His project was on the lag between snowmelt onset and shallow groundwater response across the basin's hillslope clusters, three years in, the final winter of fieldwork. If the last three months of the Pumphouse record were compromised, he lost one of his five sites for the most interesting part of the year. He could still publish. He would publish something different.

She pulled the casing log up. The transducer had been in the ground since 2022, which made it seventeen years old, which was old. Replacements existed. She had three of them in the shop at Gothic, already calibrated against the lab standard, waiting for exactly this kind of morning. The decision in front of her was whether to swap now — meaning she'd be back up here tomorrow with the replacement and a heated tent and Devon for the second pair of hands — or whether to flag the data, log the drift as a known unknown, and let Theo decide whether to use the last three months conditionally.

She considered. The wind had come up outside; she could hear it working at the corner of the hut.

If she swapped now, the continuous record had a gap of about thirty-six hours and a calibration step she'd have to document carefully. If she flagged and waited, the record stayed continuous but the last three months carried an uncertainty band Theo would have to defend in the paper. She knew which one she would do. She also knew she was going to ask Theo, because it was his data, and because asking him was part of the rhythm they had built.

She drafted the message on the tablet.

*Theo — drift on Pumphouse looks real, instrument flags transducer reference, I agree. 4 mb cumulative since mid-Oct. Three options: (1) swap tomorrow, 36h gap, clean record forward; (2) flag last three months for review, keep continuous, you defend the band; (3) swap and reconstruct the October-January segment from the upstream and downstream pair plus the soil moisture column, which I think gets you within 1 mb but isn't trivial. I lean (1). Devon free tomorrow. Hug the baby.*

She sent it. The tablet hummed and the satellite found her and the message went.

The model running on the tablet — the one that lived locally and read the basin's instrument streams and the long records side by side — had already noticed she was drafting about Pumphouse and had pulled the relevant context into a sidebar without being asked. It had also flagged, in a small unobtrusive way, that the 1994 paper notebook from the meadow plots above Gothic had a hand-drawn note about a similar reference drift in a different instrument at a different site that someone had attributed to a freeze-thaw cycle in the port. The notebook had been digitized in 2033. The model thought she might find the note interesting. She did, a little. She made a mental note to read it properly tonight.

She packed up. The hut closed with the same stubborn latch it had always had; she had filed it once and it had stayed filed. Outside, the light had gone hard and clean and the wind was pushing snow off the ridge in long white banners. She clipped into her skis.

The ski back was, as promised, good. The track was firm and the gradient was friendly and the river below her was iced over in the slow reaches and open in the fast ones. She thought about dinner. Annie was cooking — it was Annie's week — and had texted earlier asking whether Mira wanted the lentils or the other thing, and Mira had said the lentils, and now she was looking forward to them in a clear uncomplicated way. She thought about Theo's baby, whom she had not yet met. She thought about whether Devon would want to come up tomorrow or whether he'd grumble about it; probably both. She thought about the 1994 notebook entry and what the handwriting would look like.

Halfway down, the tablet buzzed against her hip. She stopped at the switchback above the confluence and read.

*(1). Thank you. Photos when you can. The baby is enormous. — T*

She smiled into her buff and pushed off again, and the skis ran, and the cold air came up off the river and into her teeth, and she was already thinking about which of the three replacement transducers she'd pull, and whether Devon would want coffee or the good coffee, and what she'd write to Theo tomorrow night after the swap was done.
